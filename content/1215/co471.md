Semidefinite programming occupies a remarkable position in modern optimization. It sits strictly between linear programming and general convex optimization in expressive power, yet retains polynomial-time solvability. Its weapon is the **positive semidefinite (PSD) cone**: a closed convex cone with rich algebraic structure, a self-dual pairing, and a geometric shape that captures a breathtaking range of problems — from eigenvalue optimization to graph coloring bounds to polynomial nonnegativity certificates.

These notes trace the theory from first principles: the algebra of symmetric matrices, the geometry of the PSD cone, the primal–dual structure of semidefinite programs, the conditions guaranteeing strong duality, the algorithms that solve SDPs in polynomial time, and the applications that make SDP one of the most powerful tools in combinatorial and continuous optimization.

## Chapter 1: Positive Semidefinite Matrices

### 1.1 Symmetric Matrices and the Trace Inner Product

We work throughout with **real symmetric matrices**. Denote by \(\mathcal{S}^n\) the space of all \(n \times n\) symmetric matrices:
\[
\mathcal{S}^n := \{X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} : X^T = X\}.
\]
This is a vector space of dimension \(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\), and it carries a natural inner product.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Trace Inner Product). For \(X, S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\), define
\[
\langle X, S \rangle := \operatorname{Tr}(X^T S) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n X_{ij} S_{ij}.
\]
On \(\mathcal{S}^n\) this becomes \(\langle X, S \rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(XS)\). This inner product is symmetric, bilinear, and positive definite.</div>

The trace is invariant under cyclic permutation: \(\operatorname{Tr}(ABC) = \operatorname{Tr}(CAB) = \operatorname{Tr}(BCA)\). This elementary fact is used constantly in proofs.

Every \(X \in \mathcal{S}^n\) has an eigenvalue decomposition. Since \(X\) is symmetric, its eigenvalues are real; we order them \(\lambda_1(X) \geq \lambda_2(X) \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n(X)\).

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Spectral Decomposition). For every \(X \in \mathcal{S}^n\) there exists an orthogonal \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) such that
\[
X = Q \operatorname{Diag}(\lambda(X)) Q^T,
\]
where \(\operatorname{Diag}(\lambda(X))\) is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. The columns of \(Q\) are the corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors.</div>

### 1.2 Positive Semidefinite Matrices

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Positive Semidefinite). A matrix \(X \in \mathcal{S}^n\) is <strong>positive semidefinite (PSD)</strong> if
\[
h^T X h \geq 0 \quad \text{for all } h \in \mathbb{R}^n.
\]
If the inequality is strict for every \(h \neq 0\), we say \(X\) is <strong>positive definite (PD)</strong>. We write \(X \succeq 0\) for PSD and \(X \succ 0\) for PD. The set of PSD matrices is denoted \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\), and the set of PD matrices is \(\mathcal{S}^n_{++}\).</div>

![2×2 PSD cone visualization](/pics/co471/psd-cone.png)

The PSD cone \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\) is a proper closed convex cone: it is closed under non-negative linear combinations and its only lineality is \(\{0\}\). For \(2 \times 2\) matrices, the condition \(\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ b & c\end{bmatrix} \succeq 0\) is equivalent to \(a \geq 0\), \(c \geq 0\), and \(ac \geq b^2\) — a solid cone in the \((a,b,c)\) space as shown above.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Equivalent Characterizations). Let \(X \in \mathcal{S}^n\). The following are equivalent:
<ol>
<li>\(X \succeq 0\).</li>
<li>All eigenvalues of \(X\) are nonnegative: \(\lambda(X) \geq 0\).</li>
<li>There exists \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) such that \(X = BB^T\) (Gram matrix).</li>
<li>For every nonempty \(J \subseteq [n]\), \(\det(X_J) \geq 0\), where \(X_J\) is the principal submatrix indexed by \(J\).</li>
<li>For every \(S \in \mathcal{S}^n_+\), \(\langle X, S \rangle \geq 0\) (i.e., \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\) is self-dual).</li>
</ol></div>

![Eigenvalue structure: PSD vs. indefinite](/pics/co471/psd-matrix-eigenvalues.png)

Characterization (2) is particularly vivid: a symmetric matrix is PSD precisely when its eigenvalues are all nonneg­ative. Characterization (3) links PSD to Gram matrices of vectors — a bridge that is essential for SDP relaxations of combinatorial problems. Characterization (5) says that \((\mathcal{S}^n_+)^* = \mathcal{S}^n_+\), making it a **self-dual cone**.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Cholesky Decomposition). Let \(X \in \mathcal{S}^n\). Then:
<ul>
<li>\(X \succeq 0\) if and only if there exists a lower triangular \(B\) with \(X = BB^T\).</li>
<li>\(X \succ 0\) if and only if there exists a nonsingular lower triangular \(B\) with \(X = BB^T\).</li>
</ul></div>

Moreover, \(\mathcal{S}^n_{++} = \operatorname{int}(\mathcal{S}^n_+)\): the positive definite matrices are the interior of the PSD cone.

### 1.3 The Schur Complement Lemma

A key tool for converting nonlinear constraints into linear matrix inequalities (LMIs) is the Schur complement condition.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Schur Complement). Let \(T \in \mathcal{S}^m_{++}\) and \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), \(X \in \mathcal{S}^n\). Then
\[
M := \begin{bmatrix} T & U^T \\ U & X \end{bmatrix} \succeq 0
\quad \iff \quad X - U T^{-1} U^T \succeq 0.
\]
Moreover, \(M \succ 0\) if and only if \(X - UT^{-1}U^T \succ 0\).</div>

![Schur complement block matrix diagram](/pics/co471/schur-complement.png)

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> Factor \(M\) by block Gaussian elimination:
\[
M = L \begin{bmatrix} T & 0 \\ 0 & X - UT^{-1}U^T \end{bmatrix} L^T, \quad L = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ UT^{-1} & I \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Since \(L\) is nonsingular, checking that the Rayleigh quotient of \(M\) is nonneg­ative is equivalent to checking the block diagonal factor. Since \(T \succ 0\) by assumption, this reduces to \(X - UT^{-1}U^T \succeq 0\). \(\square\)</div>

A special case (\(m=1, T=1\)) gives: \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x^T \\ x & X \end{bmatrix} \succeq 0 \iff X - xx^T \succeq 0\). This is the SDP-based reformulation of the constraint "\(X\) dominates \(xx^T\)" — foundational for lifting vector quadratics into SDPs.

### 1.4 Complementarity

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Proposition</strong> (Complementarity). Let \(X, S \in \mathcal{S}^n_+\). Then
\[
\langle X, S \rangle = 0 \iff XS = 0.
\]</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> The forward direction is immediate: \(\langle X, S \rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(XS) = \operatorname{Tr}(0) = 0\). For the reverse: if \(\langle X, S \rangle = 0\), write \(0 = \operatorname{Tr}(XS) = \operatorname{Tr}(X^{1/2} S X^{1/2})\). The matrix \(X^{1/2} S X^{1/2} \succeq 0\) (since \(S \succeq 0\)) and has zero trace, so all its eigenvalues are zero, giving \(X^{1/2} S X^{1/2} = 0\). It follows that \(X^{1/2} S^{1/2} = 0\) and hence \(XS = 0\). \(\square\)</div>

This result plays the role for SDPs that \(x_j \cdot s_j = 0\) plays for LP: it is the complementary slackness condition characterizing optimal pairs.

## Chapter 2: Semidefinite Programs

### 2.1 Standard Form

Recall that a **linear program** optimizes a linear function subject to linear constraints. A **semidefinite program** (SDP) optimizes a linear function of a matrix variable subject to linear (affine) constraints and a PSD constraint.

Given \(C \in \mathcal{S}^n\), \(b \in \mathbb{R}^m\), and a linear transformation \(\mathcal{A}: \mathcal{S}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\) specified by matrices \(A_1, \ldots, A_m \in \mathcal{S}^n\) with \([\mathcal{A}(X)]_i = \langle A_i, X \rangle\), the **primal SDP** is:
\[
(P) \quad \inf_{X \in \mathcal{S}^n} \langle C, X \rangle \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{A}(X) = b, \quad X \succeq 0.
\]

![2D cross-section of SDP feasible set](/pics/co471/sdp-feasible-set.png)

The feasible set is the intersection of the PSD cone with an affine subspace — a **spectrahedron**. It is convex by construction. The objective \(\langle C, X \rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(CX)\) is linear in \(X\), so the problem is convex.

**Why SDPs?** The PSD constraint \(X \succeq 0\) is highly nonlinear (in the original matrix entries), yet computationally tractable. It allows one to encode, for instance, the constraint that a Gram matrix \(X = V^T V\) represents unit vectors with prescribed inner products — the foundation for relaxing combinatorial problems like MAX-CUT.

### 2.2 The SDP Dual

The **adjoint** \(\mathcal{A}^*: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathcal{S}^n\) of \(\mathcal{A}\) is the unique linear map satisfying
\[
\langle \mathcal{A}^*(y), X \rangle = \langle y, \mathcal{A}(X) \rangle \quad \forall X \in \mathcal{S}^n, y \in \mathbb{R}^m,
\]
and one verifies \(\mathcal{A}^*(y) = \sum_{i=1}^m y_i A_i\). The **dual SDP** is:
\[
(D) \quad \sup_{y \in \mathbb{R}^m, S \in \mathcal{S}^n} b^T y \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{A}^*(y) + S = C, \quad S \succeq 0.
\]

The dual variable \(S \succeq 0\) acts as a slack in the dual constraint: feasibility of \((D)\) means \(C - \mathcal{A}^*(y) \succeq 0\). The dual of \((D)\) is equivalent to \((P)\), so SDP duality is an involution.

### 2.3 Weak Duality

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Weak Duality). Let \(\bar{X}\) be feasible in \((P)\) and \((\bar{y}, \bar{S})\) be feasible in \((D)\). Then
\[
\langle C, \bar{X} \rangle - b^T \bar{y} = \langle \bar{X}, \bar{S} \rangle \geq 0.
\]</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong>
\[
\langle C, \bar{X} \rangle - b^T \bar{y} = \langle \mathcal{A}^*(\bar{y}) + \bar{S},\, \bar{X}\rangle - b^T \bar{y} = \langle \bar{S}, \bar{X} \rangle + \bar{y}^T \mathcal{A}(\bar{X}) - b^T \bar{y} = \langle \bar{X}, \bar{S} \rangle \geq 0,
\]
using \(\mathcal{A}(\bar{X}) = b\) and \(\bar{X}, \bar{S} \succeq 0\). \(\square\)</div>

Consequences: if \((P)\) is unbounded below, then \((D)\) is infeasible; if \((D)\) is unbounded above, then \((P)\) is infeasible. If \(\langle \bar{X}, \bar{S} \rangle = 0\) for some feasible pair, then both are optimal.

### 2.4 Certifying Infeasibility

The SDP analogue of Farkas' lemma is more subtle — there is no exact analogue. However:

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong>. Let \(\mathcal{A}: \mathcal{S}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\) be linear and \(C \in \mathcal{S}^n\).
<ol>
<li>If there exists \(D \in \mathcal{S}^n_+\) with \(\mathcal{A}(D) = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Tr}(CD) < 0\), then \(\mathcal{A}^*(y) \preceq C\) is infeasible.</li>
<li>If no such \(D\) exists, then \(\mathcal{A}^*(y) \preceq C\) is <em>almost feasible</em> (feasible for every \(\epsilon\)-perturbation of \(C\)).</li>
</ol></div>

The concept of **almost feasibility** is uniquely SDP: unlike LP, infeasibility and near-infeasibility can differ. This is one of the genuine pitfalls of semidefinite programming.

## Chapter 3: Duality Theory

### 3.1 Conic Duality Framework

The SDP framework is a special case of conic convex optimization. The relevant cone here is \(K = \mathcal{S}^n_+\), with \(K^* = \mathcal{S}^n_+\) (self-dual). The general primal–dual pair is:
\[
(P): \inf \langle c, x \rangle,\ \mathcal{A}(x) = b,\ x \in K
\qquad
(D): \sup \langle b, y \rangle + \langle d, u \rangle,\ \mathcal{A}^*(y) + \mathcal{B}^*(u) + s = c,\ u \geq 0,\ s \in K^*.
\]

The following definitions formalize the two key cones used in duality:

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Dual Cone). Given \(K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\),
\[
K^* := \{s \in \mathbb{R}^d : \langle x, s \rangle \geq 0 \text{ for all } x \in K\}.
\]
Under the trace inner product, \((\mathcal{S}^n_+)^* = \mathcal{S}^n_+\).</div>

### 3.2 The Slater Condition

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Slater Point). We say that \((P)\) has a <strong>Slater point</strong> if there exists \(\bar{X} \in \mathcal{S}^n_{++}\) (strictly positive definite) satisfying \(\mathcal{A}(\bar{X}) = b\). Similarly, \((D)\) has a Slater point if there exist \(\bar{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m\) and \(\bar{S} \in \mathcal{S}^n_{++}\) with \(\mathcal{A}^*(\bar{y}) + \bar{S} = C\).</div>

Slater points — strictly interior feasible solutions — are the regularity condition that tames duality gaps in SDP. Under LP, feasibility alone suffices; under SDP, one needs strict interior feasibility.

## Chapter 4: Strong Duality, Slater Condition, and Facial Reduction

### 4.1 Strong Duality

![Primal–dual convergence to zero duality gap](/pics/co471/strong-duality-gap.png)

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Strong Duality). Suppose \((D)\) has a Slater point and its optimal value is finite. Then \((P)\) attains its optimal value and
\[
\inf_{X \succeq 0,\ \mathcal{A}(X)=b} \langle C, X \rangle = \sup_{y,\ C - \mathcal{A}^*(y) \succeq 0} b^T y.
\]</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Let \(\bar{y}, \bar{S} \in \mathcal{S}^n_{++}\) be a Slater point for \((D)\), and let \(z^* := \sup\{b^T y : \mathcal{A}^*(y) + S = C,\, S \succeq 0\}\) be finite. Define
\[
G_1 := \mathcal{S}^n_{++}, \quad G_2 := \{S \in \mathcal{S}^n : \exists y,\, S = C - \mathcal{A}^*(y),\, b^T y \geq z^*\}.
\]
Both are convex and \(G_1 \cap G_2 = \emptyset\) (otherwise \(z^*\) would be exceeded). By the hyperplane separation theorem, there exists \(\tilde{X} \in \mathcal{S}^n \setminus \{0\}\) such that
\[
\inf_{S \in G_1} \langle \tilde{X}, S \rangle \geq \sup_{S \in G_2} \langle \tilde{X}, S \rangle.
\]
Since \(G_1 = \mathcal{S}^n_{++}\) is a cone and the infimum cannot be \(-\infty\), we get \(\tilde{X} \in \mathcal{S}^n_+\). One shows \(\mathcal{A}(\tilde{X}) = \alpha b\) for some \(\alpha > 0\), and \(\hat{X} := \tilde{X}/\alpha\) is primal optimal. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Corollary.</strong>
<ul>
<li>If \((D)\) is feasible and \((P)\) has a Slater point, then \((D)\) attains its optimal value and strong duality holds.</li>
<li>If both \((P)\) and \((D)\) have Slater points, then both attain their optimal values with equal objective.</li>
</ul></div>

### 4.2 Pitfalls Without Slater Points

Unlike LP, SDPs can exhibit pathological behavior when Slater points are absent:

1. **Duality gap**: Even with both primal and dual feasible and bounded, the optimal values of \((P)\) and \((D)\) may differ.
2. **Non-attainment**: The infimum in \((P)\) (or supremum in \((D)\)) may not be achieved.
3. **Strict complementarity may fail**: Even with Slater points, there may be no optimal pair \(\bar{X}, (\bar{y}, \bar{S})\) satisfying \(\bar{X} + \bar{S} \succ 0\) (rank complementarity). Compare with LP, where strict complementarity always holds.

In LP, feasibility of both primal and dual guarantees attainment. In SDP, only Slater's condition (strict interior feasibility) provides this guarantee.

### 4.3 Faces of the PSD Cone and Facial Reduction

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Face of a Cone). Let \(K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) be a closed convex cone. A convex cone \(G \subseteq K\) is a <strong>face</strong> of \(K\) if for every \(u, v \in K\) with \(u + v \in G\), we have \(u \in G\) and \(v \in G\). A face is <strong>exposed</strong> if \(G = \{x \in K : \langle a, x \rangle = 0\}\) for some \(a \in -K^*\).</div>

![Facial reduction: feasible region on a face of the PSD cone](/pics/co471/facial-reduction.png)

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Structure of Faces of \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\)).
<ol>
<li>Every nonempty face \(G \subseteq \mathcal{S}^n_+\) is characterized by a unique subspace \(L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\): \(G = \{X \in \mathcal{S}^n_+ : \operatorname{Null}(X) \supseteq L\}\).</li>
<li>Every proper face of \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\) is exposed.</li>
<li>\(\mathcal{S}^n_+\) is projectionally exposed: every face is \((I - Q)\mathcal{S}^n_+(I-Q)\) for a projection \(Q\).</li>
</ol></div>

Every proper face of \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\) is linearly isomorphic to \(\mathcal{S}^k_+\) for some \(k < n\). This means the feasible region of \((P)\) lives in a smaller cone \(\mathcal{S}^k_+\), and replacing \((P)\) by an equivalent SDP over \(\mathcal{S}^k_+\) can restore the Slater condition.

**Facial Reduction Algorithm.** Given that \((P)\) lacks a Slater point, find \(\bar{y}\) such that \(\mathcal{A}^*(\bar{y}) \in \mathcal{S}^n_+ \setminus \{0\}\) with \(b^T \bar{y} = 0\). The hyperplane \(\{X : \langle X, \mathcal{A}^*(\bar{y}) \rangle = 0\}\) is a supporting hyperplane of \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\) containing the feasible region, exposing a proper face. By iterating at most \(n\) times, we arrive at an SDP over \(\mathcal{S}^k_+\) that necessarily satisfies the Slater condition (by minimality of the face). This is **facial reduction**.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Lemma</strong> (Dichotomy). Exactly one of the following systems has a solution:
<ol>
<li>\(\mathcal{A}(X) = b\), \(X \in \mathcal{S}^n_{++}\) (Slater point exists).</li>
<li>\(\mathcal{A}^*(y) \in \mathcal{S}^n_+ \setminus \{0\}\), \(b^T y = 0\) (facial reduction direction exists).</li>
</ol></div>

### 4.4 Extended Lagrange-Slater Dual

For the dual program \((D): \sup b^T y,\ \mathcal{A}^*(y) \preceq C\), when Slater's condition fails one may pass to the **Extended Lagrange-Slater Dual (ELSD)**:
\[
(\text{ELSD}): \inf \operatorname{Tr}(C(U+W)),\quad \mathcal{A}(U+W) = b,\quad W \in \mathcal{W}^n,\quad U \succeq 0,
\]
where \(\mathcal{W}^n \subseteq \mathcal{S}^n\) is a linear subspace determined by \(\mathcal{A}\) and \(C\).

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> If \((D)\) has a finite optimal value, then the optimal values of \((D)\) and \(\text{(ELSD)}\) coincide, and \(\text{(ELSD)}\) attains its optimal value.</div>

The ELSD thus "fixes" attainment when the primal lacks a Slater point, at the cost of enlarging the problem.

## Chapter 5: Solving SDPs

SDPs can be solved in polynomial time by two main algorithmic paradigms: the **ellipsoid method** (theoretically important, polynomial in all inputs) and **primal-dual interior-point methods** (practically efficient with \(O(\sqrt{n})\) iterations).

### 5.1 The Ellipsoid Method

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Ellipsoid). \(E \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) is an ellipsoid if
\[
E = E(A, c) := \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : (x-c)^T A^{-1} (x-c) \leq 1\}
\]
for some center \(c \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and shape matrix \(A \in \mathcal{S}^d_{++}\). Its volume is \(\sqrt{\det A} \cdot \text{vol}(B_d(0,1))\).</div>

![Ellipsoid method: successive volume reduction around feasible set](/pics/co471/ellipsoid-method.png)

The ellipsoid method needs only a **weak separation oracle**: given a point \(\bar{x}\), either confirm \(\bar{x}\) is near-feasible or produce a separating hyperplane. In each iteration, the new ellipsoid containing the feasible half of the current ellipsoid loses at least a \(1/2d\) fraction of volume:
\[
\ln \frac{\text{vol}(E^+)}{\text{vol}(E)} \leq -\frac{1}{2d}.
\]

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Ellipsoid Complexity). Given a convex set \(G \subseteq B_d(0,R)\) with a weak separation oracle, after \(O\bigl(d^2 \ln(R/\epsilon)\bigr)\) iterations the ellipsoid method either finds a point in \(\text{relax}(G, \epsilon)\) or certifies \(\text{vol}(G) \leq \epsilon\).</div>

For optimization, one additionally needs a subgradient oracle for the objective. The ellipsoid method can solve any SDP (with suitable bounds on the solution) in polynomial time.

To apply the ellipsoid method to SDPs, one observes that given Slater points \(\bar{X}\) for \((P)\) and \(\bar{S}\) for \((D)\), the modified primal
\[
(\tilde{P}): \quad \inf \langle C, X \rangle,\quad \mathcal{A}(X) = b,\quad \langle \bar{S}, X \rangle \leq 2\langle \bar{X}, \bar{S} \rangle,\quad X \succeq 0
\]
has a compact, convex feasible set amenable to the ellipsoid method.

### 5.2 Primal-Dual Interior-Point Methods

Interior-point methods generate sequences \((X^{(k)}, y^{(k)}, S^{(k)})\) with \(X^{(k)}, S^{(k)} \succ 0\), maintaining primal and dual feasibility while driving the **duality gap** \(\langle X^{(k)}, S^{(k)} \rangle \to 0\).

**Barrier function.** Define the **log-barrier**
\[
f(X) := -\ln \det X, \quad X \in \mathcal{S}^n_{++},
\]
with gradient \(f'(X)[H] = -\operatorname{Tr}(X^{-1}H)\) and Hessian \(f''(X)[H,H] = \operatorname{Tr}(X^{-1}HX^{-1}H)\). This function blows up at the boundary of the PSD cone, naturally enforcing \(X \succ 0\).

### 5.3 The Central Path

For each \(\mu > 0\), consider the barrier-penalized primal:
\[
(P_\mu): \quad \inf \frac{1}{\mu}\langle C, X \rangle - \ln \det X, \quad \mathcal{A}(X) = b.
\]
Under Slater's condition, \((P_\mu)\) has a unique optimal solution \(X(\mu)\). Paired with the dual, the optimality conditions yield the **central path equations**:
\[
\mathcal{A}(X) = b, \quad X \succ 0, \quad \mathcal{A}^*(y) + S = C, \quad S = \mu X^{-1}.
\]

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Central Path). Under Slater's condition with \(\mathcal{A}\) surjective, for every \(\mu > 0\) there is a unique solution \((X(\mu), y(\mu), S(\mu))\) to the central path equations. Along this path,
\[
\langle X(\mu), S(\mu) \rangle = \mu \operatorname{Tr}(I) = n\mu \to 0 \text{ as } \mu \to 0.
\]</div>

### 5.4 Path-Following and Potential Reduction

**Closeness to the central path** is measured by
\[
\psi(X, S) := n \ln \frac{\operatorname{Tr}(XS)}{n} + f(X) + f(S) = n \ln \frac{\operatorname{Tr}(XS)}{n} - \ln \det X - \ln \det S.
\]
By the AM-GM inequality applied to the eigenvalues of \(S^{1/2}XS^{1/2}\), \(\psi(X,S) \geq 0\) with equality iff \(S = \mu X^{-1}\) (i.e., on the central path).

The **primal-dual potential function** is
\[
\phi_\rho(X, S) := \rho \ln \langle X, S \rangle + \psi(X, S), \quad \rho > 0.
\]
Taking \(\rho = \sqrt{n}\), one designs search directions \(D_X, D_S\) that decrease \(\phi_{\sqrt{n}}\) by an absolute constant \(\delta > 0\) per iteration.

**Search direction.** For every pair \(X, S \in \mathcal{S}^n_{++}\), there is a scaling automorphism \(T \in \text{Aut}(\mathcal{S}^n_+)\) of the form \(T(Z) = WZW\) mapping \((X,S) \mapsto (V,V)\) for \(V = WSW = W^{-1}XW^{-1}\). In the \(V\)-space, the search direction that maximally reduces the duality gap is the projection of \(-V\) onto the feasible subspace, balancing closeness to the central path through a linear combination of \(V^{-1}\) and \(V\).

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Interior-Point Complexity). Starting from Slater points \(X^{(0)}, S^{(0)}\) with \(\psi(X^{(0)}, S^{(0)}) \leq \sqrt{n} \ln(1/\epsilon)\), the primal-dual potential reduction algorithm terminates in at most
\[
O\!\left(\sqrt{n} \ln \frac{\langle X^{(0)}, S^{(0)}\rangle}{\epsilon}\right)
\]
iterations with \(\langle X^{(k)}, S^{(k)} \rangle \leq \epsilon \cdot \langle X^{(0)}, S^{(0)} \rangle\).</div>

**Finding initial Slater points.** One introduces an auxiliary SDP with a slack variable \(\xi \geq 0\):
\[
(P_{\text{aux}}): \quad \inf\, \xi \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{A}(X) + \xi(b - \mathcal{A}(I)) = b,\ \langle I, X \rangle \leq M,\ \xi \geq 0,\ X \succeq 0,
\]
for which \((X^{(0)}, \xi_0) = (I, 1)\) is a Slater point.

**Computational remarks.** For LP, data of bit-length \(L\) ensures rational optima and bounds \(\gamma \approx 2^{-L}, M \approx 2^L\). For SDP, even with binary data, the optimal solution may be irrational, and the bounds can be doubly exponential in \(L\). SDP feasibility is in NP and co-NP in the real-number model; whether it is in P (in the Turing machine model) remains **open**.

## Chapter 6: Applications

### 6.1 MAX-CUT via Goemans-Williamson

**Problem.** Given an undirected graph \(G = (V, E)\) with edge weights \(w \in \mathbb{R}^E_+\), find \(U \subseteq V\) maximizing \(\sum_{ij \in \delta(U)} w_{ij}\).

![MAX-CUT SDP relaxation on a 5-vertex graph](/pics/co471/sdp-example-maxcut.png)

**Nonconvex formulation.** Encode each cut by \(u \in \{-1, 1\}^n\) (with \(u_i = 1 \iff i \in U\)):
\[
\max_{u \in \{-1,1\}^n} \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i,j} w_{ij}(1 - u_i u_j).
\]
Identifying \(X \leftrightarrow uu^T\), this is equivalent to
\[
\max \tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(\langle W, \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T \rangle - \langle W, X \rangle\bigr) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \operatorname{diag}(X) = \mathbf{1},\quad X \succeq 0,\quad \operatorname{rank}(X) = 1.
\]

**SDP relaxation.** Drop the rank-1 constraint:
\[
(\text{SDP}): \quad \max -\tfrac{1}{4}\operatorname{Tr}(WX) + \tfrac{1}{4}\mathbf{1}^T W \mathbf{1} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \operatorname{diag}(X) = \mathbf{1},\quad X \succeq 0.
\]

**Goemans-Williamson random hyperplane rounding.** Given an optimal solution \(\hat{X} = BB^T\) with columns \(v^{(1)}, \ldots, v^{(n)}\) (unit vectors), pick \(r\) uniformly on the unit sphere and set \(U = \{i : r^T v^{(i)} \geq 0\}\).

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Goemans-Williamson '95). The expected weight of the cut satisfies
\[
\mathbb{E}[\text{GW-Cut}] \geq \rho \cdot \text{OPT},
\]
where \(\rho = \min_{u \in [-1,1]} \frac{2}{\pi} \frac{\arccos(u)}{(1-u)/2} \approx 0.87856\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof sketch.</strong> The probability that vertices \(i, j\) are separated is \(\frac{\theta_{ij}}{\pi}\) where \(\theta_{ij} = \arccos\langle v^{(i)}, v^{(j)} \rangle\). The key lemma shows \(\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos(u) \geq \frac{\rho}{2}(1-u)\) for all \(u \in [-1,1]\), giving
\[
\mathbb{E}[\text{GW-Cut}] = \sum_{ij} w_{ij} \frac{\theta_{ij}}{\pi} \geq \frac{\rho}{4}\sum_{ij} w_{ij}(1 - \langle v^{(i)}, v^{(j)}\rangle) = \rho \cdot \text{OPT(SDP)} \geq \rho \cdot \text{OPT}.
\]
\(\square\)</div>

This approximation ratio \(\rho \approx 0.878\) is **optimal** assuming the Unique Games Conjecture (Khot-Vishnoi).

### 6.2 Quadratic Optimization over Sign Vectors

For \(W \in \mathcal{S}^n\), define \(\bar{f}(W) := \max_{x \in \{-1,1\}^n} x^T W x\) and its SDP relaxation \(\bar{F}(W) := \max\{\operatorname{Tr}(WX) : \operatorname{diag}(X) = \mathbf{1},\, X \succeq 0\}\).

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong>. For every \(W \in \mathcal{S}^n_+\),
\[
\frac{2}{\pi} \bar{F}(W) \leq \bar{f}(W) \leq \bar{F}(W).
\]
For general \(W \in \mathcal{S}^n\), a refined bound still yields a \((\frac{2}{\pi})^2\)-approximation for the gap \(\bar{f}(W) - f(W)\).</div>

The key identity is \(\bar{f}(W) = \frac{2}{\pi} \max\{\langle W, \arcsin(X) \rangle : \operatorname{diag}(X) = \mathbf{1},\, X \succeq 0\}\), connecting the nonconvex problem to a transformed SDP via the arcsine function.

### 6.3 The Lovász Theta Function

The **stable set polytope** \(\text{STAB}(G) := \text{conv}\{\chi^S : S \text{ stable in } G\}\) is NP-hard to optimize over in general. The **fractional stable set polytope** is
\[
\text{FRAC}(G) := \{x \in [0,1]^V : x_i + x_j \leq 1 \text{ for all } ij \in E\}.
\]

![Petersen graph: Lovász theta and stable set structure](/pics/co471/lovasz-theta.png)

The **theta body** interpolates between them:
<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Theta Body). For a graph \(G = (V, E)\),
\[
\text{TH}(G) := \{x \in \mathbb{R}^V_+ : \forall c \in \mathbb{R}^n, \|c\|_2 = 1,\ \forall u : V \to \mathbb{R}^n \text{ orthonormal repr. of } G,\quad \sum_{j \in V} (c^T u(j))^2 x_j \leq 1\}.
\]
An orthonormal representation assigns unit vectors \(v(i)\) to vertices with \(\langle v(i), v(j)\rangle = 0\) for all \(ij \notin E\).</div>

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Lovász Sandwich). For every graph \(G\),
\[
\text{STAB}(G) \subseteq \text{TH}(G) \subseteq \text{CLQ}(G) \subseteq \text{FRAC}(G).
\]</div>

The **Lovász theta function** \(\theta(G) := \max_{x \in \text{TH}(G)} \mathbf{1}^T x\) is computable by SDP and satisfies:
<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Characterizations of \(\theta(G)\)). The following are all equal:
<ul>
<li>\(\max\{\operatorname{Tr}(W X) : X_{ij} = 0\, \forall ij \in E,\, \operatorname{Tr}(X) = 1,\, X \succeq 0\}\) (primal SDP).</li>
<li>\(\min\{\eta : \eta I - S \succeq W,\, \operatorname{diag}(S) = 0,\, S_{ij} = 0\, \forall ij \notin E\}\) (dual SDP).</li>
<li>\(\min_{u \text{ ortho. repr.}, c} \max_i (c^T u(i))^{-2}\) (geometric characterization).</li>
</ul></div>

**Shannon capacity.** The Shannon capacity of a graph models the maximum rate of error-free communication over a noisy channel modeled by \(G\):
\[
\Theta(G) := \limsup_{k \to \infty} \alpha(G^{\boxtimes k})^{1/k},
\]
where \(G^{\boxtimes k}\) is the strong product and \(\alpha\) is the independence number.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Lovász '79). For every graph \(G\),
\[
\alpha(G) \leq \Theta(G) \leq \theta(G) \leq \chi(\bar{G}).
\]
Moreover, \(\theta(G \boxtimes H) = \theta(G) \cdot \theta(H)\), so \(\Theta(G) \leq \theta(G)\).</div>

For the **Petersen graph**, \(\alpha = 4\) and \(\theta = 4\), confirming \(\Theta(\text{Petersen}) = 4\) — a result that eluded direct combinatorial proof for years.

**Perfect graphs.** Lovász's theorem takes its most elegant form for perfect graphs:
<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Perfect Graph Theorem). The following are equivalent for a graph \(G\):
<ol>
<li>\(G\) is perfect (i.e., \(\omega(H) = \chi(H)\) for every induced subgraph \(H\)).</li>
<li>\(G\) contains no odd-hole or odd-antihole (Strong Perfect Graph Theorem, Chudnovsky et al. 2006).</li>
<li>\(\text{STAB}(G) = \text{TH}(G) = \text{CLQ}(G)\).</li>
</ol></div>

### 6.4 Lift-and-Project Methods

Given a polytope \(P \subseteq [0,1]^d\) and the target \(P_I := \text{conv}(P \cap \{0,1\}^d)\), the **\(\text{LS}_+\) operator** lifts \(P\) into a spectrahedron and projects back:
\[
\text{LS}_+(P) := \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : \exists Y \in M_+(K),\ (1, x)^T = Y e_0\},
\]
where \(M_+(K)\) is the PSD-feasible region of a lifted system encoding \(Y e_i \in K\) and \(Y(e_0 - e_i) \in K\) for all \(i\).

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem.</strong>
\[
P \supseteq \text{LS}_+(P) \supseteq \text{LS}^2_+(P) \supseteq \cdots \supseteq \text{LS}^d_+(P) = \text{conv}(P \cap \{0,1\}^d).
\]
Moreover, \(\text{LS}_+(G)) \subseteq \text{OC}(G) \cap \text{ANTI-HOLE}(G) \cap \text{WHEEL}(G) \cap \text{CLQ}(G) \cap \text{TH}(G)\) for \(G = \text{FRAC}(G)\).</div>

Applied to \(\text{FRAC}(G)\), a single application of \(\text{LS}_+\) recovers odd-cycle inequalities, odd-antihole inequalities, and odd-wheel inequalities simultaneously — a remarkable unification of combinatorial cutting plane methods via SDP.

### 6.5 Sum-of-Squares and Polynomial Optimization

A polynomial \(p(x)\) of degree \(2d\) is a **sum of squares (SOS)** if \(p(x) = h(x)^T X h(x)\) for some \(X \succeq 0\), where \(h(x)\) is the vector of monomials of degree \(\leq d\). Checking SOS-ness is an SDP in \(X\).

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (SOS Certificate). A polynomial \(p(x) - z\) is a sum of squares if and only if \(\{X \in \mathcal{S}^N_+ : h(x)^T X h(x) = p(x)\} \cap \{X : X \succeq z e_1 e_1^T\} \neq \emptyset\).</div>

This gives a hierarchy of SDP relaxations for polynomial optimization:
\[
\inf_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n} p_0(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad p_1(x) \geq 0, \ldots, p_m(x) \geq 0.
\]
For each degree bound \(d\), one solves an SDP whose feasibility certifies positivity of \(p_0\) on the feasible set. By Stengle's Positivstellensatz (the real analogue of Hilbert's Nullstellensatz), the hierarchy is complete.

### 6.6 Extension Complexity

Given a polytope \(P \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) with \(m\) facets and \(n\) extreme points, the **slack matrix** \(S \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}_+\) has \(S_{ij} = b_i - \langle a^{(i)}, v^{(j)} \rangle\). The **nonnegative rank** \(\text{rank}_+(S)\) is the smallest \(k\) for which \(S = FV^T\) with \(F \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}_+\), \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}_+\).

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Yannakakis '91). The **extension complexity** \(\text{xc}(P)\) (minimum facets in any polyhedral lift of \(P\)) equals \(\text{rank}_+(S)\).</div>

The SDP generalization (Gouveia-Parrilo-Thomas '13): a convex set \(G\) has a lifted \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\)-representation if and only if the slack function \(S_G(x,s) = 1 - \langle x, s \rangle\) has a \(\mathcal{S}^n_+\)-factorization — pairing \(\text{ext}(G) \to \mathcal{S}^n_+\) with \(\text{ext}(G^\circ) \to \mathcal{S}^n_+\).
