# Lecture 1: Introduction

## The Probability Space

Random graph theory sits at the intersection of combinatorics and probability, asking: what does a "typical" large graph look like? To make this precise, we work inside a **probability space**, denoted by the triple <span>&#92;((\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{F})&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(\mathcal{G}&#92;)</span> is a class of graphs, <span>&#92;(\mathcal{P}&#92;)</span> is a probability measure, and <span>&#92;(\mathcal{F}&#92;)</span> is a sigma-algebra. In practice, <span>&#92;(\mathcal{G}&#92;)</span> is finite, <span>&#92;(\mathcal{P}&#92;)</span> is a discrete probability measure, and <span>&#92;(\mathcal{F} = 2^{\mathcal{G}}&#92;)</span> is the full power set.

A **discrete probability space** consists of a countable set <span>&#92;(\Omega&#92;)</span> and a probability function <span>&#92;(P : \Omega \to [0,1]&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(\sum&#95;{\omega \in \Omega} P(\omega) = 1&#92;)</span>. A subset of <span>&#92;(\Omega&#92;)</span> is an event, with probability <span>&#92;(P(A) = \sum&#95;{\omega \in A} P(\omega)&#92;)</span>. A **random variable** is a function <span>&#92;(X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}&#92;)</span> with expectation <span>&#92;(E(X) = \sum&#95;{\omega \in \Omega} X(\omega) P(\omega)&#92;)</span>.

Key tools from elementary probability carry over immediately. The **Inclusion-Exclusion Principle** gives:
<span>&#92;[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = \sum_i P(A_i) - \sum_{i_1 < i_2} P(A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2}) + \cdots + (-1)^{n-1} P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i\right). &#92;]</span>
The **Union Bound** (Boole's inequality) is the convenient weakening <span>&#92;(P\!\left(\bigcup A&#95;i\right) \leq \sum P(A&#95;i)&#92;)</span>. Two events are **independent** if <span>&#92;(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)&#92;)</span>. **Linearity of expectation** states <span>&#92;(E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)&#92;)</span> for any two random variables, with no independence assumption required.

## The Erdős–Rényi Models

The two central models in this course are both due to Erdős and Rényi.

- **<span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span>**: a graph on vertex set <span>&#92;([n] = \{1, \ldots, n\}&#92;)</span> is constructed by including each of the <span>&#92;(\binom{n}{2}&#92;)</span> possible edges independently with probability <span>&#92;(p&#92;)</span>.
- **<span>&#92;(G(n,m)&#92;)</span>**: a graph is chosen uniformly at random from all graphs on <span>&#92;([n]&#92;)</span> with exactly <span>&#92;(m&#92;)</span> edges.

One can think of <span>&#92;(G(n,m)&#92;)</span> as labelling the edges of <span>&#92;(K&#95;n&#92;)</span> with a random permutation and taking the first <span>&#92;(m&#92;)</span>. Other models exist: the random <span>&#92;(d&#92;)</span>-regular graph <span>&#92;(G(n,d)&#92;)</span>, the random graph with prescribed degree sequence <span>&#92;(G(n,\tilde{d})&#92;)</span>, the random geometric graph <span>&#92;(G(n,r)&#92;)</span> on <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span> points in the unit square with edges at distance <span>&#92;(\leq r&#92;)</span>, and random labelled trees (one of <span>&#92;(n^{n-2}&#92;)</span> trees by Cayley's formula). This course focuses primarily on the Erdős–Rényi model.

## Asymptotic Notation and a.a.s

Given a sequence of probability spaces <span>&#92;((\Omega&#95;n, P&#95;n)&#95;{n \geq 1}&#92;)</span>, we say an event <span>&#92;(A&#95;n&#92;)</span> holds **asymptotically almost surely** (**a.a.s**) if <span>&#92;(P&#95;n(A&#95;n) \to 1&#92;)</span> as <span>&#92;(n \to \infty&#92;)</span>. This is the standard mode of convergence throughout random graph theory.

### Useful Estimates

A key approximation used repeatedly: if <span>&#92;(k = o(\sqrt{n})&#92;)</span> then
<span>&#92;[ \binom{n}{k} \sim \frac{n^k}{k!}. &#92;]</span>
More precisely, <span>&#92;\frac{n^k}{k!} \leq \left(\frac{en}{k}\right)^k&#92;</span>. Stirling's formula gives <span>&#92;(n! = \sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n(1 + O(n^{-1}))&#92;)</span>, and the inequality <span>&#92;(e^t \geq 1 + t&#92;)</span> holds for all <span>&#92;(t \in \mathbb{R}&#92;)</span>.

# Lecture 2: Concentration Inequalities, Coupling, and the Connection Theorem

## Concentration Inequalities

The two classical one-sided and two-sided tail bounds are fundamental throughout.

**Markov's Inequality**: for any non-negative random variable <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(t > 0&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ \Pr(X \geq t) \leq \frac{E[X]}{t}. &#92;]</span>
*Proof*: let <span>&#92;(I&#95;t = \mathbf{1}[X \geq t]&#92;)</span>; then <span>&#92;(X \geq t \cdot I&#95;t&#92;)</span>, so taking expectations gives <span>&#92;(E[X] \geq t \cdot P(X \geq t)&#92;)</span>.

**Chebyshev's Inequality**: for any random variable <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(t \geq 0&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ P(|X - E[X]| \geq t) \leq \frac{\text{Var}(X)}{t^2}. &#92;]</span>

As an example, let <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> be the number of edges in <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span>, so <span>&#92;(X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(N, p)&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(N = \binom{n}{2}&#92;)</span>. Then <span>&#92;(E[X] = Np&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(\text{Var}(X) = p(1-p)N&#92;)</span>. By Chebyshev, for any <span>&#92;(f&#95;n \to \infty&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ P\!\left(|X - Np| \geq f&#95;n\sqrt{p(1-p)N}\right) \leq \frac{1}{f&#95;n^2} = o(1). &#92;]</span>

## Coupling

A **coupling** of two random variables <span>&#92;(X, Y&#92;)</span> is a joint distribution <span>&#92;((\hat{X}, \hat{Y})&#92;)</span> on the same probability space such that marginally <span>&#92;(\hat{X} \sim X&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(\hat{Y} \sim Y&#92;)</span>.

**Coupling Lemma**:
(a) For <span>&#92;(0 \leq p_1 < p_2 \leq 1&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(0 \leq m_1 < m_2 \leq N&#92;)</span>, there exist couplings such that <span>&#92;(G(n,p&#95;1) \subseteq G(n,p&#95;2)&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(G(n,m&#95;1) \subseteq G(n,m&#95;2)&#92;)</span> always. For the <span>&#92;(p&#92;)</span>-model: start from <span>&#92;(G&#95;1 \sim G(n,p&#95;1)&#92;)</span> and add each non-edge of <span>&#92;(G&#95;1&#92;)</span> independently with probability <span>&#92;(q = 1 - (1-p&#95;2)/(1-p&#95;1)&#92;)</span>; then <span>&#92;(G&#95;2 \sim G(n,p&#95;2)&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(G&#95;1 \subseteq G&#95;2&#92;)</span> always.

(b) Let <span>&#92;(m&#95;1 = pN - f\sqrt{p(1-p)N}&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(m&#95;2 = pN + f\sqrt{p(1-p)N}&#92;)</span>. There exists a coupling <span>&#92;((G&#95;1, H, G&#95;2)&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(G&#95;1 \sim G(n,m&#95;1)&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(H \sim G(n,p)&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(G&#95;2 \sim G(n,m&#95;2)&#92;)</span>, and <span>&#92;(P(G&#95;1 \subseteq H \subseteq G&#95;2) = 1 - o(1)&#92;)</span>.

A graph property <span>&#92;(Q&#92;)</span> is **monotone increasing** if <span>&#92;(G \in Q&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(H \supseteq G&#92;)</span> implies <span>&#92;(H \in Q&#92;)</span>. It is **convex** if <span>&#92;(G&#95;1, G&#95;2 \in Q&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(G&#95;1 \subseteq G&#95;2&#92;)</span> implies <span>&#92;(H \in Q&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(G&#95;1 \subseteq H \subseteq G&#95;2&#92;)</span>.

For monotone properties, coupling implies: if <span>&#92;(p&#95;1 \leq p&#95;2&#92;)</span>, then <span>&#92;(P(G(n,p&#95;1) \in Q) \leq P(G(n,p&#95;2) \in Q)&#92;)</span>, and similarly for the <span>&#92;(m&#92;)</span>-model.

## The Connection Theorem

The connection theorem allows us to transfer results between the <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(G(n,m)&#92;)</span> models.

**Theorem 2.4** (Connection Theorem): Let <span>&#92;(Q&#92;)</span> be a graph property.

(i) Given <span>&#92;(p = p(n)&#92;)</span>. If for all <span>&#92;(m = pN + O(\sqrt{p(1-p)N})&#92;)</span> we have <span>&#92;(G(n,m) \in Q&#92;)</span> a.a.s, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p) \in Q&#92;)</span>.

(ii) If <span>&#92;(Q&#92;)</span> is convex and <span>&#92;(G(n, m/N) \in Q&#92;)</span> a.a.s, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,m) \in Q&#92;)</span>.

*Proof sketch*: Part (i) writes <span>&#92;(P(G(n,p) \in Q)&#92;)</span> via the law of total probability, conditioning on the edge count <span>&#92;(m&#92;)</span>. Proposition 2.1 says all but an <span>&#92;(o(1)&#92;)</span>-fraction of the probability mass lies within <span>&#92;(f&#95;n&#92;)</span> standard deviations of the mean. Part (ii) conditions on edge count and uses convexity with the coupling to transfer between models.

# Lecture 3: Thresholds and First-Order Logic

## Threshold Functions

A **threshold** for a property <span>&#92;(Q&#92;)</span> is a function <span>&#92;(p&#95;0(n)&#92;)</span> such that
<span>&#92;[ P(G(n,p) \in Q) \to \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } p \ll p&#95;0 \\ 1 & \text{if } p \gg p&#95;0. \end{cases} &#92;]</span>
A **sharp threshold** further requires that for all <span>&#92;(\varepsilon > 0&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ P(G(n,p) \in Q) \to \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } p \leq (1-\varepsilon)p&#95;0 \\ 1 & \text{if } p \geq (1+\varepsilon)p&#95;0. \end{cases} &#92;]</span>
The **window** of a threshold is <span>&#92;(\delta(\varepsilon) = p&#95;{1-\varepsilon} - p&#95;\varepsilon&#92;)</span>. Bollobás and Thomason (1987) proved that every non-trivial monotone property has a threshold.

## First-Order Logic and the 0–1 Law

The **first-order logic of graphs** uses quantifiers <span>&#92;(\forall, \exists&#92;)</span>, equality, and the adjacency relation <span>&#92;(\sim&#92;)</span>. For example, the property of having diameter at most 2 is expressed as
<span>&#92;[ \forall x\, \forall y\, \exists z\; (x = y \;\vee\; x \sim y \;\vee\; (x \sim z \wedge y \sim z)). &#92;]</span>

Fix <span>&#92;(k > 0&#92;)</span> and let <span>&#92;(P&#95;k&#92;)</span> be the **extension property**: for any disjoint sets <span>&#92;(W, V \subseteq V(G)&#92;)</span> of order at most <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>, there exists a vertex <span>&#92;(x \notin W \cup V&#92;)</span> adjacent to every vertex in <span>&#92;(W&#92;)</span> and to none in <span>&#92;(V&#92;)</span>.

**Lemma 3.1**: If <span>&#92;(m(n)&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(p(n)&#92;)</span> satisfy <span>&#92;(mn^{-2+\varepsilon} \to \infty&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;((N-m)n^{-2+\varepsilon} \to \infty&#92;)</span> (resp. <span>&#92;(pn^\varepsilon \to \infty&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;((1-p)n^\varepsilon \to \infty&#92;)</span>) for every fixed <span>&#92;(\varepsilon > 0&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p) \in P&#95;k&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(G(n,m) \in P&#95;k&#92;)</span>.

**Theorem 3.2** (0–1 Law, first-order logic): Under the conditions of Lemma 3.1, every graph property expressible by a first-order sentence either holds a.a.s or fails a.a.s. *Proof sketch*: the argument uses an Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game. Player 1 picks vertices from either graph; Player 2 responds from the other graph. After <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span> rounds, Player 2 wins if the map <span>&#92;(v&#95;i \mapsto v&#95;i'&#92;)</span> is an isomorphism between the induced subgraphs. Two graphs sharing the same theory <span>&#92;(\mathrm{Th}&#95;k&#92;)</span> (first-order sentences of quantifier depth <span>&#92;(\leq k&#92;)</span>) are indistinguishable by Player 2.

# Lecture 4: Small Subgraphs and Theorem E

## Cycles and Small Subgraphs

**Lemma 4.1**: If <span>&#92;(p = o(1/n)&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> has no cycles. *Proof*: Markov's inequality applied to the expected cycle count.

Let <span>&#92;(Y&#95;k&#92;)</span> denote the number of <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>-cycles in <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span>. For <span>&#92;(p = c/n&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(c&#92;)</span> fixed, the expected number of <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>-cycles is
<span>&#92;[ E[Y&#95;k] = \binom{n}{k} \cdot \frac{(k-1)!}{2} \cdot p^k \sim \frac{n^k}{k!} \cdot \frac{(k-1)!}{2} \cdot \frac{c^k}{n^k} = \frac{c^k}{2k}. &#92;]</span>

**Theorem 5.2**: For <span>&#92;(p = c/n&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(c > 0&#92;)</span> fixed, <span>&#92;(Y&#95;k \to \mathrm{Po}(c^k/(2k))&#92;)</span> as <span>&#92;(n \to \infty&#92;)</span>.

The convergence to a Poisson distribution uses the **method of moments**: a sequence <span>&#92;((X&#95;n)&#92;)</span> converges to <span>&#92;(\mathrm{Po}(\lambda)&#92;)</span> if and only if all factorial moments satisfy <span>&#92;(E[(X&#95;n)&#95;r] \to \lambda^r&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(r \geq 0&#92;)</span> (where <span>&#92;((x)&#95;r = x(x-1)\cdots(x-r+1)&#92;)</span>). For the proof, one counts ordered lists of <span>&#92;(r&#92;)</span> vertex-disjoint <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>-cycles (the dominant contribution) and shows the vertex-intersecting cases contribute <span>&#92;(o(1)&#92;)</span>.

## Theorem E: The Evolution of Random Graphs

The following theorem, which we call **Theorem E**, describes how the component structure of <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> evolves as <span>&#92;(p&#92;)</span> increases from 0 to 1. It is one of the central results of the theory.

**Theorem E** (Erdős–Rényi, Bollobás–Łuczak):

**(a)** Fix <span>&#92;(k \geq 2&#92;)</span>. If <span>&#92;(n^{(k-2)/(k-1)} \ll p \ll n^{(k-1)/k - 2}&#92;)</span>... [forest regime with max component order <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>].

**(b)** If <span>&#92;(p \ll 1/n&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> is a forest and the largest component has order <span>&#92;(o(\log n)&#92;)</span>.

**(c)** If <span>&#92;(p = c/n&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(0 < c < 1&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s every component of <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> is a tree or unicyclic, and the largest component has order <span>&#92;(\Theta(\log n)&#92;)</span>.

**(d)** If <span>&#92;(p = c/n&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(c > 1&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> contains a **unique giant component** of linear order, and all other components have order <span>&#92;(O(\log n)&#92;)</span>.

**(e)** When <span>&#92;(p \geq (\log n + \log\log n + \omega(1))/n&#92;)</span>, a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> has a giant component and only finitely many isolated vertices.

**(f)** When <span>&#92;(p \geq (\log n + \omega(1))/n&#92;)</span>, a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> is connected and has a perfect matching (if <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span> is even) or a near-perfect matching.

**(g)** When <span>&#92;(p \geq (\log n + \log\log n + \omega(1))/n&#92;)</span>, a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> is **Hamiltonian**.

*Proof of (a)*: The forest statement follows from the cycle count computation above. To show the largest component has order exactly <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>, let <span>&#92;(X&#95;t&#92;)</span> be the number of trees of order <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span> in <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span>. Show <span>&#92;(E\!\left[\sum&#95;{t \geq k+1} X&#95;t\right] = o(1)&#92;)</span> and apply Markov's inequality. Then show existence of a tree of order <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span> by the second moment method.

# Lecture 5: Degree Distribution and the Critical Window

## Degree Distribution

Let <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> be the number of isolated vertices in <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span).

**Theorem 5.3**: Let <span>&#92;(p = c\log n / n&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(c > 0&#92;)</span> fixed.
- If <span>&#92;(c < 1&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s there exist isolated vertices.
- If <span>&#92;(c > 1&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s there are no isolated vertices.

This is a sharp threshold. **Theorem 5.6**: Let <span>&#92;(p = (\log n + c)/n&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(c&#92;)</span> fixed. Then
<span>&#92;[ P(G(n,p) \text{ is connected}) \to e^{-e^{-c}}. &#92;]</span>

*Proof sketch*: Let <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> count isolated vertices. Then <span>&#92;(E[X] = n(1-p)^{n-1} \sim ne^{-pn} = e^{-c}&#92;)</span> and the Poisson convergence <span>&#92;(X \to \mathrm{Po}(e^{-c})&#92;)</span> gives <span>&#92;(P(X = 0) \to e^{-e^{-c}}&#92;)</span>. One shows that connectedness fails a.a.s if and only if there are isolated vertices (the probability of more complex small components is negligible at this edge density).

## The Critical Window

The critical window is the regime <span>&#92;(p = (1 + \varepsilon)/n&#92;)</span> for <span>&#92;(\varepsilon = \varepsilon(n) \to 0&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(|\varepsilon|^3 n \to \infty&#92;)</span>. This is where the phase transition of Theorem E(d) actually occurs — near <span>&#92;(p = 1/n&#92;)</span>, the graph passes from the subcritical regime (many small tree components) to the supercritical regime (a unique giant component).

In the subcritical regime <span>&#92;(c < 1&#92;)</span>, the largest component has order <span>&#92;(\Theta(\log n)&#92;)</span>. In the supercritical regime <span>&#92;(c > 1&#92;)</span>, the giant component has order <span>&#92;(\rho n + o(n)&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(\rho = \rho(c)&#92;)</span> is the survival probability of a <span>&#92;(\mathrm{Po}(c)&#92;)</span> Galton–Watson branching process (the unique solution in <span>&#92;((0,1)&#92;)</span> of <span>&#92;(1 - \rho = e^{-c\rho}&#92;)</span>).

# Lecture 6: Bipartite Random Graphs and Perfect Matchings

*(Jan 20)*

## Bipartite Random Graphs

The **bipartite random graph** <span>&#92;(G(n,n,p)&#92;)</span> has two vertex classes of size <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span> each, with every cross-edge included independently with probability <span>&#92;(p&#92;)</span>.

**Exercise**: Let <span>&#92;(p = (\log n + c)/n&#92;)</span>. Let <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> count isolated vertices (vertices of degree 0). Then <span>&#92;(E[X] = 2n(1-p)^n \sim 2e^{-c}&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(P(G(n,n,p) \text{ has no isolated vertices}) \to e^{-2e^{-c}}&#92;)</span>.

A **cherry** is a path of length 2 (one vertex of degree 2, two of degree 1). Cherries can be analyzed similarly via the method of moments.

## Hall's Theorem and Perfect Matchings

A **perfect matching** in <span>&#92;(G(n,n,p)&#92;)</span> is a set of <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span> vertex-disjoint edges covering all vertices. **Hall's Theorem**: a bipartite graph with parts <span>&#92;(A&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(B&#92;)</span> has a perfect matching if and only if for every <span>&#92;(S \subseteq A&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(|N(S)| \geq |S|&#92;)</span>.

When Hall's condition fails, there is a set <span>&#92;(S \subseteq A&#92;)</span> such that <span>&#92;(|N(S)| < |S|&#92;)</span>. Analyzing such sets: if <span>&#92;(|S| = k&#92;)</span>, then the <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span> vertices of <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> must have all their neighbours in a set of size at most <span>&#92;(k-1&#92;)</span>. The proof of the perfect matching threshold uses three structural cases on the obstruction set.

**Key Lemma**: For <span>&#92;((3/4)\log n / n < p < 2\log n / n&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(\sum&#95;{k} E[X&#95;k] = o(1)&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(X&#95;k&#92;)</span> counts the bad sets of size <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>.

**Theorem**: <span>&#92;(P(G(n,n,p) \text{ has a perfect matching}) \to e^{-2e^{-c}}&#92;)</span> when <span>&#92;(p = (\log n + c)/n&#92;)</span>. The threshold coincides with the disappearance of isolated vertices, just as in the non-bipartite case.

## Tree Components in <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span>

Let <span>&#92;(T&#95;k&#92;)</span> denote a tree component of order <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span> in <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span). One can show:
<span>&#92;[ E[T&#95;k] \sim \frac{k^{k-2}}{k!}(ce^{-c})^k \cdot n &#92;]</span>
and the expected number of vertices in tree components is <span>&#92;(E[T(G(n,p))] = n \cdot t(c)&#92;)</span> where
<span>&#92;[ t(c) = \frac{1}{c}\sum&#95;{k \geq 1} \frac{k^{k-1}}{k!}(ce^{-c})^k. &#92;]</span>
This function <span>&#92;(t(c)&#92;)</span> connects to the survival probability: for <span>&#92;(c > 1&#92;)</span>, the fraction of vertices in finite components is <span>&#92;(1 - \rho(c)&#92;)</span>.

# Lecture 7: Galton–Watson Branching Processes and Chernoff Bounds

*(Feb 3)*

## Threshold Functions: Formal Definition

A threshold clarification from lecture: function <span>&#92;(p&#95;0(n)&#92;)</span> is a threshold for property <span>&#92;(Q&#92;)</span> if for all <span>&#92;(\varepsilon > 0&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \lim&#95;{n \to \infty} P(G(n,p) \in Q) = \begin{cases} 0 & p \leq (1-\varepsilon)p&#95;0(n) \\ 1 & p \geq (1+\varepsilon)p&#95;0(n). \end{cases} &#92;]</span>
Recall Theorem E(d): when <span>&#92;(p = c/n&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(c > 1&#92;)</span>, the fraction of vertices in the giant component is a.a.s <span>&#92;(\rho + o(1)&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(\rho = \rho(c)&#92;)</span>.

## Galton–Watson Branching Processes

A **Galton–Watson branching process** starts with one particle. Each particle independently produces offspring according to a law <span>&#92;(Z&#92;)</span> (here <span>&#92;(Z \sim \mathrm{Po}(c)&#92;)</span>). Let <span>&#92;(P&#95;Z&#92;)</span> be the **extinction probability**: the probability that the process eventually dies out.

The **probability generating function** (pgf) is <span>&#92;(G(x) = E[x^Z] = e^{c(x-1)}&#92;)</span> for <span>&#92;(Z \sim \mathrm{Po}(c)&#92;)</span>.

**Extinction Theorem**:
- (i) If <span>&#92;(E[Z] < 1&#92;)</span>, then <span>&#92;(P&#95;Z = 1&#92;)</span> (certain extinction).
- (ii) If <span>&#92;(E[Z] = 1&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(P(Z = 0) > 0&#92;)</span>, then <span>&#92;(P&#95;Z = 1&#92;)</span>.
- (iii) If <span>&#92;(E[Z] > 1&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(P(Z = 0) > 0&#92;)</span>, then <span>&#92;(0 < P_Z < 1&#92;)</span>.

*Proof by picture*: <span>&#92;(P&#95;Z&#92;)</span> is the smallest fixed point of <span>&#92;(G(x) = x&#92;)</span> in <span>&#92;([0,1]&#92;)</span>. The curve <span>&#92;(y = G(x)&#92;)</span> starts at <span>&#92;(G(0) = e^{-c} > 0&#92;)</span>, is convex, and has slope <span>&#92;(G'(1) = c&#92;)</span> at <span>&#92;(x = 1&#92;)</span>. For <span>&#92;(c > 1&#92;)</span>, the tangent at <span>&#92;(x=1&#92;)</span> has slope exceeding 1, so the curve crosses <span>&#92;(y = x&#92;)</span> at a unique interior point in <span>&#92;((0,1)&#92;)</span>.

The connection to random graphs: in <span>&#92;(G(n,c/n)&#92;)</span), exploring the component of a vertex looks locally like a <span>&#92;(\mathrm{Po}(c)&#92;)</span> Galton–Watson process. The survival probability <span>&#92;(\rho = 1 - P&#95;Z&#92;)</span> (the probability the tree survives forever) gives the fraction of vertices in the giant component.

## Chernoff Bounds

The Chernoff bound gives exponentially tight concentration for sums of independent indicator random variables, far stronger than Chebyshev.

**Theorem** (Chernoff Bound): Let <span>&#92;(X&#95;1, \ldots, X&#95;n&#92;)</span> be independent <span>&#92;(\{0,1\}&#92;)</span>-valued random variables, <span>&#92;(X = \sum X&#95;i&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(\mu = E[X]&#92;)</span>. Then:

**(a)** For any <span>&#92;(\delta > 0&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(X \geq (1+\delta)\mu\right) \leq \left(\frac{e^\delta}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^\mu. &#92;]</span>

**(b)** For <span>&#92;(0 < \delta \leq 1&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(X \geq (1+\delta)\mu\right) \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{\mu\delta^2}{3}\right). &#92;]</span>

**(c)** For <span>&#92;(0 < \delta \leq 1&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(X \leq (1-\delta)\mu\right) \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{\mu\delta^2}{2}\right). &#92;]</span>

*Proof*: Apply Markov's inequality to <span>&#92;(e^{tX}&#92;)</span> for <span>&#92;(t > 0&#92;)</span>: <span>&#92;(\Pr(X \geq (1+\delta)\mu) \leq e^{-t(1+\delta)\mu} E[e^{tX}]&#92;)</span>. Since the <span>&#92;(X&#95;i&#92;)</span> are independent, <span>&#92;(E[e^{tX}] = \prod E[e^{tX&#95;i}] \leq \exp(\mu(e^t - 1))&#92;)</span>. Optimize over <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span> (setting <span>&#92;(t = \log(1+\delta)&#92;)</span>) to get (a). Bounds (b) and (c) are simplified forms.

**Remark**: Chernoff bounds hold for any collection of <span>&#92;(\{0,1\}&#92;)</span>-valued random variables that are negatively correlated (not just independent). This generalization is useful when analyzing graph exploration.

# Lecture 8: Stochastic Dominance and Proof of Theorem E(d)

*(Feb 5)*

## Stochastic Dominance

We say <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> **stochastically dominates** <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span>, written <span>&#92;(X \succeq Y&#92;)</span>, if <span>&#92;(P(X \geq x) \geq P(Y \geq x)&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(x&#92;)</span>.

**Coupling Lemma**: <span>&#92;(X \succeq Y&#92;)</span> if and only if there exists a coupling <span>&#92;((\hat{X}, \hat{Y})&#92;)</span> such that <span>&#92;(\hat{X} \geq \hat{Y}&#92;)</span> always.

**Corollary**: For a monotone property <span>&#92;(Q&#92;)</span>, if <span>&#92;(X \succeq Y&#92;)</span> then <span>&#92;(P(X \in Q) \geq P(Y \in Q)&#92;)</span>.

This is applied as follows: the number of new vertices discovered when exploring a component from a vertex is stochastically dominated by a <span>&#92;(\mathrm{Bin}(n, p)&#92;)</span> random variable (the actual distribution is <span>&#92;(\mathrm{Bin}(n - (\text{visited}), p)&#92;)</span>, which is smaller). This connects graph exploration to branching processes.

## Proof of Theorem E(d)

We prove that for <span>&#92;(p = c/n&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(c > 1&#92;)</span), a.a.s there is exactly one linear-order component and all others have order <span>&#92;(O(\log n)&#92;)</span).

Set <span>&#92;(k&#95;0 = c \log n&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(k&#95;1 = n^{2/3}&#92;)</span>. We prove three things:

**(i) No components of order in <span>&#92;((k&#95;0, k&#95;1)&#92;)</span>**: Run the **graph exploration process** starting from an arbitrary vertex <span>&#92;(v&#92;)</span>. Let <span>&#92;(S&#95;t&#92;)</span> be the active frontier (vertices reached but whose neighbours haven't been fully explored), <span>&#92;(U&#95;t&#92;)</span> the visited set. Define <span>&#92;(Z&#95;t = X&#95;t - X&#95;{t-1} + 1&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(X&#95;t = |S&#95;t| + |U&#95;t|&#92;)</span> is the total explored count. Then <span>&#92;(Z&#95;{t+1} \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n - (X&#95;t + t), p)&#92;)</span>. Using the Chernoff bound and union bound over all starting times <span>&#92;(\tau \in (k&#95;0, k&#95;1)&#92;)</span>, one shows a.a.s no exploration path ever reaches size in <span>&#92;((k&#95;0, k&#95;1)&#92;)</span>.

**(ii) At most one component of order <span>&#92;(\geq k&#95;1&#92;)</span>**: For any two vertices <span>&#92;(u, v&#92;)</span>, the probability that they lie in different components each of order <span>&#92;(> k&#95;1&#92;)</span> is <span>&#92;(o(n^{-2})&#92;)</span> by a second moment argument; a union bound over all <span>&#92;(\binom{n}{2}&#92;)</span> pairs finishes.

**(iii) The fraction of vertices in small components converges to <span>&#92;(1 - \rho&#92;)</span>**: Let <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span> count vertices in components of order <span>&#92;(\leq k_0&#92;)</span). A **slowed-down branching process** argument shows <span>&#92;(E[Y] = (1 + o(1))\rho \cdot n&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(\rho = P&#95;Z&#92;)</span> is the extinction probability of the <span>&#92;(\mathrm{Po}(c)&#92;)</span> branching process.

# Lecture 9: Tree Components and Hamiltonian Cycles

*(Feb 10)*

## Completing Theorem E(d): Part (iii)

To bound <span>&#92;(E[Y]&#92;)</span> from below, we use a slowed-down branching process <span>&#92;(\mathrm{Bin}(n, (1+\varepsilon)c/n)&#92;)</span> and show the survival probability <span>&#92;(P&#95;{k&#95;0} \geq P - \varepsilon&#92;)</span> for large enough <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span>. This gives <span>&#92;(E[Y] = (1+o(1))\rho \cdot n&#92;)</span>, completing the proof of Theorem E(d).

## Hamiltonian Cycles: The Threshold

A **Hamiltonian cycle** is a cycle visiting every vertex exactly once. This is the most demanding connectivity structure, corresponding to part (g) of Theorem E.

**Theorem**: Let <span>&#92;(p = (\log n + \log\log n + \omega(n))/n&#92;)</span>.
- **(a)** If <span>&#92;(\omega(n) \to +\infty&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> is Hamiltonian.
- **(b)** If <span>&#92;(\omega(n) \to -\infty&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> is not Hamiltonian.

The threshold coincides exactly with the point where minimum degree becomes 2, illustrating a deep connection between local and global structure.

**Lemma** (exercise):
- **(a)** If <span>&#92;(pn - (\log n + \log\log n) \to -\infty&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p) \notin \mathcal{D}&#95;2&#92;)</span> (some vertex has degree <span>&#92;(< 2&#92;)</span>).
- **(b)** If <span>&#92;(\to +\infty&#92;)</span>, then a.a.s <span>&#92;(G(n,p) \in \mathcal{D}&#95;2&#92;)</span>.

## Posá Rotations

**Posá's rotation technique** is the engine of the Hamiltonicity proof. Given a **longest path** <span>&#92;(P = v&#95;0 v&#95;1 \cdots v&#95;k&#92;)</span> in the graph, suppose the current endpoint <span>&#92;(v&#95;k&#92;)</span> is adjacent to some interior vertex <span>&#92;(v&#95;i&#92;)</span>. Then delete the edge <span>&#92;(v&#95;i v&#95;{i+1}&#92;)</span> and add the edge <span>&#92;(v&#95;k v&#95;i&#92;)</span>; this produces another path of the same length, now ending at <span>&#92;(v&#95;{i+1}&#92;)</span>. This operation is a **Posá rotation** with pivot <span>&#92;(v&#95;i&#92;)</span>.

Define <span>&#92;(\mathrm{End}(v&#95;0)&#92;)</span> as the set of all vertices that can appear as an endpoint of a path obtainable from <span>&#92;(P&#92;)</span> by a sequence of Posá rotations (keeping <span>&#92;(v&#95;0&#92;)</span> fixed).

**Lemma**: <span>&#92;(|N(\mathrm{End}(v_0))| < 2|\mathrm{End}(v_0)|&#92;)</span> would lead to a contradiction with the maximality of <span>&#92;(P&#92;)</span>. *Proof*: If <span>&#92;(v&#95;i \in N(\mathrm{End}(v&#95;0))&#92;)</span>, then at least one of <span>&#92;(v&#95;{i-1}&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(v&#95;{i+1}&#92;)</span> must lie in <span>&#92;(\mathrm{End}(v_0)&#92;)</span) (since a rotation at <span>&#92;(v&#95;i&#92;)</span> moves the endpoint to one of these).

# Lecture 10: The 2-Expander Lemma and Hamiltonicity

*(Feb 12)*

## The 2-Expander Lemma

The key structural property needed for Hamiltonicity is that the graph is a **2-expander**: every small set of vertices has a large neighbourhood.

**Lemma** (2-Expander): Assume <span>&#92;(pn - (\log n + \log\log n) \to +\infty&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(pn \leq 2\log n&#92;)</span>. Then a.a.s for all <span>&#92;(S \subseteq [n]&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(|S| \leq \varepsilon n&#92;)</span>: <span>&#92;(|N(S)| \geq 2|S|&#92;)</span>. Proof is deferred to Lecture 11.

## Boosters and the Hamiltonicity Proof

Define the set of **boosters** as <span>&#92;(\Xi = \{\{x,y\} : x \in \mathrm{End}(v&#95;0),\, y \in \mathrm{End}(x)\}&#92;)</span>. Adding any booster to the current path either creates a Hamiltonian cycle (if the endpoints are the same) or extends the set of reachable endpoints.

**Key step**: Under the 2-expander property, <span>&#92;(|\Xi| \geq \varepsilon&#95;0 n^2&#92;)</span> for some constant <span>&#92;(\varepsilon&#95;0 > 0&#92;)</span>.

**Sprinkling argument**: The Hamiltonicity proof uses a two-round exposure. First expose most of the edges (say with probability <span>&#92;(p - f&#95;0/8n&#92;)</span>) to get a graph with good expansion. Then "sprinkle" <span>&#92;(f&#95;0/8&#92;)</span> additional random edges as "trials". Each trial is a booster with probability <span>&#92;(\geq c\varepsilon&#95;0^2&#92;)</span>, so
<span>&#92;[ \Pr(G \notin \mathrm{HAM}) \leq \exp(-n \cdot \delta(\varepsilon&#95;0)) = o(1). &#92;]</span>

The structural properties (a), (b), (c) of the 2-expander needed for the proof involve bounds on how the neighbourhood of a set <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> can be partitioned into "light" and "heavy" vertices, depending on how many neighbours in <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> each has.

*(Feb 20)*

## Proof of the 2-Expander Lemma

We prove: a.a.s for all <span>&#92;(S \subseteq [n]&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(|S| \leq \varepsilon n&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(|N(S)| \geq 2|S|&#92;)</span>. Assume for contradiction that there is such an <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(|N(S)| < 2|S|&#92;)</span>.

Partition: let <span>&#92;(X = S&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(Y = N(S) \cap S&#92;)</span> (hmm, more precisely: partition <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> into part <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> with many external neighbours and part <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span> with few). Write <span>&#92;(Z&#95;0 = N(X) \cap Y&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(Z&#95;1 = N(X) \setminus Y&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(Z_2 = N(Y) \setminus (X \cup Z_1)&#92;)</span). Then <span>&#92;(|N(S)| = |Z&#95;1| + |Z&#95;2|&#92;)</span>.

**Case <span>&#92;(Y = \emptyset&#92;)</span>**: <span>&#92;(|N(S)| = |N(X)| \geq 2|X| = 2|S|&#92;)</span> by the minimum degree condition <span>&#92;(\mathcal{D}&#95;2&#92;)</span>. No contradiction.

**Case <span>&#92;(Y \neq \emptyset&#92;)</span>** (leads to contradiction):
- By <span>&#92;(\mathcal{D}&#95;2&#92;)</span>: <span>&#92;(|Z&#95;0| + |Z&#95;1| \geq 2|X|&#92;)</span>.
- The number of edges from <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> to <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span> satisfies: <span>&#92;(|Z&#95;0| = e(X,Y) \leq |Y|&#92;)</span>.
- Similarly: <span>&#92;(e(Y, Z&#95;1) \leq |Y|&#92;)</span>.

From these inequalities, <span>&#92;(|N(S)| = |Z&#95;1| + |Z&#95;2|&#92;)</span>, and a careful counting argument shows <span>&#92;(|Y| = 0&#92;)</span), contradiction. The constant 100 (or similar) in the proof is an arbitrary choice for the partition threshold — any sufficiently large constant works.

# Lecture 12: Martingales and Azuma–Hoeffding

*(Feb 26)*

## Conditional Expectation

The **conditional expectation** <span>&#92;(E[Y | X]&#92;)</span> is the unique (up to a.s. equality) random variable <span>&#92;(Z&#92;)</span> such that for every event <span>&#92;(A&#92;)</span> in the sigma-algebra generated by <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ \int&#95;A Z\, dP = \int&#95;A Y\, dP. &#92;]</span>
The **Tower Property** (iterated expectation) states: <span>&#92;(E[E[Y|X&#95;1, X&#95;2] | X&#95;1] = E[Y|X&#95;1]&#92;)</span>.

## Doob Martingales and Exposure Martingales

A sequence <span>&#92;((X&#95;t)&#95;{t \geq 0}&#92;)</span> is a **martingale** with respect to <span>&#92;((Y&#95;t)&#92;)</span> if <span>&#92;(E[X_t | Y_0, \ldots, Y_{t-1}] = X_{t-1}&#92;)</span) for all <span>&#92;(t \geq 1&#92;)</span>.

**Doob's martingale**: given any function <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> and a sequence of random variables <span>&#92;(Y&#95;1, \ldots, Y&#95;n&#92;)</span>, the sequence <span>&#92;(X&#95;t = E[f(Y&#95;1,\ldots,Y&#95;n) | Y&#95;1, \ldots, Y&#95;t]&#92;)</span> is a martingale. This is verified immediately by the Tower Property.

**Edge Exposure Martingale**: reveal the edges of <span>&#92;(K&#95;n&#92;)</span> one by one in some fixed order. Let <span>&#92;(Y&#95;i = \mathbf{1}[\text{the }i\text{-th edge is in }G(n,p)]&#92;)</span>. Then <span>&#92;(X&#95;t = E[f(G(n,p)) | Y&#95;1, \ldots, Y&#95;t]&#92;)</span> is a martingale for any graph function <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span>.

**Vertex Exposure Martingale**: reveal the vertices of <span>&#92;(G(n,p)&#92;)</span> one by one. Let <span>&#92;(G&#95;t&#92;)</span> be the subgraph induced by vertices <span>&#92;(\{1, \ldots, t\}&#92;)</span>. Then <span>&#92;(X&#95;t = E[f(G(n,p)) | G&#95;{[t]} = H&#95;{[t]}]&#92;)</span> is a martingale.

**Example**: For <span>&#92;(G(4, 1/4)&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> = chromatic number, the vertex exposure martingale starts at <span>&#92;(X&#95;0 = E[\chi(G(4,1/4))]&#92;)</span> and ends at <span>&#92;(X&#95;4 = \chi(G(4,1/4))&#92;)</span>. Each step satisfies <span>&#92;(E[X&#95;t | X&#95;0, \ldots, X&#95;{t-1}] = X&#95;{t-1}&#92;)</span> by the Tower Property.

## Azuma–Hoeffding Inequality

**Theorem** (Azuma–Hoeffding): Let <span>&#92;((X&#95;t)&#95;{t \geq 0}&#92;)</span> be a martingale with respect to <span>&#92;((Y&#95;t)&#92;)</span>, and suppose <span>&#92;(|X&#95;t - X&#95;{t-1}| \leq C&#95;t&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(t \geq 1&#92;)</span>. Then for all <span>&#92;(t > 0&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(|X&#95;n - X&#95;0| \geq t\right) \leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\sum&#95;{i=1}^n C&#95;i^2}\right). &#92;]</span>

The bound says that if a martingale has bounded increments, it concentrates tightly around its initial value. The key application: for the vertex exposure martingale of a 1-Lipschitz function <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> (one where changing one vertex changes <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> by at most 1), all <span>&#92;(C&#95;i = 1&#92;)</span>, so:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr(|f(G) - E[f(G)]| \geq t) \leq 2e^{-t^2/(2n)}. &#92;]</span>

# Lecture 13: Proof of Azuma–Hoeffding and Chromatic Number

*(Mar 2)*

## Proof of Azuma–Hoeffding

Let <span>&#92;(Z&#95;i = X&#95;i - X&#95;{i-1}&#92;)</span>, so <span>&#92;(X&#95;n = \sum Z&#95;i&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(-C&#95;i \leq Z&#95;i \leq C&#95;i&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(E[Z_i | Y_0, \ldots, Y_{i-1}] = 0&#92;)</span). For the upper tail, use the exponential moment method: for any <span>&#92;(\alpha > 0&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ \Pr(X&#95;n \geq t) \leq e^{-\alpha t} E[e^{\alpha X&#95;n}]. &#92;]</span>
For <span>&#92;(x \in [-c, c]&#92;)</span>, the inequality <span>&#92;(e^x \leq \frac{\sinh c}{c} x + \cosh c&#92;)</span> holds. This is applied iteratively: conditioned on <span>&#92;(Y&#95;0, \ldots, Y&#95;{i-1}&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ E[e^{\alpha Z&#95;i} | Y&#95;0,\ldots,Y&#95;{i-1}] \leq \cosh(\alpha C&#95;i) \leq e^{\alpha^2 C&#95;i^2 / 2}. &#92;]</span>
Induction gives <span>&#92;(E[e^{\alpha X_n}] \leq \exp\!\left(\alpha^2 \sum C_i^2 / 2\right)&#92;)</span). Setting <span>&#92;(\alpha = t / \sum C&#95;i^2&#92;)</span> optimally gives <span>&#92;(\Pr(X&#95;n \geq t) \leq \exp(-t^2 / (2\sum C&#95;i^2))&#92;)</span>. The lower tail follows by applying the same argument to <span>&#92;(-X&#95;n&#92;)</span>.

## Shamir–Spencer Theorem

**Theorem** (Shamir–Spencer, 1987): For any <span>&#92;(t > 0&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(|\chi(G(n,p)) - E[\chi(G(n,p))]| \geq t\right) \leq 2e^{-t^2/(2n)}. &#92;]</span>
*Proof*: use the vertex exposure martingale. Adding or removing one vertex changes the chromatic number by at most 1 (since an extra vertex needs at most one new colour), so the function is 1-Lipschitz. Azuma–Hoeffding gives the bound.

This theorem says the chromatic number is concentrated in an interval of width <span>&#92;(O(\sqrt{n})&#92;)</span> around its mean.

## Bollobás's Theorem: Chromatic Number of <span>&#92;(G(n,1/2)&#92;)</span>

**Theorem** (Bollobás, 1988): A.a.s
<span>&#92;[ \chi(G(n,1/2)) \sim \frac{n}{2\log&#95;2 n}. &#92;]</span>

**Lower bound**: Let <span>&#92;(k&#95;0 = \min\{k \geq 2 : f(k) \leq 1\}&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(f(k) = E[\text{# }k\text{-cliques}] = \binom{n}{k}(1/2)^{\binom{k}{2}}&#92;)</span>. One checks <span>&#92;(k&#95;0 \sim 2\log&#95;2 n&#92;)</span>. Since <span>&#92;(\omega(G) \leq k_0 - 1&#92;)</span) a.a.s (by Markov, since <span>&#92;(E[X&#95;{k&#95;0}] \leq 1&#92;)</span>), and since for the complementary graph <span>&#92;(\tilde{G} \sim G(n,1/2)&#92;)</span> we have <span>&#92;(\omega(\tilde{G}) = \alpha(G)&#92;)</span>, we get <span>&#92;(\alpha(G) \leq k&#95;0 - 1&#92;)</span> a.a.s. Since <span>&#92;(\chi(G) \cdot \alpha(G) \geq n&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ \chi(G(n,1/2)) \geq \frac{n}{\alpha(G)} \geq (1+o(1))\frac{n}{2\log&#95;2 n}\quad\text{a.a.s.} &#92;]</span>

The upper bound proof continues into Lecture 14.

# Lecture 14: Chromatic Number Upper Bound and Differential Equations Method

*(Mar 4)*

## Upper Bound for <span>&#92;(\chi(G(n,1/2))&#92;)</span>

Set <span>&#92;(k = k&#95;0 - 1&#92;)</span> (so <span>&#92;(f(k) = \omega(n^2)&#92;)</span) and let <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span> be the size of a maximum family of **edge-disjoint** <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>-cliques.

**Key Lemma**: <span>&#92;(E[Y] \geq (1+o(1))\dfrac{n^2}{2k^4}&#92;)</span>.

Using the edge exposure martingale on <span>&#92;(G(n,1/2)&#92;)</span) with <span>&#92;(Y&#95;0 = E[Y]&#92;)</span>, we observe that each edge belongs to at most one clique in the maximal edge-disjoint family, so <span>&#92;(|Y_m - Y_{m-1}| \leq 1&#92;)</span). By Azuma–Hoeffding (with <span>&#92;(t = n^2/(8k^4)&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(\binom{n}{2}&#92;)</span> steps),
<span>&#92;[ \Pr(\omega(G(n,1/2)) < k) = \Pr(Y = 0) \leq 2\exp\!\left(-\Omega\!\left(\frac{n^2}{\log^4 n}\right)\right). &#92;]</span>

**Proof of Key Lemma**: Let <span>&#92;(\mathcal{C}&#92;)</span> be a random family of <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>-cliques obtained by including each <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>-clique of <span>&#92;(G(n,1/2)&#92;)</span> independently with probability <span>&#92;(p&#92;)</span>. Let <span>&#92;(W&#92;)</span> count pairs <span>&#92;((\alpha, \beta)&#92;)</span> in <span>&#92;(\mathcal{C}&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(E(\alpha) \cap E(\beta) \neq \emptyset&#92;)</span>. Form <span>&#92;(\mathcal{C}'&#92;)</span> by deleting one clique from each conflicting pair; <span>&#92;(\mathcal{C}'&#92;)</span> is edge-disjoint, so:
<span>&#92;[ E[Y] \geq E[|\mathcal{C}'|] \geq E[|\mathcal{C}|] - E[|W|]. &#92;]</span>
Now <span>&#92;(E[|\mathcal{C}|] = p \cdot f(k)&#92;)</span>. By the exercise, the expected number of pairs of <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>-cliques sharing at least one edge is <span>&#92;(\sim f(k)^2 \cdot k^4 / (2n^2)&#92;)</span>, so <span>&#92;(E[|W|] \sim p^2 f(k)^2 k^4/(2n^2)&#92;)</span). Choosing <span>&#92;(p = n^2/(k^4 f(k))&#92;)</span> gives
<span>&#92;[ E[Y] \geq p \cdot f(k) - (1+o(1))p^2 \cdot \frac{f(k)^2 k^4}{2n^2} = (1+o(1))\frac{n^2}{2k^4}. \quad\square &#92;]</span>

**Completing the upper bound**: Set <span>&#92;(m = \lfloor\sqrt{n} \cdot \log^{1/2} n\rfloor&#92;)</span> (or similar small quantity). For any <span>&#92;(m&#92;)</span>-set <span>&#92;(S \subseteq [n]&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(G[S] \sim G(m, 1/2)&#92;)</span> and the same argument gives <span>&#92;(\Pr(\alpha(G[S]) < k) \leq \exp(-\Omega(n^2/\log^8 n))&#92;)</span>. Since there are fewer than <span>&#92;(2^n&#92;)</span> subsets:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(\exists \text{ }m\text{-set }S:\, \alpha(G[S]) < k\right) \leq 2^n \cdot \exp\!\left(-\Omega(n^2/\log^8 n)\right) = o(1). &#92;]</span>
**Coloring strategy**: Repeatedly extract an independent set of order <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span> (assigning it a new colour) until fewer than <span>&#92;(m&#92;)</span> vertices remain, then colour each remaining vertex with a distinct colour. This uses at most <span>&#92;(n/k + m \sim n/(2\log&#95;2 n)&#92;)</span> colours, giving <span>&#92;(\chi(G(n,1/2)) \leq (1+o(1))n/(2\log&#95;2 n)&#92;)</span> a.a.s. <span>&#92;(\square&#92;)</span>

## Chromatic Number Concentration

**Theorem**: Let <span>&#92;(p = n^{-\alpha}&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(\alpha > 0&#92;)</span> fixed. Then there exists an integer <span>&#92;(u = u(n,p)&#92;)</span> such that a.a.s <span>&#92;(u \leq \chi(G(n,p)) \leq u + 3&#92;)</span>.

*Proof*: Fix <span>&#92;(\varepsilon > 0&#92;)</span>. Let <span>&#92;(u&#92;)</span> be the smallest integer with <span>&#92;(\Pr(\chi(G(n,p)) \leq u) \geq \varepsilon&#92;)</span). Let <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span> be the size of the smallest set <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> such that <span>&#92;(G - S&#92;)</span> is <span>&#92;(u&#92;)</span>-colourable; then <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span> satisfies the 1-Lipschitz condition (removing one more vertex changes <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span> by at most 1). By Azuma's inequality:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr(Y > E[Y] + \lambda\sqrt{n}) \leq e^{-\lambda^2/2},\quad \Pr(Y < E[Y] - \lambda\sqrt{n}) \leq e^{-\lambda^2/2}. &#92;]</span>
Choose <span>&#92;(\lambda&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(e^{-\lambda^2/2} = \varepsilon&#92;)</span>. Since <span>&#92;(Y = 0 \Leftrightarrow \chi(G(n,p)) \leq u&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(\Pr(Y = 0) \geq \varepsilon&#92;)</span>, we get <span>&#92;(E[Y] \leq \lambda\sqrt{n}&#92;)</span). Then <span>&#92;(\Pr(Y > 2\lambda\sqrt{n}) < \varepsilon&#92;)</span>, so with probability <span>&#92;(\geq 1 - \varepsilon&#92;)</span>, there is a set <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> of size <span>&#92;(\leq 2\lambda\sqrt{n}&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(G - S&#92;)</span> being <span>&#92;(u&#92;)</span>-colourable. From the assignment, any set of size <span>&#92;(\leq 2\lambda\sqrt{n}&#92;)</span> is 3-colourable. Therefore <span>&#92;(\Pr(\chi \leq u-1 \text{ or } \chi \geq u+4) \leq 3\varepsilon&#92;)</span>, and letting <span>&#92;(\varepsilon \to 0&#92;)</span> finishes. <span>&#92;(\square&#92;)</span>

**What happens for <span>&#92;(p \sim c/n&#92;)</span>?** For fixed <span>&#92;(c > 0&#92;)</span>, the chromatic number threshold is more delicate.

**Theorem** (Achlioptas–Friedgut, 1999): For any fixed <span>&#92;(k \geq 3&#92;)</span>, there is a sharp threshold sequence <span>&#92;(c&#95;k = c&#95;k(n)&#92;)</span> such that for all <span>&#92;(\varepsilon > 0&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \Pr(G(n, c/n)\text{ is }k\text{-colourable}) = \begin{cases} 0 & c < (1-\varepsilon)c_k(n) \\ 1 & c > (1+\varepsilon)c_k(n). \end{cases} &#92;]</span>
Determining the exact value of <span>&#92;(c&#95;k&#92;)</span> remains an open problem.

## Introduction to the Differential Equations Method

The **Differential Equations Method** (Wormald 1995) is a powerful technique for tracking the evolution of graph processes, replacing the complex stochastic evolution by a deterministic ODE system.

**Toy example** (balls into bins): Fix <span>&#92;(c > 0&#92;)</span>. Throw <span>&#92;(cn&#92;)</span> balls one by one uniformly at random into <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span> bins. Let <span>&#92;(X&#95;i&#92;)</span> = number of empty bins after <span>&#92;(i&#92;)</span> balls have been thrown.

- <span>&#92;(X&#95;0 = n&#92;)</span>
- <span>&#92;(X&#95;{i+1} = X&#95;i - \xi&#95;{i+1}&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(\xi&#95;{i+1} = \mathbf{1}[(i+1)\text{-th ball hits empty bin}]&#92;)</span>
- <span>&#92;(\Pr(\xi&#95;{i+1} = 1) = X&#95;i/n&#92;)</span>, formally <span>&#92;(E[\xi&#95;{i+1} | X&#95;0,\ldots,X&#95;i] = X&#95;i/n&#92;)</span>
- Hence <span>&#92;(E[X&#95;{i+1} - X&#95;i | H&#95;i] = -X&#95;i/n&#92;)</span>

To find the **trajectory** of <span>&#92;((X&#95;i)&#92;)</span>, assume (heuristically) that <span>&#92;(X&#95;i \approx E[X&#95;i]&#92;)</span> and set <span>&#92;(x(t) = E[X&#95;{tn}]/n&#92;)</span>. Then:
<span>&#92;[ E[X&#95;{i+1}] - E[X&#95;i] = -\frac{E[X&#95;i]}{n} \implies n\!\left(x\!\left(t+\tfrac{1}{n}\right) - x(t)\right) = -x(t). &#92;]</span>
As <span>&#92;(n \to \infty&#92;)</span>, this becomes the ODE <span>&#92;(x'(t) = -x(t)&#92;)</span>. Solving: <span>&#92;(dx/dt = -x \implies x(t) = Ce^{-t}&#92;)</span>. Since <span>&#92;(X&#95;0 = n&#92;)</span>, we get <span>&#92;(x(0) = 1&#92;)</span> so <span>&#92;(C = 1&#92;)</span). Thus:
<span>&#92;[ E[X&#95;i] \approx ne^{-i/n}. &#92;]</span>

**Concentration**: Let <span>&#92;(Z&#95;j = E[X&#95;\tau | H&#95;j]&#92;)</span> where <span>&#92;(H&#95;j&#92;)</span> is the sigma-field generated by the first <span>&#92;(j&#92;)</span> ball placements. Then <span>&#92;(|Z&#95;j - Z&#95;{j-1}| \leq 1&#92;)</span> (one ball placement changes the count by at most 1). By Azuma's Inequality,
<span>&#92;[ \Pr(|X&#95;\tau - E[X&#95;\tau]| \geq 2\sqrt{\alpha\tau}) \leq 2e^{-\alpha/2}. &#92;]</span>
Thus a.a.s with probability <span>&#92;(\geq 1 - e^{-n^{1/2}}&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(X&#95;\tau = ne^{-\tau/n} + o(n)&#92;)</span>.

## Formal Setup

A **discrete-time random process** is a probability space <span>&#92;((Q&#95;0, Q&#95;1, Q&#95;2, \ldots)&#92;)</span> where each <span>&#92;(Q&#95;i&#92;)</span> takes values in some set <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span>. Elements of the space are sequences <span>&#92;((q&#95;0, q&#95;1, q&#95;2, \ldots)&#92;)</span>. We write <span>&#92;(h&#95;t = (q&#95;0, q&#95;1, \ldots, q&#95;t)&#92;)</span> for the **history** up to step <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span>, and <span>&#92;(H&#95;t&#92;)</span> for its random counterpart.

We study a **sequence of processes** indexed by <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span>, with <span>&#92;(q&#95;i = q&#95;i^{(n)}&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(S = S^{(n)}&#92;)</span>. Asymptotics refer to <span>&#92;(n \to \infty&#92;)</span>. Let <span>&#92;(S^{\mathrm{cnt}}&#92;)</span> denote the set of all histories <span>&#92;(h&#95;t = (q&#95;0, \ldots, q&#95;t)&#92;)</span> where each <span>&#92;(q&#95;i \in S^{(n)}&#92;)</span>.

A function <span>&#92;(f(u&#95;1, \ldots, u&#95;j)&#92;)</span> satisfies a **Lipschitz condition** on <span>&#92;(D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^j&#92;)</span> if there exists a constant <span>&#92;(L&#92;)</span> such that
<span>&#92;[ |f(u&#95;1,\ldots,u&#95;j) - f(v&#95;1,\ldots,v&#95;j)| \leq L \cdot \max&#95;{1 \leq i \leq j} |u&#95;i - v&#95;i|. &#92;]</span>

**Stopping time**: given a connected open set <span>&#92;(D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^a&#92;)</span>, define
<span>&#92;[ T&#95;D(Y&#95;1,\ldots,Y&#95;a) = \min\!\left\{t \geq 0 : \left(\frac{t}{n}, \frac{Y&#95;1(t)}{n}, \ldots, \frac{Y&#95;a(t)}{n}\right) \notin D\right\}. &#92;]</span>

## Theorem: Wormald's Differential Equations Method

**Theorem** (Wormald's DE Method): Fix <span>&#92;(a&#92;)</span> and let <span>&#92;(Y&#95;1, \ldots, Y&#95;a&#92;)</span> be random variables depending on the process, with functions <span>&#92;(\hat{y}&#95;l : S^{\mathrm{cnt}} \to \mathbb{R}&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(f&#95;l : \mathbb{R}^{a+1} \to \mathbb{R}&#92;)</span> for <span>&#92;(1 \leq l \leq a&#92;)</span>. Suppose <span>&#92;(|Y&#95;l(h&#95;t)| \leq Cn&#92;)</span> for all histories <span>&#92;(h&#95;t \in S^{\mathrm{cnt}}&#92;)</span> and all <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span>.

Let <span>&#92;(D&#92;)</span> be a bounded, connected, open set containing the closure of
<span>&#92;[ \mathcal{E} = \{(0, z&#95;1, \ldots, z&#95;a) : \Pr(Y&#95;l(0) = z&#95;l n,\, 1 \leq l \leq a) \neq 0 \text{ for some }n\}. &#92;]</span>

Assume the following conditions hold for <span>&#92;(t < T_D&#92;)</span>:

**(i) Boundedness Hypothesis**: For some fixed <span>&#92;(C > 0&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ \max&#95;{1 \leq l \leq a} |Y&#95;l(t+1) - Y&#95;l(t)| \leq C. &#92;]</span>

**(ii) Trend Hypothesis**: For all <span>&#92;(1 \leq l \leq a&#92;)</span>,
<span>&#92;[ \left|E[Y&#95;l(t+1) - Y&#95;l(t) | H&#95;t] - f&#95;l\!\left(\frac{t}{n}, \frac{Y&#95;1(t)}{n}, \ldots, \frac{Y&#95;a(t)}{n}\right)\right| = o(1). &#92;]</span>

**(iii) Approximate Lipschitz Hypothesis**: Each function <span>&#92;(f&#95;l&#92;)</span> is continuous and satisfies a Lipschitz condition on <span>&#92;(D \cap \{(t, z&#95;1, \ldots, z&#95;a) : t \geq 0\}&#92;)</span>.

**Conclusions**:

**(a)** For any <span>&#92;((0, \tilde{z}&#95;1, \ldots, \tilde{z}&#95;a) \in D&#92;)</span>, the system of ODEs
<span>&#92;[ \frac{dz&#95;l}{dt} = f&#95;l(t, z&#95;1, \ldots, z&#95;a),\quad l = 1, \ldots, a &#92;]</span>
has a unique solution in <span>&#92;(D&#92;)</span> passing through <span>&#92;(z&#95;l(0) = \tilde{z}&#95;l&#92;)</span> for <span>&#92;(1 \leq l \leq a&#92;)</span>, which extends to points arbitrarily close to the boundary of <span>&#92;(D&#92;)</span>.

**(b)** For every <span>&#92;(\varepsilon > 0&#92;)</span>, a.a.s
<span>&#92;[ Y&#95;l(t) = n \cdot z&#95;l(t/n) + o(n) &#92;]</span>
uniformly for <span>&#92;(0 \leq t \leq \sigma n&#92;)</span> and all <span>&#92;(1 \leq l \leq a&#92;)</span>, where <span>&#92;(z&#95;l(x)&#92;)</span> is the solution from (a) with <span>&#92;(\tilde{z}&#95;l = Y&#95;l(0)/n&#92;)</span>, and <span>&#92;(\sigma = \sigma(n, \varepsilon)&#92;)</span> is the supremum of <span>&#92;(x&#92;)</span> to which the solution can be extended while remaining within <span>&#92;\ell^\infty&#92;)</span>-distance <span>&#92;(\varepsilon&#92;)</span> of the boundary of <span>&#92;(D&#92;)</span>.

## Back to the Toy Example

Applying the DE Method to the balls-into-bins example:
- Define <span>&#92;(f(x, z) = -z&#92;)</span>
- **(b)**: <span>&#92;(0 \leq X(t) \leq n&#92;)</span> for <span>&#92;(0 \leq t \leq cn&#92;)</span>
- **(c)**: <span>&#92;(X(0) = n&#92;)</span>
- **(d)**: <span>&#92;(|X(t+1) - X(t)| \leq 1&#92;)</span>

These verify all three hypotheses with <span>&#92;(D = \{(x, z) : -\infty < x, z < \infty\}&#92;)</span> appropriately chosen. The conclusion is that <span>&#92;(dz/dt = -z&#92;)</span> has unique solution <span>&#92;(z(x) = e^{-x}&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(z(0) = 1&#92;)</span>. By part (b), a.a.s <span>&#92;(X(i) = ne^{-i/n} + o(n)&#92;)</span> uniformly over all <span>&#92;(0 \leq i \leq \sigma cn&#92;)</span>.

## The Min-Degree Graph Process

The **min-degree graph process** builds a graph on vertex set <span>&#92;([n]&#92;)</span> incrementally: at each step, choose a vertex <span>&#92;(u&#92;)</span> of minimum degree, then choose a vertex <span>&#92;(v&#92;)</span> not yet adjacent to <span>&#92;(u&#92;)</span> uniformly at random, and add the edge <span>&#92;(uv&#92;)</span>. Partition the process into **phases**: for <span>&#92;(k \geq 0&#92;)</span>, **phase <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>** consists of all steps where the minimum-degree vertex has degree <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>.

Let <span>&#92;(G&#95;t&#92;)</span> be the graph after step <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(Y&#95;i(t)&#92;)</span> the number of vertices of degree <span>&#92;(i&#92;)</span> in <span>&#92;(G&#95;t&#92;)</span>, and <span>&#92;(H&#95;t = (G&#95;0, G&#95;1, \ldots, G&#95;t)&#92;)</span>.

## Applying the DE Method to Phase 0

During phase 0, the chosen vertex <span>&#92;(u&#92;)</span> has degree 0 and <span>&#92;(v&#92;)</span> is a uniform random vertex from the remaining <span>&#92;(n-1&#92;)</span>. Let <span>&#92;(X&#95;i(t+1) = \mathbf{1}[d(v&#95;{t+1}) = i]&#92;)</span> (where <span>&#92;(v&#95;{t+1}&#92;)</span> is the chosen <span>&#92;(v&#92;)</span> at step <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span>). Then:
<span>&#92;[\begin{aligned} Y&#95;0(t+1) &= Y&#95;0(t) - 1 - X&#95;0(t+1) \\ Y&#95;1(t+1) &= Y&#95;1(t) + 1 + X&#95;0(t+1) - X&#95;1(t+1) \\ Y&#95;i(t+1) &= Y&#95;i(t) + X&#95;{i-1}(t+1) - X&#95;i(t+1) \quad\text{for }i \geq 2. \end{aligned}&#92;]</span>
Setting <span>&#92;(X&#95;{-1}(t) = 0&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span>, this unifies to:
<span>&#92;[ Y&#95;i(t+1) = Y&#95;i(t) - \delta&#95;{i0} + \delta&#95;{i1} + X&#95;{i-1}(t+1) - X&#95;i(t+1). &#92;]</span>
Since <span>&#92;(E[X&#95;i(t+1) | H&#95;t] = Y&#95;i(t)/(n-1)&#92;)</span> (vertex <span>&#92;(v&#92;)</span> is uniform among <span>&#92;(n-1&#92;)</span> remaining vertices),
<span>&#92;[ E[Y&#95;i(t+1) - Y&#95;i(t) | H&#95;t] = -\delta&#95;{i0} + \delta&#95;{i1} + \frac{Y&#95;{i-1}(t) - Y&#95;i(t)}{n-1} = -\delta&#95;{i0} + \delta&#95;{i1} + \frac{Y&#95;{i-1}(t) - Y&#95;i(t)}{n} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right). &#92;]</span>

## Solving the ODE System

Setting <span>&#92;(Y&#95;{-1}(t) = 0&#92;)</span>, define the trend functions:
<span>&#92;[ z&#95;i' = -\delta&#95;{i0} + \delta&#95;{i1} + z&#95;{i-1} - z&#95;i, \quad i \geq 0 &#92;]</span>
with initial conditions <span>&#92;(z&#95;0(0) = 1&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(z&#95;i(0) = 0&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(i \geq 1&#92;)</span>.

**For <span>&#92;(i = 0&#92;)</span>**: <span>&#92;(z&#95;0' = -1 - z&#95;0&#92;)</span>. Solving (integrating factor <span>&#92;(e^x&#92;)</span>):
<span>&#92;[ z&#95;0(x) = 2e^{-x} - 1. &#92;]</span>

**For <span>&#92;(i = 1&#92;)</span>**: <span>&#92;(z&#95;1' = 1 + (2e^{-x} - 1) - z&#95;1 = 2e^{-x} - z&#95;1&#92;)</span>. The general solution via integrating factor:
<span>&#92;[ z&#95;1(x) = 2xe^{-x}. &#92;]</span>

**In general**:
<span>&#92;[ z&#95;i(x) = \frac{2x^i}{i!} e^{-x}. &#92;]</span>
(Proof by induction: substituting <span>&#92;(z&#95;i = 2x^i e^{-x}/i!&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(z&#95;{i-1} = 2x^{i-1}e^{-x}/(i-1)!&#92;)</span> into <span>&#92;(z&#95;i' = z&#95;{i-1} - z&#95;i&#92;)</span> for <span>&#92;(i \geq 2&#92;)</span> checks out.)

**Theorem**: Fix <span>&#92;(a \in \mathbb{Z}&#92;)</span> and suppose <span>&#92;(0 < c < d \cdot n^2&#92;)</span> is fixed. Then a.a.s:
<span>&#92;[ Y&#95;l(t) = n \cdot z&#95;l(t/n) + o(n) \quad\text{uniformly for all }0 \leq l \leq a,\; 0 \leq t \leq \sigma n. &#92;]</span>
*Proof*: By checking all hypotheses of the DE Theorem (Exercise). ∎

The sequence <span>&#92;(z&#95;i(x) = \frac{2x^i}{i!}e^{-x}&#92;)</span> is a Poisson distribution: <span>&#92;(\sum&#95;{i \geq 0} z&#95;i(x) = 2e^{-x}\sum&#95;{i \geq 0} x^i/i! = 2&#92;)</span>. Wait — noting that <span>&#92;(\sum&#95;i z&#95;i(x) = 2e^{-x} \cdot e^x = 2&#92;)</span>. This says the total "mass" is 2, which makes sense since each step adds an edge (touching two vertices and increasing both their degrees by 1).

## Proof of the DE Method Theorem (Sketch)

We prove the case <span>&#92;(a = 1&#92;)</span> (one random variable <span>&#92;(Y&#92;)</span>, one ODE).

**Step 1: Concentration of <span>&#92;(Y(t+\omega) - Y(t)&#92;)</span>**. Fix a "chunk size" <span>&#92;(\omega = \lambda n&#92;)</span> for small <span>&#92;(\lambda = o(1)&#92;)</span>. Assume <span>&#92;((t/n, Y(t)/n)&#92;)</span> is at <span>&#92;\ell^\infty&#92;)</span>-distance at least <span>&#92;(2\varepsilon&#92;)</span> from <span>&#92;(\partial D&#92;)</span>. For <span>&#92;(k = 1, \ldots, \omega&#92;)</span>:
- By hypothesis (i): <span>&#92;(Y(t+k) = Y(t) + O(C)&#92;)</span>, so <span>&#92;(((t+k)/n, Y(t+k)/n) \in D&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(k \leq \omega&#92;)</span).
- By hypotheses (ii) and (iii) together (using Lipschitz continuity of <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span>): <span>&#92;(E[Y(t+k+1) - Y(t+k) | H&#95;{t+k}] = f(t/n, Y(t)/n) + O(\varepsilon) + o(1)&#92;)</span>.

This shows <span>&#92;((Y(t+k) - Y(t) - k f(t/n, Y(t)/n) - k s(n))&#95;{k \geq 0}&#92;)</span> is a supermartingale for some <span>&#92;(s(n) = o(1)&#92;)</span>. Checking that the increments are bounded by a constant <span>&#92;(\tilde{C}&#92;)</span>, Azuma's Inequality gives:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(Y(t+\omega) - Y(t) - \omega f\!\left(\tfrac{t}{n}, \tfrac{Y(t)}{n}\right) \geq \omega s(n) + \tilde{C}\sqrt{2\omega\alpha} \;\Big|\; H&#95;t\right) \leq e^{-\alpha}. &#92;]</span>
The lower tail follows by the same argument with a submartingale, giving
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(\left|Y(t+\omega) - Y(t) - \omega f\!\left(\tfrac{t}{n}, \tfrac{Y(t)}{n}\right)\right| \geq \omega s(n) + \tilde{C}\sqrt{2\omega\alpha} \;\Big|\; H&#95;t\right) \leq 2e^{-\alpha}. &#92;]</span>

**Step 2: Comparison with the ODE solution**. Divide the interval <span>&#92;([0, \sigma n]&#92;)</span> into chunks <span>&#92;(k&#95;i = i\omega&#92;)</span> for <span>&#92;(i = 0, 1, \ldots, i&#95;0&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(i&#95;0 = \lfloor \sigma n/\omega \rfloor&#92;)</span>. We show by induction on <span>&#92;(i&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(|Y(k&#95;i) - z(k&#95;i/n) \cdot n| \geq B&#95;i\right) = O(i \cdot e^{-\alpha}) &#92;]</span>
where <span>&#92;(B&#95;i = B\omega \cdot \frac{x&#95;i}{\omega} \cdot \left(\left(1 + \frac{B&#95;i}{\omega}\right)^i - 1\right)&#92;)</span> is the accumulated error, <span>&#92;(B&#92;)</span> a constant, <span>&#92;(\lambda = o(1)&#92;)</span>.

**Decomposition**: Write
<span>&#92;[ |Y(k&#95;{i+1}) - z(k&#95;{i+1}/n) \cdot n| \leq |A&#95;1| + |A&#95;2| + |A&#95;3| + |A&#95;4| &#92;]</span>
where:
- <span>&#92;(A&#95;1 = Y(k&#95;i) - n \cdot z(k&#95;i/n)&#92;)</span>  ← inductive error
- <span>&#92;(A&#95;2 = Y(k&#95;{i+1}) - Y(k&#95;i) - \omega f(k&#95;i/n, Y(k&#95;i)/n)&#92;)</span>  ← stochastic fluctuation (bounded by Step 1)
- <span>&#92;(A&#95;3 = \omega z'(k&#95;i/n) + n z(k&#95;i/n) - n z(k&#95;{i+1}/n)&#92;)</span>  ← Taylor approximation error
- <span>&#92;(A&#95;4 = \omega f(k&#95;i/n, Y(k&#95;i)/n) - \omega z'(k&#95;i/n)&#92;)</span>  ← Lipschitz error

By induction: <span>&#92;(|A&#95;1| \leq B&#95;i&#92;)</span> with probability <span>&#92;(\geq 1 - O(i e^{-\alpha})&#92;)</span>. Step 1 bounds <span>&#92;(|A&#95;2|&#92;)</span>. Since <span>&#92;(z&#92;)</span> is the ODE solution and <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> is Lipschitz, Taylor expansion gives:
<span>&#92;[ \left|z\!\left(\frac{k&#95;{i+1}}{n}\right) - z\!\left(\frac{k&#95;i}{n}\right) - \frac{\omega}{n} z'\!\left(\frac{k&#95;i}{n}\right)\right| \leq C'' \cdot \frac{\omega^2}{n^2} &#92;]</span>
for some constant <span>&#92;(C'' > 0&#92;)</span>, bounding <span>&#92;(|A&#95;3|&#92;)</span>. The Lipschitz condition bounds <span>&#92;(|A_4|&#92;)</span) by the inductive error scaled by the Lipschitz constant. Combining all four bounds and choosing <span>&#92;(\alpha&#92;)</span> appropriately gives the inductive step.

**Conclusion**: After <span>&#92;(i&#95;0 = O(n/\omega)&#92;)</span> chunks with union bound,
<span>&#92;[ \Pr\!\left(\exists\, t \leq \sigma n: |Y(t) - n z(t/n)| \geq Bn^{1/2+\delta}\right) \to 0 &#92;]</span>
for any <span>&#92;(\delta > 0&#92;)</span>, establishing part (b) of the theorem. The full argument sets <span>&#92;(\omega = n^{2/3}&#92;)</span> (or similar) to balance the error terms. <span>&#92;(\square&#92;)</span>
