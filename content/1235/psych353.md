# Chapter 1: Accessibility and Social Knowledge

## Section 1.1: What Is Social Cognition?

Social cognition is the study of how people perceive, represent, interpret, and remember information about themselves and others. It is the intersection of cognitive psychology (how minds process information) and social psychology (how that processing is shaped by social context).

Key questions:
- How do we form impressions of other people and of ourselves?
- How does the social knowledge we've accumulated guide current perception?
- To what extent are social cognitive processes controllable?
- How do motivation and affect colour our thinking?

## Section 1.2: Mental Representation and Social Knowledge

Mental representations are the internal cognitive structures by which we store information about the social world. Key types:

- **Schemas**: structured knowledge about concepts, including typical features, relationships, and default values. Social schemas include **person schemas** (knowledge about specific individuals or types), **role schemas** (expectations about occupants of social roles), and **event schemas** (scripts for typical social situations).
- **Stereotypes**: schemas for social groups. They have cognitive functions (efficiency, organization) but also produce systematic biases (Chapter 3).
- **Exemplars**: memories of specific instances, which influence judgments about categories through similarity-based retrieval.

## Section 1.3: Accessibility

**Accessibility** refers to the ease or readiness with which a mental construct comes to mind. A construct is accessible when it is:
- **Chronically accessible**: habitually active due to frequent or recent use.
- **Temporarily accessible** (**primed**): activated by immediate context.

Accessible constructs are used to interpret ambiguous information. The **semantic priming paradigm**: prior exposure to a word or concept speeds recognition of related concepts, demonstrating spreading activation in memory.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Priming.</strong> A procedure in which exposure to a stimulus increases the likelihood that a related mental construct will be activated and used to interpret subsequent ambiguous stimuli. Priming can be <em>overt</em> (deliberately presented stimulus) or <em>subliminal</em> (below conscious awareness).
</div>

Carlston's (2013) chapter provides an overview of how social knowledge is structured and how it influences social inference, emphasizing that social cognition is neither purely bottom-up (driven by incoming information) nor purely top-down (driven by prior knowledge) but an interaction of both.

# Chapter 2: Automaticity, Control, and Shared Reality

## Section 2.1: Automatic vs. Controlled Processing

A central distinction in social cognition is between **automatic** and **controlled** cognitive processes:

| Automatic | Controlled |
|---|---|
| Fast, efficient | Slow, effortful |
| Unintentional | Intentional |
| Outside conscious awareness | Consciously available |
| Difficult to inhibit | Can be overridden |

The distinction is not binary but a set of dimensions (Bargh, 1994). A process can be automatic along some dimensions but not others (e.g., intentionally initiated but running to completion automatically).

**Goal-directed automaticity**: with sufficient practice, behaviours and cognitive processes that initially require controlled effort can become automatic. Social habits, well-practiced skills, and cultural stereotypes can all operate automatically.

## Section 2.2: Cesario et al. (2006) — Regulatory Fit and Motivation

Cesario, Plaks, and Higgins (2006) demonstrated that the style in which social information is presented — eagerly (focused on approach) vs. vigilantly (focused on avoidance) — affects not just evaluations but actual behaviour.

The concept of **regulatory fit** (Higgins, 2000): when the manner of pursuing a goal fits the person's chronic regulatory orientation (promotion or prevention), the pursuit feels right, and motivation increases.

**Promotion focus**: concerned with advancement, accomplishment, and gains. **Prevention focus**: concerned with safety, responsibility, and non-losses. These two systems produce different emotional signatures (enthusiasm/dejection vs. relaxation/agitation) and different processing styles (approach-based exploration vs. vigilant scanning).

## Section 2.3: Shared Reality

**Shared reality** (Echterhoff, Higgins & Levine, 2009): the experience of having in common with others a cognition (including attitudes, beliefs, perceptions) about the world. Communication creates and validates shared realities.

The **saying-is-believing effect**: when people communicate about a target person to an audience with a given attitude, their own memory of the target shifts to align with what they said — even when the message was tailored to the audience's bias. Shared reality formation is not merely an encoding artifact but reflects the fundamental human motive to connect with others through common understanding.

# Chapter 3: Perceiving Others

## Section 3.1: Person Perception

Impression formation is rapid, influential, and often inaccurate. Asch's (1946) **configural model**: some traits are **central** (like "warm" vs. "cold") and change the meaning of other traits in a list, rather than simply adding their individual values.

Thin-slices research (Ambady & Rosenthal): brief exposures (as short as 5 seconds) to a person's behaviour predict outcomes like teaching evaluations nearly as well as 15-minute exposures. First impressions form quickly and are difficult to revise.

## Section 3.2: Berk & Andersen (2000) — Transference

**Transference** is a psychodynamic concept now studied empirically by Andersen and colleagues. When a new person resembles a significant other (in features, personality, or role), representations of the significant other are activated and applied to the new person.

Berk and Andersen (2000) showed that people form affective responses to new individuals (liking/disliking) based on the extent to which those individuals resemble significant others — even when the resemblance is limited to a few listed features. This occurs automatically and outside awareness.

Transference provides a mechanism for the persistence of interpersonal patterns across relationships and contexts.

# Chapter 4: Motivated Perception and Construal Level Theory

## Section 4.1: Motivated Perception

People do not perceive the world objectively; they see what they want or need to see. **Motivated perception** is the influence of motivations, desires, and goals on what is perceived, encoded, and interpreted.

**Balcetis and Dunning (2006)** demonstrated that desirability shapes perception: participants were more likely to interpret an ambiguous figure (something between the letter B and the number 13) as a letter (B) when the letter would provide a desirable outcome (a juice drink) and as a number (13) when the number would provide a desirable outcome. Motivation literally changes what is seen, not merely how it is interpreted.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Wishful seeing.</strong> The tendency to perceive ambiguous visual stimuli in the way that is consistent with what one hopes to see (Balcetis & Dunning, 2006). Extends the concept of wishful thinking into the perceptual domain.
</div>

## Section 4.2: Construal Level Theory

**Construal Level Theory (CLT)** (Trope & Liberman, 2010) proposes that psychological distance along any of several dimensions (temporal, spatial, social, hypothetical) affects the **level of abstraction** (construal level) used to represent objects and events.

- **Distal events** are represented in **high-level construals**: abstract, essential, decontextualized features. ("Working out" = "staying healthy.")
- **Proximal events** are represented in **low-level construals**: concrete, incidental, contextualized features. ("Working out" = "going to the gym at 7am and lifting weights.")

Rim, Hansen, and Trope (2013) extended CLT to examine how mental contrasting (imagining a desired future and then the obstacle) interacts with psychological distance.

**Practical implication**: when planning future actions, people tend to use high-level construals (why am I doing this?) but underestimate the concrete obstacles (how will I actually do this?). The **planning fallacy** — systematic underestimation of completion time — partially reflects this construal-level mismatch.

# Chapter 5: Heuristics, Hypothesis Testing, and Affective Forecasting

## Section 5.1: Kahneman's System 1 and System 2

Kahneman's (2011) dual-process framework distinguishes:

- **System 1** (automatic, fast, associative): generates quick intuitive judgments and first impressions. Susceptible to predictable errors.
- **System 2** (slow, deliberate, effortful): monitors System 1 outputs and can override them, but requires cognitive resources and motivation.

Three classic heuristics from the Kahneman/Tversky program:

1. **Availability**: frequency/probability judged by ease of retrieval. Dramatic events (plane crashes) seem more frequent than mundane ones (car accidents). Fluency and retrieval ease serve as evidence for frequency and truth.

2. **Representativeness**: membership judged by resemblance to prototype. Leads to **base rate neglect** and the **conjunction fallacy**.

3. **Anchoring and adjustment**: estimates start from an initial value (anchor) and adjust insufficiently. The anchor may be arbitrary or externally provided.

## Section 5.2: Hypothesis Testing and Confirmation Bias

People test hypotheses confirmatorily: they seek, prefer, and recall evidence consistent with their current hypothesis.

**Snyder and Swann (1978)**: when asked to determine if a target is an introvert, participants ask questions that would confirm introversion ("What factors make it hard for you to really open up to people?"), not questions that would disconfirm it.

**Confirmation bias** is not simple ignoring of evidence — people do update on disconfirming evidence, but less than on confirming evidence, and they generate more counterarguments to disconfirming evidence.

## Section 5.3: Affective Forecasting

**Affective forecasting** (Wilson & Gilbert, 2005) is the process of predicting how we will feel in response to future events.

People systematically **overestimate** the duration and intensity of their emotional responses to future events — positive and negative — a phenomenon called **impact bias**. They forget:

- **Focal bias (focalism)**: when predicting, people focus on the event and neglect other factors that will occupy attention and moderate the emotional response.
- **Psychological immune system**: people have more capacity to rationalize, reframe, and adapt to negative events than they expect. **Immune neglect**: people fail to predict how well their psychological immune system will work.

Implications: people make choices based on affective forecasts that systematically overweight the emotional impact of specific outcomes, leading to suboptimal decisions.

# Chapter 6: Memory and Social Cognition

## Section 6.1: Reconstructive Memory

Memory is not a recording but a reconstruction. Bartlett (1932): memory for stories is shaped by schemas — people remember schema-consistent information better, transform schema-inconsistent information toward consistency, and fill gaps with schema-derived inferences.

Applied to social cognition: memory for people and events is shaped by current schemas and goals. We remember people as more consistent with our current impression of them than they actually were.

## Section 6.2: Ross and Wilson (2003) — Temporal Self-Appraisal

Ross and Wilson (2003) examined how people evaluate their past selves relative to their present selves. The **temporal self-appraisal theory** proposes that people strategically distance themselves from unflattering past selves and maintain proximity to flattering past selves, in service of current self-esteem.

People perceive themselves as having improved over time in most domains, even when objective measures show no change. This **memory for improvement** serves self-enhancement: "I used to be worse, but I've grown."

## Section 6.3: Eyewitness Memory

Hellmann and Memon (2016) address the reliability of eyewitness memory in legal contexts. Key findings:
- **Post-event information** (misleading suggestions) can alter memories of witnessed events (Loftus misinformation effect).
- **Leading questions** during police interviews alter witness reports.
- **CI (Cognitive Interview)**: interview techniques based on memory principles (mental reinstatement of context, report everything, change perspective, change temporal order) improve accuracy of witness accounts.

# Chapter 7: Counterfactuals and Self-Identity

## Section 7.1: Counterfactual Thinking

**Counterfactual thoughts** are mental simulations of alternative outcomes ("if only..."). They are triggered by negative outcomes, near misses, and exceptional events.

Functions of counterfactual thinking:
- **Affect regulation**: upward counterfactuals ("things could have been better") produce regret; downward counterfactuals ("things could have been worse") produce relief.
- **Causal reasoning**: counterfactuals identify causal factors by varying specific elements.
- **Behavioral preparation**: regret motivates correction; "I should have left earlier" leads to earlier departures.

**Seto et al. (2015)** examined how counterfactuals contribute to self-understanding and identity by exploring the role of "might have been" selves in shaping current self-concept.

## Section 7.2: Self-Identity and the Self-Concept

Brown, Bailey, Stoll, and McConnel (2016) examined how people manage self-identity following identity-relevant events. The **self-concept** is not static but dynamically maintained through ongoing cognitive and social processes:

- **Self-affirmation theory** (Steele, 1988): when one aspect of self is threatened, people can restore self-integrity by affirming unrelated self-values.
- **Temporal self-comparison**: people compare their current self to their past selves; improvements enhance self-esteem.
- **Possible selves** (Markus & Nurius, 1986): representations of what one might become (hoped-for selves) and feared selves. They serve motivational functions by directing behaviour toward hoped-for selves and away from feared selves.

# Chapter 8: Culture, Cognitive Styles, and Wisdom

## Section 8.1: Cultural Variation in Cognitive Style

**Nisbett et al.** (2001) documented systematic differences in thinking style between East Asian and Western samples:

- **Analytic (Western)** style: focus on individual objects, categorize by rules and necessary features, use formal logic.
- **Holistic (East Asian)** style: attend to the field and relationships among objects, categorize by similarity and family resemblance, use dialectical rather than formal reasoning.

These differences are attributed to ecological and sociological factors: farming societies (more interdependent) develop holistic thinking; hunting/pastoral societies (more independent) develop analytic thinking.

Savani, Stephens, and Markus (2017) examined how "choice" is culturally constructed — showing that the Western conception of choice as individual autonomous decision is itself a cultural artifact.

## Section 8.2: Wisdom and the Third-Person Effect (Grossman & Kross, 2014)

**Grossman and Kross (2014)** demonstrated that **self-distancing** — reasoning about one's own problems from a third-person perspective ("Why does he/she feel that way?") rather than a first-person perspective — improves wise reasoning.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Solomon's Paradox.</strong> People reason more wisely about other people's problems than about their own — they show greater intellectual humility, perspective-taking, and recognition of uncertainty. Grossman and Kross named this phenomenon after the biblical King Solomon, renowned for wisdom toward others but foolish in his own life.
</div>

Self-distancing reduces ego-involvement and emotional arousal, allowing more balanced assessment of situations from multiple perspectives. This has practical implications for how people should approach their own decision-making.

# Chapter 9: Regulatory Focus and Implicit Theories

## Section 9.1: Regulatory Focus Theory

**Regulatory focus theory** (Higgins, 1997) proposes two distinct self-regulatory systems:

<div class="definition">
<strong>Promotion focus.</strong> A self-regulatory orientation toward advancement, accomplishment, and the presence vs. absence of positive outcomes. Associated with approach motivation, eagerness, and sensitivity to gains.
</div>

<div class="definition">
<strong>Prevention focus.</strong> A self-regulatory orientation toward security, safety, and the presence vs. absence of negative outcomes. Associated with avoidance motivation, vigilance, and sensitivity to losses.
</div>

Scholer and Higgins (2012) review how regulatory focus shapes not just the goals people pursue but the strategies they use and the emotions they experience in pursuit of goals. Regulatory focus is both a chronic individual difference and a state that can be induced situationally.

Key predictions:
- Promotion-focused individuals perform better on tasks requiring generation and creativity; prevention-focused on tasks requiring careful accuracy.
- Different emotions signal success and failure: **cheerfulness** (vs. dejection) for promotion; **quiescence/calm** (vs. anxiety/agitation) for prevention.
- Regulatory fit amplifies motivation regardless of focus: doing things in a manner consistent with one's focus increases engagement.

## Section 9.2: Implicit Theories of Intelligence and Ability

**Implicit theories** (Dweck, 1999) are lay beliefs about whether personal attributes are fixed or malleable:

- **Entity theory** (fixed mindset): intelligence/ability is a fixed trait; performance reflects the underlying level.
- **Incremental theory** (growth mindset): intelligence/ability is malleable and can be developed through effort.

Molden and Dweck (2006) review how these implicit theories shape goals, attributions, and responses to setbacks:

| Domain | Entity theorists | Incremental theorists |
|---|---|---|
| Goals | Performance goals (prove ability) | Learning goals (develop ability) |
| Attribution of failure | Lack of ability (stable, uncontrollable) | Insufficient effort or strategy (controllable) |
| Response to setback | Helpless, withdraw | Mastery-oriented, persist |
| Challenge-seeking | Avoid challenges (risk exposing low ability) | Seek challenges (opportunity to grow) |

Implicit theories can be changed through intervention, producing durable changes in motivation and achievement.

# Chapter 10: Goals, Motivation, and Social Self-Regulation

## Section 10.1: Goal Dynamics

**Fishbach and Dhar (2005)** examined how making progress toward a goal affects subsequent goal-directed behaviour — finding that people who make progress sometimes reduce effort (self-licensing) and sometimes increase effort (goal activation).

The key moderator is **why** the person attributes their progress:
- If progress signals **commitment** to the goal: continue or increase effort (goal activation).
- If progress signals **sufficient attainment** of the goal: reduce effort (self-licensing).

This has practical implications for health behaviour: eating a salad for lunch can either signal commitment to healthy eating (eat healthy dinner too) or license an indulgence (deserve a treat).

## Section 10.2: Social Self-Regulation

**Fitzsimons and Finkel (2011)** examined **outsourcing of self-regulation** to close others: people rely on relationship partners to help them regulate themselves, achieving goals they might not achieve alone.

Key mechanisms:
- **Partner-specific goal pursuit**: people pursue certain goals preferentially in the context of a particular relationship (e.g., being artistic with one friend, being intellectual with another).
- **Regulatory outsourcing**: delegating self-regulatory functions (monitoring, motivating) to partners reduces reliance on the self-regulatory system.
- **Risk**: when the relationship ends or the partner is unavailable, goals that were outsourced to that partner may become inaccessible — contributing to post-breakup changes in self-concept and motivation.

This framework integrates relational and motivational processes: social relationships are not just contexts for goal pursuit but active participants in self-regulation.
