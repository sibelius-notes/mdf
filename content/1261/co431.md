# Chapter 1: Partitions and Young Diagrams

The theory of symmetric functions rests on the combinatorics of integer partitions. A **partition** of a non-negative integer \( n \) is a weakly decreasing sequence \( \lambda = (\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_k > 0) \) of positive integers summing to \( n \). We write \( |\lambda| = \sum_i \lambda_i = n \) and say \( \lambda \vdash n \). The number of parts is \( \ell(\lambda) = k \), and the number of partitions of \( n \) is denoted \( p(n) \). Euler showed the generating function satisfies

\[
\sum_{n \geq 0} p(n) q^n = \prod_{k \geq 1} \frac{1}{1 - q^k},
\]

a product that encodes the independent choices of how many times each part size appears. The resulting pentagonal number theorem gives a beautiful recursive formula for \( p(n) \).

The **Young (Ferrers) diagram** of \( \lambda \) is the left-justified array of boxes with \( \lambda_i \) boxes in row \( i \). Two notations are common: English notation places row 1 at the top, while French notation places it at the bottom. We work primarily in English notation. The **conjugate partition** \( \lambda' \) is obtained by reflecting across the main diagonal; explicitly, the \( j \)-th part of \( \lambda' \) counts the number of rows of \( \lambda \) with at least \( j \) boxes. It satisfies \( (\lambda')' = \lambda \).

Partitions of the same integer can be compared via the **dominance order**: \( \lambda \geq_{\mathrm{dom}} \mu \) if and only if \( \sum_{i \leq k} \lambda_i \geq \sum_{i \leq k} \mu_i \) for all \( k \geq 1 \). This partial order on partitions of \( n \) plays a fundamental role in the positivity properties of change-of-basis matrices.

For a box \( (i, j) \in \lambda \), the **hook length** is \( h(i,j) = \lambda_i - j + \lambda'_j - i + 1 \), counting the box itself plus those directly to its right and directly below it in the diagram. These integers appear in the celebrated **hook-length formula**:

<div>
The number of standard Young tableaux of shape \( \lambda \vdash n \) is
\[
f^\lambda = \frac{n!}{\prod_{(i,j) \in \lambda} h(i,j)}.
\]
</div>

A **semistandard Young tableau (SSYT)** of shape \( \lambda \) is a filling of the Young diagram with positive integers that is weakly increasing along each row and strictly increasing down each column. The **content** (or weight) of a tableau \( T \) is the vector \( \mu \) where \( \mu_i \) counts the number of \( i \)'s appearing in \( T \). The **Kostka number** \( K_{\lambda\mu} \) counts the SSYT of shape \( \lambda \) and content \( \mu \). A **standard Young tableau (SYT)** is the special case where the entries are exactly \( \{1, \ldots, n\} \), each appearing once; equivalently, \( K_{\lambda, (1^n)} = f^\lambda \).

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| \( \lambda \vdash n \) | Integer partition of \( n \) |
| \( \ell(\lambda) \) | Number of parts |
| \( \lambda' \) | Conjugate partition |
| \( K_{\lambda\mu} \) | Kostka number: \#SSYT of shape \( \lambda \), content \( \mu \) |
| \( f^\lambda \) | Number of SYT of shape \( \lambda \) |

# Chapter 2: The Ring of Symmetric Functions

A polynomial \( f \in \mathbb{Z}[x_1, \ldots, x_n] \) is **symmetric** if \( f(x_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, x_{\sigma(n)}) = f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \) for every permutation \( \sigma \in S_n \). The symmetric polynomials form a subring \( \Lambda_n = \mathbb{Z}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]^{S_n} \). Passing to the inverse limit as \( n \to \infty \) yields the **ring of symmetric functions**

\[
\Lambda = \varprojlim_n \Lambda_n,
\]

whose elements are formal power series in infinitely many variables, symmetric under all finite permutations. The ring \( \Lambda \) is graded: \( \Lambda = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} \Lambda^n \), where \( \Lambda^n \) consists of homogeneous symmetric functions of degree \( n \). The standard bases of \( \Lambda \) are indexed by partitions.

**Monomial symmetric functions.** For \( \lambda \vdash n \), define \( m_\lambda = \sum x^{\alpha} \), summed over all distinct monomials \( x^\alpha \) with exponent multiset \( \lambda \). For example, \( m_{(2,1)} = \sum_{i \neq j} x_i^2 x_j \). The \( \{ m_\lambda \}_{\lambda \vdash n} \) form a \( \mathbb{Z} \)-basis of \( \Lambda^n \).

**Elementary symmetric functions.** Define \( e_k = m_{(1^k)} = \sum_{i_1 < \cdots < i_k} x_{i_1} \cdots x_{i_k} \). The generating function is
\[
\sum_{k \geq 0} e_k t^k = \prod_{i \geq 1} (1 + x_i t).
\]
A fundamental theorem states \( \Lambda = \mathbb{Z}[e_1, e_2, \ldots] \), a polynomial algebra in the \( e_k \).

**Complete homogeneous symmetric functions.** Define \( h_k = \sum_{i_1 \leq \cdots \leq i_k} x_{i_1} \cdots x_{i_k} = \sum_{|\lambda| = k} m_\lambda \). The generating function is
\[
\sum_{k \geq 0} h_k t^k = \prod_{i \geq 1} \frac{1}{1 - x_i t}.
\]
Similarly, \( \Lambda = \mathbb{Z}[h_1, h_2, \ldots] \). The two generating functions are reciprocal: \( \left(\sum_k e_k t^k\right)\left(\sum_k h_k (-t)^k\right) = 1 \), which yields **Newton's identity**:
\[
h_n - e_1 h_{n-1} + e_2 h_{n-2} - \cdots + (-1)^n e_n = 0.
\]

**Power sum symmetric functions.** Define \( p_k = \sum_i x_i^k = m_{(k)} \). For a partition \( \lambda = (\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_\ell) \), set \( p_\lambda = p_{\lambda_1} \cdots p_{\lambda_\ell} \). Over \( \mathbb{Q} \), the \( p_\lambda \) form a basis: \( \Lambda_\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Q}[p_1, p_2, \ldots] \), but over \( \mathbb{Z} \) this fails (e.g., \( h_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(p_1^2 + p_2) \)).

The **involution** \( \omega : \Lambda \to \Lambda \) is the algebra map defined by \( \omega(e_k) = h_k \) (equivalently, \( \omega(h_k) = e_k \)). It satisfies \( \omega^2 = \mathrm{id} \) and \( \omega(p_\lambda) = \varepsilon(\lambda) p_\lambda \) where \( \varepsilon(\lambda) = (-1)^{|\lambda| - \ell(\lambda)} \) is the sign of a permutation of cycle type \( \lambda \). The involution \( \omega \) is a graded algebra isomorphism that will recur throughout the theory.

**Schur functions** are the central objects of the theory. They can be defined in several equivalent ways; we will see all of them over the course. The **combinatorial definition** is:
\[
s_\lambda = \sum_{T \text{ SSYT of shape } \lambda} x^{\mathrm{wt}(T)},
\]
where \( x^{\mathrm{wt}(T)} = \prod_i x_i^{\mu_i} \) for content \( \mu \). Expanding over all SSYT, one obtains \( s_\lambda = \sum_\mu K_{\lambda\mu} m_\mu \), showing that \( \{s_\lambda\} \) is a \( \mathbb{Z} \)-basis of \( \Lambda \) (since \( K_{\lambda\lambda} = 1 \) and \( K_{\lambda\mu} = 0 \) unless \( \lambda \geq_{\mathrm{dom}} \mu \), making the transition matrix unitriangular).

# Chapter 3: Schur Functions and the Jacobi-Trudi Identity

The algebraic definition of Schur functions is the **Jacobi-Trudi identity**:

\[
s_\lambda = \det\bigl(h_{\lambda_i - i + j}\bigr)_{1 \leq i, j \leq \ell(\lambda)},
\]

where \( h_k = 0 \) for \( k < 0 \) and \( h_0 = 1 \). A dual form uses the conjugate partition and elementary functions:
\[
s_\lambda = \det\bigl(e_{\lambda'_i - i + j}\bigr)_{1 \leq i, j \leq \ell(\lambda')}.
\]
These identities are proved by the **Lindström-Gessel-Viennot (LGV) lemma**, which interprets a determinant of path-counting generating functions as the generating function for tuples of non-intersecting lattice paths. The SSYT of shape \( \lambda \) biject naturally with such path configurations, establishing both the determinantal formula and the combinatorial definition simultaneously.

The **Pieri rules** describe multiplication of a Schur function by a complete homogeneous or elementary symmetric function:
\[
s_\lambda \cdot h_n = \sum_{\mu} s_\mu, \qquad s_\lambda \cdot e_n = \sum_{\nu} s_\nu,
\]
where the first sum is over all shapes \( \mu \) obtained from \( \lambda \) by adding \( n \) boxes with at most one new box per column, and the second is over shapes obtained by adding \( n \) boxes with at most one per row. The Pieri rules give a recursive method for expanding products of Schur functions.

The **Murnaghan-Nakayama rule** handles multiplication by power sums. A **border strip** (or ribbon) of \( \lambda \) of size \( n \) is a connected skew shape \( \lambda/\mu \) of size \( n \) containing no \( 2 \times 2 \) square. Its height is \( \mathrm{ht}(R) = (\text{number of rows it occupies}) - 1 \). Then
\[
s_\lambda \cdot p_n = \sum_{R} (-1)^{\mathrm{ht}(R)} s_{\lambda \cup R},
\]
summed over border strips \( R \) of size \( n \) that can be added to \( \lambda \). This rule is powerful for computing character values of \( S_n \), as we will see in Chapter 6.

**Skew shapes.** For \( \mu \subseteq \lambda \) (meaning \( \mu_i \leq \lambda_i \) for all \( i \)), the **skew shape** \( \lambda/\mu \) is the set of boxes in \( \lambda \) but not \( \mu \). The **skew Schur function** is defined by the skew Jacobi-Trudi identity:
\[
s_{\lambda/\mu} = \det\bigl(h_{\lambda_i - \mu_j - i + j}\bigr)_{1 \leq i,j \leq \ell(\lambda)},
\]
or equivalently as \( s_{\lambda/\mu} = \sum_T x^{\mathrm{wt}(T)} \) over SSYT of skew shape \( \lambda/\mu \). Crucially, the skew Schur functions expand in the Schur basis as
\[
s_{\lambda/\mu} = \sum_\nu c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} s_\nu,
\]
where \( c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} \) are the **Littlewood-Richardson coefficients**, the subject of Chapter 7.

# Chapter 4: The Hall Inner Product and Dual Bases

The **Hall inner product** \( \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : \Lambda \times \Lambda \to \mathbb{Z} \) is the unique symmetric bilinear pairing defined by declaring \( \{h_\lambda\} \) and \( \{m_\lambda\} \) to be dual bases:
\[
\langle h_\lambda, m_\mu \rangle = \delta_{\lambda\mu}.
\]
An equivalent description uses power sums: \( \langle p_\lambda, p_\mu \rangle = z_\lambda \delta_{\lambda\mu} \), where
\[
z_\lambda = \prod_{i \geq 1} i^{m_i(\lambda)} m_i(\lambda)!,
\]
with \( m_i(\lambda) \) the multiplicity of \( i \) in \( \lambda \). This \( z_\lambda \) also equals the size of the centralizer of a permutation of cycle type \( \lambda \) in \( S_n \).

The most important orthogonality result is:

<div>
<strong>Theorem.</strong> The Schur functions are orthonormal: \( \langle s_\lambda, s_\mu \rangle = \delta_{\lambda\mu} \).
</div>

This follows from the unitriangularity of the Kostka matrix together with the dual relationship between the \( h \) and \( m \) bases. The proof proceeds by showing the transition matrix from \( s \) to \( h \) (which is the Kostka matrix \( K \)) and the transition matrix from \( m \) to \( s \) (which is \( K^T \)) satisfy \( K \cdot K^T = I \) under the Hall pairing.

The involution \( \omega \) is an isometry: \( \langle \omega(f), \omega(g) \rangle = \langle f, g \rangle \) for all \( f, g \in \Lambda \). One consequence is \( \omega(s_\lambda) = s_{\lambda'} \), relating Schur functions indexed by conjugate partitions.

The **Cauchy identity** encodes the orthonormality of Schur functions through a generating function in two sets of variables \( x = (x_1, x_2, \ldots) \) and \( y = (y_1, y_2, \ldots) \):
\[
\sum_\lambda s_\lambda(x) s_\lambda(y) = \prod_{i,j \geq 1} \frac{1}{1 - x_i y_j} = \sum_\lambda h_\lambda(x) m_\lambda(y).
\]
Applying \( \omega_x \) (the involution in the \( x \)-variables) yields the **dual Cauchy identity**:
\[
\sum_\lambda s_\lambda(x) s_{\lambda'}(y) = \prod_{i,j \geq 1} (1 + x_i y_j).
\]
These identities have important combinatorial interpretations via RSK (Chapter 5).

The **Kostka numbers** satisfy \( s_\lambda = \sum_\mu K_{\lambda\mu} m_\mu \), so \( K_{\lambda\mu} = \langle s_\lambda, h_\mu \rangle \). The matrix \( K = (K_{\lambda\mu}) \) is upper unitriangular with respect to dominance order and has non-negative integer entries. The entries count SSYT, so positivity is obvious combinatorially; non-trivially, the dominance condition means \( K_{\lambda\mu} = 0 \) unless \( \lambda \geq_{\mathrm{dom}} \mu \).

| Bases \( f \), \( g \) | \( \langle f_\lambda, g_\mu \rangle \) |
|---|---|
| \( h_\lambda \), \( m_\mu \) | \( \delta_{\lambda\mu} \) |
| \( p_\lambda \), \( p_\mu \) | \( z_\lambda \delta_{\lambda\mu} \) |
| \( s_\lambda \), \( s_\mu \) | \( \delta_{\lambda\mu} \) |
| \( e_\lambda \), \( \ldots \) | (derived via \( \omega \)) |

# Chapter 5: The RSK Correspondence

The **Robinson-Schensted-Knuth (RSK) correspondence** is one of the deepest bijections in combinatorics. In its most general form, RSK is a bijection between matrices \( A = (a_{ij})_{i,j \geq 1} \) of non-negative integers with finite sum \( N = \sum a_{ij} \), and pairs \( (P, Q) \) of SSYT of the same shape \( \lambda \vdash N \), where the content of \( P \) encodes column sums of \( A \) and the content of \( Q \) encodes row sums.

**Row insertion.** The key operation is inserting an integer \( k \) into a tableau \( T \). Find the leftmost entry in row 1 that exceeds \( k \); bump that entry to row 2, and insert \( k \) in its place. Repeat in row 2, and so on, until a new box is added at the end of some row. The result is a new SSYT \( T \leftarrow k \) of size one larger. Inserting entries one by one builds the **insertion tableau** \( P(A) \) while a **recording tableau** \( Q(A) \) tracks where each new box was added.

Key properties of RSK include: transposing \( A \) swaps \( P \) and \( Q \); the shape of \( P \) (and \( Q \)) is the same partition \( \lambda \); and the Cauchy identity is the generating-function shadow of RSK, since summing over all \( A \) gives \( \sum_\lambda s_\lambda(x) s_\lambda(y) = \prod_{i,j} (1-x_iy_j)^{-1} \).

**RSK for permutations.** When \( A \) is a permutation matrix, \( P \) and \( Q \) are both SYT of the same shape \( \lambda \vdash n \). The bijection \( w \mapsto (P(w), Q(w)) \) gives a proof that \( \sum_{\lambda \vdash n} (f^\lambda)^2 = n! \). Under RSK, the permutation \( w^{-1} \) maps to \( (Q(w), P(w)) \); involutions \( w = w^{-1} \) correspond to pairs with \( P = Q \). The **length of the longest increasing subsequence** of \( w \) equals \( \lambda_1 \), the first-row length of \( P(w) \) — a theorem connecting combinatorial optimization to RSK.

The **Knuth relations** on words are local transformations (e.g., swapping \( xzy \leftrightarrow zxy \) when \( x \leq y < z \)) that preserve the insertion tableau \( P \). Words related by Knuth moves generate the same \( P \)-tableau, and the equivalence classes form the **plactic monoid**. The Schur polynomial \( s_\lambda(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \) is the sum of \( x^{\mathrm{wt}(T)} \) over the plactic class corresponding to \( \lambda \).

**Dual RSK** applies to 0-1 matrices (i.e., to bipartite graphs), and produces pairs of SYT; it is the combinatorial shadow of the dual Cauchy identity \( \sum_\lambda s_\lambda(x) s_{\lambda'}(y) = \prod_{i,j}(1+x_iy_j) \).

The asymptotic study of RSK leads to deep results: the **Vershik-Kerov / Logan-Shepp theorem** (1977) shows that the expected length of the longest increasing subsequence of a uniformly random permutation in \( S_n \) grows like \( 2\sqrt{n} \), and the limiting shape of the RSK Young diagram (rescaled) is the celebrated "arctic circle" curve.

# Chapter 6: Representation Theory of the Symmetric Group

The irreducible complex representations of \( S_n \) are indexed by partitions \( \lambda \vdash n \). For each \( \lambda \), there is an irreducible representation \( V^\lambda \) (the **Specht module**) of dimension \( f^\lambda \). The decomposition \( \sum_{\lambda \vdash n} (f^\lambda)^2 = n! \) (proved by RSK) confirms that this list is complete: the number of irreducibles equals the number of conjugacy classes (= the number of partitions of \( n \)).

The **character** \( \chi^\lambda \) of \( V^\lambda \) is a class function on \( S_n \); its value \( \chi^\lambda(\mu) \) on the conjugacy class of cycle type \( \mu \) is a non-negative integer when \( \lambda = (n) \), but in general is a signed integer. The full character table of \( S_n \) has rows and columns indexed by partitions of \( n \), and is a highly structured integer matrix.

The **Frobenius characteristic map** is the key link between representation theory and symmetric functions:
\[
\mathrm{ch} : \mathrm{Rep}(S_n) \to \Lambda^n, \qquad \mathrm{ch}(V) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum_{w \in S_n} \chi_V(w) p_{\mathrm{cyc}(w)},
\]
where \( p_{\mathrm{cyc}(w)} = p_{\mu} \) for \( w \) of cycle type \( \mu \). The fundamental result is \( \mathrm{ch}(V^\lambda) = s_\lambda \): the Frobenius image of the Specht module is the Schur function. The map \( \mathrm{ch} \) is an isometry from \( \mathrm{Rep}(S_n) \) (with the inner product \( \langle \chi, \psi \rangle = \frac{1}{n!} \sum_w \chi(w)\overline{\psi(w)} \)) to \( \Lambda^n \) (with the Hall inner product).

**Reading off characters.** Expanding a Schur function in the power-sum basis:
\[
s_\lambda = \sum_{\mu \vdash n} \frac{\chi^\lambda(\mu)}{z_\mu} p_\mu,
\]
so \( \chi^\lambda(\mu) = \langle s_\lambda, p_\mu \rangle \cdot z_\mu \). This means the character table of \( S_n \) is entirely encoded in the Schur functions, and conversely any identity among symmetric functions translates to a character-theoretic statement.

The **Murnaghan-Nakayama rule** (Chapter 3) now gives an algorithm: \( \chi^\lambda(\mu) \) is computed by summing \( (-1)^{\mathrm{ht}(T)} \) over all border-strip tableaux \( T \) of shape \( \lambda \) and type \( \mu \), where a border-strip tableau fills \( \lambda \) with border strips of sizes \( \mu_1, \mu_2, \ldots \) in order.

**Induction and restriction.** Under the Frobenius map, induced representations correspond to products of symmetric functions. Specifically, if \( \mu \vdash k \) and \( \nu \vdash m \) with \( k + m = n \), then
\[
\mathrm{ch}\!\left(\mathrm{Ind}_{S_k \times S_m}^{S_n} V^\mu \otimes V^\nu\right) = s_\mu \cdot s_\nu = \sum_\lambda c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} s_\lambda,
\]
and the **Littlewood-Richardson coefficient** \( c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} \) counts the multiplicity of \( V^\lambda \) in the induced representation.

**Schur-Weyl duality** connects \( S_n \) and \( \mathrm{GL}(V) \): both groups act on \( V^{\otimes n} \) (where \( V = \mathbb{C}^d \)), and these actions centralize each other. The decomposition
\[
V^{\otimes n} \cong \bigoplus_{\lambda \vdash n,\, \ell(\lambda) \leq d} W^\lambda \otimes V^\lambda
\]
simultaneously decomposes \( V^{\otimes n} \) into \( \mathrm{GL}(V) \)-irreps \( W^\lambda \) (polynomial representations) and \( S_n \)-irreps \( V^\lambda \), with the same indexing set. This duality is the geometric origin of the fact that Schur functions index both types of irreducible representations.

# Chapter 7: Littlewood-Richardson Rule and Applications

The **Littlewood-Richardson (LR) coefficients** \( c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} \) are defined by the product formula
\[
s_\mu \cdot s_\nu = \sum_\lambda c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} s_\lambda.
\]
They also equal the multiplicity of \( V^\lambda \) in the \( S_n \)-representation \( \mathrm{Ind}_{S_{|\mu|} \times S_{|\nu|}}^{S_n} V^\mu \otimes V^\nu \), and appear in the decomposition of tensor products of \( \mathrm{GL}(n) \)-representations (Clebsch-Gordan coefficients). Their non-negativity is obvious from representation theory but non-trivial combinatorially; the LR rule provides an explicit count.

**The LR rule.** A word \( w = w_1 w_2 \cdots w_k \) with letters from \( \mathbb{Z}_{>0} \) is a **ballot sequence** (or lattice word) if every initial segment \( w_1 \cdots w_j \) contains at least as many \( i \)'s as \( (i+1) \)'s, for all \( i \geq 1 \). Given a skew SSYT \( T \) of shape \( \lambda/\mu \), its **reverse reading word** \( \mathrm{rw}(T) \) is obtained by reading each row right-to-left, starting from the top row. Then:

<div>
<strong>Littlewood-Richardson Rule.</strong> \( c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} \) equals the number of SSYT \( T \) of skew shape \( \lambda/\mu \) and content \( \nu \) such that the reverse reading word of \( T \) is a ballot sequence.
</div>

This rule has several equivalent formulations (involving jeu de taquin, RSK on biwords, or the hive model), each illuminating a different aspect of the structure.

**Jeu de taquin** (Schützenberger's sliding algorithm) provides an alternative approach. A **forward slide** at an inner corner of a skew shape moves a box left or up; an **evacuation** sequence of slides rectifies the skew tableau to a straight shape. The remarkable fact is that rectification is independent of the order of slides. The LR rule can be restated: \( c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} \) counts SSYT of shape \( \nu \) that rectify to the unique SSYT of shape \( \mu \) with all \( 1 \)'s in row 1, \( 2 \)'s in row 2, etc.

**Special cases.** When \( \nu = (n) \), \( s_\nu = h_n \) and the LR rule reduces to the Pieri rule (each \( c^\lambda_{\mu,(n)} \in \{0,1\} \)). When \( \nu = (1^n) \), \( s_\nu = e_n \) and we get the other Pieri rule. Character values arise when \( \mu = \emptyset \), giving \( c^\lambda_{\emptyset, \nu} = \delta_{\lambda\nu} \).

**The Saturation Theorem** (Knutson-Tao, 1999): if \( c^{N\lambda}_{N\mu,N\nu} > 0 \) for some positive integer \( N \), then \( c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} > 0 \). This deep result, proved using the theory of **honeycombs** (integer points in a polyhedral cone), resolved a long-standing conjecture and has implications for Hermitian matrix eigenvalue problems (the Horn conjecture).

**Applications.** The LR coefficients govern intersection theory in **Grassmannians**: the Grassmannian \( \mathrm{Gr}(k, n) \) has cohomology ring \( H^*(\mathrm{Gr}(k,n), \mathbb{Z}) \cong \Lambda / I \), where \( I \) is an ideal, and the Schubert classes \( \sigma_\lambda \) multiply by \( \sigma_\mu \cdot \sigma_\nu = \sum_\lambda c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} \sigma_\lambda \). Thus LR coefficients count intersection points of Schubert varieties in general position — a striking instance of combinatorics governing geometry. The **Berenstein-Zelevinsky polytope** and **Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns** provide yet another interpretation of these coefficients as lattice point counts.

# Chapter 8: Further Topics — Quasisymmetric Functions and Applications

**Quasisymmetric functions** \( \mathrm{QSym} \) generalize symmetric functions by relaxing the symmetry condition. A formal power series \( f \in \mathbb{Z}[[x_1, x_2, \ldots]] \) is **quasisymmetric** if for every composition \( \alpha = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_k) \), the coefficient of \( x_{i_1}^{\alpha_1} \cdots x_{i_k}^{\alpha_k} \) depends only on \( \alpha \), not on the choice of indices \( i_1 < \cdots < i_k \). Two natural bases of \( \mathrm{QSym} \) are the **monomial quasisymmetric functions** \( M_\alpha = \sum_{i_1 < \cdots < i_k} x_{i_1}^{\alpha_1} \cdots x_{i_k}^{\alpha_k} \) and the **fundamental quasisymmetric functions** \( F_\alpha = \sum_{\beta \text{ refines } \alpha} M_\beta \). The inclusion \( \Lambda \subset \mathrm{QSym} \) holds, with \( m_\lambda = \sum_\alpha M_\alpha \) (sum over compositions \( \alpha \) that are rearrangements of \( \lambda \)).

**Stanley's theory of \( P\)-partitions.** Given a labeled poset \( P \) on \( [n] \), a \( P \)-partition is an order-reversing map \( f : P \to \mathbb{Z}_{>0} \). The generating function \( \Gamma(P) = \sum_f x_{f(1)} \cdots x_{f(n)} \) is always quasisymmetric. If \( P \) is a forest (or more generally an "incomparability graph" of a forest), then \( \Gamma(P) \) is symmetric. The fundamental quasisymmetric functions arise as \( \Gamma \) applied to chains with a specific labeling.

**Noncommutative symmetric functions** \( \mathrm{NSym} \) form the dual Hopf algebra to \( \mathrm{QSym} \). \( \mathrm{NSym} \) is freely generated as an algebra by noncommutative analogs \( H_n \) of the \( h_n \), and has bases of **ribbon Schur functions** \( r_\alpha \) indexed by compositions. The dual pairing \( \langle r_\alpha, F_\beta \rangle = \delta_{\alpha\beta} \) mirrors the \( \langle s_\lambda, s_\mu \rangle = \delta_{\lambda\mu} \) duality. The descent algebra of \( S_n \) embeds into \( \mathrm{NSym} \), connecting these algebraic structures to the combinatorics of permutation descents.

**Chromatic symmetric functions.** For a graph \( G = (V, E) \), Stanley defined
\[
X_G = \sum_{\kappa : V \to \mathbb{Z}_{>0},\ \kappa \text{ proper}} \prod_{v \in V} x_{\kappa(v)},
\]
a symmetric function generalizing the chromatic polynomial \( \chi_G(n) = X_G(1^n) \) (setting all variables to 1). Expanding \( X_G = \sum_\lambda c_\lambda e_\lambda \) in the \( e \)-basis, the coefficients \( c_\lambda \) count proper colorings of type \( \lambda \) (up to sign in the standard expansion). The **Stanley-Stembridge \( (3+1) \)-free conjecture** asserts: if \( G \) is the incomparability graph \( \mathrm{inc}(P) \) of a poset \( P \) containing no induced \( (3+1) \) or \( (2+2) \) subposet, then \( X_G \) is \( e \)-positive (all \( e \)-coefficients are non-negative). This conjecture remains open and is one of the central open problems in algebraic combinatorics.

**Macdonald polynomials.** The Macdonald symmetric functions \( \tilde{H}_\lambda(x; q, t) \) are a two-parameter family that specialize at various limit values: at \( q = t \) they yield Schur functions; at \( q = 0 \) they give the Hall-Littlewood polynomials; and at \( t = q^\alpha \) as \( q \to 1 \) they give Jack polynomials. The **\( (q,t) \)-Kostka polynomials** \( \tilde{K}_{\lambda\mu}(q,t) \) are defined by \( \tilde{H}_\lambda = \sum_\mu \tilde{K}_{\lambda\mu} s_\mu \). Macdonald conjectured that \( \tilde{K}_{\lambda\mu}(q,t) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[q,t] \); this **Macdonald positivity conjecture** was proved by **Haiman (2001)** using the geometry of the Hilbert scheme of points on \( \mathbb{C}^2 \), and the \( n! \)-theorem that the module of diagonal harmonics has dimension \( (n+1)^{n-1} \). This is among the deepest results in modern combinatorics.

**Schubert calculus and enumerative geometry.** The connection between Schur functions and geometry goes further: Schubert polynomials \( \mathfrak{S}_w \) (Lascoux-Schützenberger) represent cohomology classes of Schubert varieties in the complete flag variety, and specialize to Schur polynomials when \( w \) is a Grassmannian permutation. The quantum cohomology ring \( QH^*(\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)) \) has a **quantum LR rule** where the structure constants count rational curves meeting three Schubert varieties. These geometric applications demonstrate that symmetric function theory is not merely combinatorial abstraction: it encodes the enumerative geometry of some of the most studied spaces in mathematics.
