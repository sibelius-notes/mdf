# Chapter 1: Historical and Sociocultural Transformations of Sexuality

## 1.1 Introduction: Sexuality as a Multidimensional Domain

<div class="definition">
<strong>Human sexuality</strong> is a complex, multidimensional domain encompassing biological (anatomical, physiological, genetic), psychological (cognitive, emotional, motivational), relational (interpersonal, negotiatory), and sociocultural (normative, political, historical) dimensions. No single disciplinary perspective is sufficient; a <strong>biopsychosocial model</strong> integrates all levels.
</div>

Sexuality is not a fixed, natural essence but is shaped by historical forces, cultural contexts, and power structures. What is considered "normal," "healthy," or "deviant" sexuality varies substantially across cultures and across time.

## 1.2 A Brief Modern History of Sexual Thought

### 1.2.1 Pre-20th Century Perspectives

**Ancient and medieval periods**: sexuality was framed primarily through religious and procreative lenses. Augustine's influential doctrine treated all sexual pleasure as suspect unless ordered toward reproduction within marriage. Same-sex behaviour, masturbation, and contraception were categorized as "sins against nature."

**19th century sexology** (early scientific study of sexuality):
- **Krafft-Ebing** (1886, *Psychopathia Sexualis*): classified sexual "perversions" (sadism, masochism, fetishism, homosexuality) as medical pathologies — medicalizing what had previously been moral categories.
- **Havelock Ellis** (*Studies in the Psychology of Sex*, 1897–1928): argued that many "deviant" behaviours were natural variations; more sympathetic treatment of homosexuality.
- **Sigmund Freud**: sexuality (the libido) as the primary motivational force in human psychology. Psychosexual development (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital stages); Oedipus/Electra complexes; repression of sexuality → neurosis. Highly influential, largely not supported empirically.

### 1.2.2 20th Century Scientific Revolution

**Alfred Kinsey** (1948 *Sexual Behavior in the Human Male*; 1953 *Sexual Behavior in the Human Female*): First large-scale empirical surveys of American sexual behaviour. Key contributions:
- **Kinsey Scale** (0 = exclusively heterosexual, 6 = exclusively homosexual): sexuality as a continuum, not binary categories.
- Documented widespread prevalence of masturbation, premarital sex, extramarital sex, and same-sex experience — showing that "normative" sexuality was much broader than officially acknowledged.
- Methodological limitations: non-random convenience sample, over-representation of incarcerated individuals.

**Masters & Johnson** (1966, *Human Sexual Response*; 1970, *Human Sexual Inadequacy*): First laboratory physiological studies of sexual arousal and response. Developed the four-stage **Human Sexual Response Cycle** (excitement, plateau, orgasm, resolution). Also developed behavioural sex therapy for sexual dysfunctions.

**Second-wave feminism** (1960s–1970s): Re-examined sexuality from the perspective of gender power dynamics. Challenged ideologies naturalizing male dominance, female passivity, and heterosexuality as compulsory (**compulsory heterosexuality**, Rich, 1980). Transformed study of sexuality, rape, and reproductive rights.

**HIV/AIDS crisis** (1980s): Foregrounded sexuality in public health discourse; drove research into sexual behaviour, STI transmission, and the politics of sexuality. Led to the development of explicit public health sexual education campaigns.

## 1.3 Modernization Processes and Sexuality

**Sexual revolution** (1960s–1970s): The oral contraceptive pill (1960), legal reforms, feminist movement, and cultural changes produced significant shifts in sexual behaviour and attitudes:
- Decline of the **double standard** (different sexual standards for men vs. women).
- Increasing premarital sexual activity.
- Declining age at first intercourse.
- Increasing sexual diversity visibility.

**Sexual script theory** (Gagnon & Simon, 1973): Sexual behaviour is not driven by biological impulses but is **scripted** — organized by social, cultural, and interpersonal scripts that specify who, what, when, where, and how sex happens. Scripts operate at cultural, interpersonal, and intrapsychic levels.

## 1.4 Gender and Sexual Diversity

<div class="definition">
<strong>Sex</strong>: biological characteristics (chromosomes, gonads, hormones, genitalia). Typically binary (male/female) but intersex conditions (~1.7% of the population) involve atypical combinations.

<strong>Gender</strong>: the social, psychological, and cultural roles, identities, and expressions associated with being a man, woman, or non-binary person. Distinct from biological sex; shaped by socialization and culture.

<strong>Gender identity</strong>: a person's internal sense of their own gender.

<strong>Gender expression</strong>: how a person presents their gender outwardly (dress, behaviour, etc.).

<strong>Sexual orientation</strong>: the direction of a person's enduring sexual and romantic attraction.
</div>

**Transgender and non-binary identities**: Trans people's gender identity differs from their sex assigned at birth. Non-binary, genderqueer, and agender identities do not fit within the male/female binary. Gender diversity is documented across cultures and throughout history (e.g., Two-Spirit identities in many Indigenous cultures).

**Sexual orientation** is multidimensional — encompassing attraction, behaviour, identity, and fantasy. The Kinsey Scale was groundbreaking but has been supplemented by the **Klein Sexual Orientation Grid** (seven dimensions including attraction, behaviour, fantasies, emotional preference, social preference, self-identification, lifestyle — each rated at three time points).

**Prevalence**: Cross-national surveys consistently find approximately 3–5% of adults identify as gay or lesbian, 3–4% as bisexual, and a larger proportion report some same-sex experience without identity. Numbers vary significantly by survey method and cultural context.

**Causes of sexual orientation**: No single cause. Evidence points to a mosaic of biological factors (genetic contributions: ~40–50% heritability; prenatal hormone exposure; fraternal birth order effect for gay men) interacting with developmental and sociocultural factors. Attempts at changing sexual orientation ("conversion therapy") are ineffective and harmful.

# Chapter 2: Sexual Anatomy and Physiology

## 2.1 Female Sexual Anatomy

**External genitalia** (vulva):
- **Mons pubis**: fatty pad over pubic symphysis.
- **Labia majora**: outer skin folds; contain adipose tissue, sweat glands.
- **Labia minora**: inner skin folds; rich in nerve endings; swell with arousal.
- **Clitoris**: primary organ of female sexual pleasure; homologous to the penis. The visible **glans** is only the tip — the full clitoris is an internal structure with crura (legs) and vestibular bulbs extending internally around the vaginal opening. Contains ~8,000 nerve endings (more than the glans of the penis).
- **Vestibule**: area between the labia minora containing the urethral and vaginal openings; Bartholin's glands lubricate the vestibule during arousal.

**Internal structures**:
- **Vagina**: muscular canal from vaginal opening to cervix; serves as birth canal and receives penis during coitus. Normally collapsed; expands dramatically with arousal (vaginal tenting) and during childbirth.
- **Uterus**: hollow muscular organ; site of fetal development.
- **Fallopian tubes**: transport ova from ovaries to uterus; site of fertilization.
- **Ovaries**: produce ova and sex hormones (estrogens, progesterone, testosterone).

## 2.2 Male Sexual Anatomy

**External genitalia**:
- **Penis**: organ of urination, sexual pleasure, and sperm deposition. Consists of the **glans** (head), **corona** (rim), **shaft** (body), and **frenulum** (sensitive tissue on underside). Three erectile cylinders: two **corpora cavernosa** and one **corpus spongiosum** surrounding the urethra.
- **Scrotum**: sac containing the testes; maintains temperature ~2°C below core body temperature for spermatogenesis.

**Internal structures**:
- **Testes**: produce sperm (spermatogenesis) and testosterone.
- **Epididymis**: coiled tube where sperm mature and are stored.
- **Vas deferens**: tube transporting sperm from epididymis to ejaculatory duct.
- **Seminal vesicles**: produce ~60% of semen volume (fructose, prostaglandins).
- **Prostate gland**: produces alkaline fluid (~30% of semen volume) that protects sperm in acidic vaginal environment.
- **Cowper's glands**: secrete pre-ejaculatory fluid (pre-cum) that neutralizes urethral acidity.

## 2.3 Prenatal Sexual Differentiation

<div class="definition">
<strong>Sexual differentiation</strong> refers to the developmental process by which the embryo becomes anatomically male, female, or intersex. It proceeds through chromosomal, gonadal, and hormonal stages.
</div>

**Chromosomal sex**: established at fertilization (XX = female; XY = male). The Y chromosome carries the **SRY gene** (sex-determining region Y) that triggers testis development.

**Gonadal differentiation**: Until ~6 weeks of gestation, the gonads are **bipotential** (can develop into either testes or ovaries). In XY embryos, SRY protein activates a cascade leading to Sertoli cell development → testis. In XX embryos, absent SRY → ovaries develop by default.

**Hormonal differentiation**: Fetal testes secrete testosterone and **Müllerian-inhibiting factor (MIF)**:
- Testosterone → masculinizes the external genitalia and promotes Wolffian duct development (→ vas deferens, seminal vesicles, epididymis).
- MIF → causes regression of Müllerian ducts (which would otherwise become the uterus, fallopian tubes, upper vagina).
- In the absence of testosterone → feminization occurs by default.

**Brain differentiation**: Testosterone and its aromatized product estradiol organize certain brain circuits during critical periods, producing sex differences in the brain (e.g., hypothalamic sexually dimorphic nuclei). These organizational effects are permanent; later activational effects of hormones are reversible.

**Intersex conditions** (DSDs — Differences of Sex Development):
- **Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)**: excess adrenal androgens in XX fetuses → virilization of external genitalia; interested in female-typical activities; often heterosexual or bisexual.
- **Androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS)**: XY individuals with non-functional androgen receptors → female-typical external genitalia; typically female gender identity and heterosexual attraction.
- **5-alpha-reductase deficiency**: XY individuals; external genitalia appear female at birth, but masculinize at puberty due to testosterone surge.

# Chapter 3: Sexual Arousal, Response, and Behaviour

## 3.1 Models of the Human Sexual Response Cycle

### 3.1.1 Masters and Johnson's Four-Stage Model

**Masters & Johnson (1966)** described four physiological stages:

| Stage | Key physiological responses |
|-------|---------------------------|
| **Excitement** | Vasocongestion (genital engorge ment); vaginal lubrication; penile erection; nipple erection; heart rate ↑ |
| **Plateau** | Continued vasocongestion; clitoral retraction under hood; Cowper's gland secretion; sex flush |
| **Orgasm** | Rhythmic contractions of genitalia (0.8 s intervals); uterine/vaginal contractions; ejaculation; myotonia peaks; subjective pleasure |
| **Resolution** | Return to unaroused state; male **refractory period** (females have less defined refractory period → potential for multiple orgasms) |

### 3.1.2 Kaplan's Three-Stage Model

Helen Singer Kaplan (1979) proposed three phases, adding **desire** as a separate stage preceding Masters & Johnson's cycle:
1. **Desire**: psychological motivation/urge for sexual activity (driven by testosterone, dopaminergic systems, psychological factors).
2. **Excitement**: vasocongestion (combines Masters & Johnson's excitement + plateau).
3. **Orgasm**.

Kaplan's model proved clinically important because **hypoactive sexual desire disorder** requires different treatment from excitement or orgasm disorders.

### 3.1.3 Basson's Circular Model (Female Sexuality)

Rosemary Basson (2001) proposed that women's sexual response often does not begin with spontaneous desire but with a willingness to engage with sexual stimuli for reasons of intimacy:

**Neutral/receptive → intimacy motivation → engage with sexual stimuli → responsive desire/arousal → sexual satisfaction → increased willingness**

This model better captures the **responsive** (stimulus-driven) rather than **spontaneous** (drive-based) nature of many women's desire and helped reconceptualize female sexual dysfunction.

## 3.2 Sexual Behaviours and Fantasies

Sexual behaviour is diverse across individuals and cultures. Major categories include:
- **Masturbation**: the most common sexual behaviour across all demographics; serves functions of pleasure, stress reduction, and learning about one's own body.
- **Oral sex**: cunnilingus, fellatio; highly prevalent among young adults in Western cultures; not universally accepted cross-culturally.
- **Vaginal intercourse**: the statistically most common form of partnered sex in heterosexual couples.
- **Anal sex**: practiced across sexual orientations; requires negotiation of risk for STI transmission.
- **Manual stimulation**: touching and stroking partner's genitals.

**Sexual fantasies**: nearly universal; serve functions of arousal, planning, and exploring scenarios that may not be desired in reality. The most common fantasies involve sex with a current or past partner, novel partners, and multi-partner scenarios. Fantasizing about a scenario does not indicate desire to enact it.

# Chapter 4: Sexual Development Across the Lifespan

## 4.1 Childhood Sexuality

Infants and young children are capable of physiological sexual responses (erection, vaginal lubrication) from birth. These are reflex responses, not experiences of adult-like sexuality.

Childhood involves natural exploratory behaviour (self-touching, "playing doctor") that is normative and distinct from adult sexuality. **Age-inappropriate sexual knowledge or behaviour** may indicate sexual abuse.

## 4.2 Adolescence

**Puberty** is triggered by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis: pulsatile GnRH → LH and FSH → sex hormone production.

**Female puberty** (average onset: 10–12 years): breast development (thelarche), pubic/axillary hair (adrenarche), growth spurt, menarche (first menstruation; average 12.4 years in Canada).

**Male puberty** (average onset: 11–13 years): testicular enlargement, pubic/axillary/facial hair, voice change, penis growth, sperm production, growth spurt, first ejaculation (~12.5 years).

**Adolescent sexual development** includes: developing sexual identity, first sexual experiences, navigating peer norms and media messages about sex. First intercourse in Canada: ~16–17 years on average. Comprehensive sex education delays first intercourse, increases contraceptive use, and reduces STI rates — abstinence-only programs are ineffective.

## 4.3 Adulthood

Sexual activity and satisfaction can remain high through middle adulthood. Relationship quality is the strongest predictor of sexual satisfaction.

**Pregnancy and postpartum**: sexual desire often decreases during late pregnancy and postpartum (hormonal changes, fatigue, body image); communication and flexibility are important.

## 4.4 Later Life

**Menopause** (average: 51 years): cessation of ovulation and menstruation; declining estrogen → vaginal atrophy, reduced lubrication, potential dyspareunia (painful sex). Hormone therapy and lubricants can address these changes. Psychological and relational factors often have greater impact on sexual satisfaction than physiology.

**Male aging**: testosterone gradually declines (not an abrupt "male menopause"); erectile dysfunction prevalence increases; vascular and neurological health become more important. Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil/Viagra) treat vascular ED by preventing cGMP breakdown → sustained vasocongestion.

Sexual activity can continue throughout the lifespan, constrained by health, partner availability, and cultural ageist attitudes about older people's sexuality.

# Chapter 5: Sexuality, Love, and Relationship Configurations

## 5.1 The Diversity of Relationship Configurations

Partnered sexuality occurs in many relationship structures:
- **Monogamy**: exclusive sexual and romantic partnership with one person.
- **Consensual non-monogamy (CNM)**: any relationship structure in which all partners consent to romantic or sexual relationships with multiple people. Includes:
  - **Polyamory**: multiple loving relationships with full knowledge and consent of all involved.
  - **Open relationships**: primary couple permits sexual (and sometimes romantic) relationships with others.
  - **Swinging**: recreational sex with other couples; emphasis on sexual variety over emotional intimacy.
  - **Relationship anarchy**: no hierarchy of relationships; each relationship defined by its own terms.

CNM is practiced by an estimated 4–5% of adults in North America. Research finds CNM relationship quality comparable to monogamous relationships when based on genuine consent.

## 5.2 Love and Attraction

(See also PSYCH 354, Ch. 8.) In the context of sexuality:

**Attachment and sexuality** (Shaver, Hazan, Bradshaw): adult romantic relationships activate three behavioural systems — attachment (proximity-seeking to partner), caregiving (nurturing the partner), and sexuality (sexual desire and behaviour). These systems evolved independently and are integrated in pair-bonded relationships.

**Sexual desire**: distinct from love and from sexual arousal. Desire is the motivational state (wanting sex); arousal is the physiological response; satisfaction is the affective consequence. They can dissociate: a person can be aroused without desire (e.g., non-consensual arousal) or desire without arousal (hypoactive sexual desire with intact arousal capacity).

# Chapter 6: Sexual Problems and Paraphilias

## 6.1 Sexual Dysfunctions

<div class="definition">
<strong>Sexual dysfunctions</strong> are clinically significant persistent disturbances in sexual desire, arousal, orgasm, or pain that cause personal distress or interpersonal difficulty. They are categorized in the DSM-5 and are highly prevalent but under-treated.
</div>

| Dysfunction | Description | Prevalence |
|-------------|-------------|-----------|
| **Female sexual interest/arousal disorder** | Reduced/absent interest in sex or sexual arousal | ~10–40% (varies by definition) |
| **Male hypoactive sexual desire disorder** | Persistently low sexual desire | ~6% |
| **Erectile disorder** | Difficulty achieving/maintaining erection | ~20% of men >50; 5% of men <40 |
| **Female orgasmic disorder** | Difficulty or inability to achieve orgasm | ~10–15% |
| **Delayed ejaculation** | Significantly delayed/absent ejaculation | ~1–4% |
| **Premature (early) ejaculation** | Ejaculation within ~1 min of penetration; distressing | ~20–30% |
| **Genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder** | Pain during intercourse or attempted penetration (vaginismus/dyspareunia) | ~10–20% of women |

**Biopsychosocial model of dysfunctions**: most dysfunctions have biological (vascular, hormonal, neurological), psychological (anxiety, depression, body image, trauma), and relational (communication, partner factors) contributors. Treatment is most effective when all levels are addressed.

**Masters & Johnson's behavioural sex therapy**: sensate focus exercises (non-genital, then genital touching without pressure for performance) reduce sexual performance anxiety and restore natural arousal patterns.

## 6.2 Paraphilic Disorders

<div class="definition">
A <strong>paraphilia</strong> is an intense and persistent sexual interest in atypical objects, situations, or individuals (e.g., non-human objects, non-consenting persons, suffering). A <strong>paraphilic disorder</strong> is a paraphilia that causes significant distress or harm to self or others (DSM-5).
</div>

Key distinction: a paraphilia that causes no harm or distress is not a disorder. The harm criterion differentiates normative sexual diversity from clinical concern.

| Paraphilic disorder | Target/focus | Notes |
|--------------------|-------------|-------|
| **Voyeuristic disorder** | Watching non-consenting others undress/have sex | Without consent = illegal |
| **Exhibitionistic disorder** | Exposing genitals to non-consenting others | Without consent = illegal |
| **Frotteuristic disorder** | Rubbing against non-consenting person | Without consent = illegal |
| **Sexual sadism/masochism disorder** | Inflicting/receiving pain | Only disorder if non-consenting or causing distress |
| **Fetishistic disorder** | Sexual arousal to non-living objects | Disorder only if causing distress/impairment |
| **Pedophilic disorder** | Sexual attraction to prepubescent children | Always a disorder regardless of distress; acting on it is abuse |
| **Transvestic disorder** | Cross-dressing for sexual arousal | Disorder only if distressing to individual |

# Chapter 7: Sexual Consent and Violence

## 7.1 Defining Sexual Consent

<div class="definition">
<strong>Sexual consent</strong> is the voluntary, informed, enthusiastic, and ongoing agreement to engage in sexual activity. Key features: it must be given freely (without coercion, intoxication, or power imbalance), it is specific to each act, and it is revocable at any time.
</div>

**Affirmative consent** models ("yes means yes") replaced older **no-means-no** models in many university policies and some legal jurisdictions. Affirmative consent requires positive indication of willingness rather than the mere absence of resistance.

**Consent and intoxication**: Highly intoxicated individuals cannot legally provide meaningful consent. This is a leading factor in sexual assault cases.

## 7.2 Sexual Coercion and Violence

<div class="definition">
<strong>Sexual assault</strong>: any sexual touching or activity without consent. <strong>Rape</strong>: penetration (vaginal, anal, oral) without consent. <strong>Sexual coercion</strong>: using pressure, persistent requests, emotional manipulation, or alcohol/drugs to obtain sex.
</div>

**Prevalence**: ~1 in 3–4 women and ~1 in 6–10 men experience sexual assault over their lifetime (UN Women, 2021). The majority of perpetrators are known to the victim; intimate partner sexual assault is common and underreported.

**Rape myths** (false beliefs that blame victims and excuse perpetrators):
- "She was asking for it" (victim's dress or behaviour invited assault).
- "Men can't control themselves."
- "Women lie about rape."
- "It's not rape if they're in a relationship."

These beliefs are empirically false and actively harmful — they reduce victim reporting and support culture tolerant of assault.

**Consequences of sexual violence**: PTSD (experienced by ~50% of rape survivors), depression, sexual dysfunction, substance abuse, physical health effects. Trauma-informed care is essential in treatment.

## 7.3 Prevention

Effective prevention programs address:
- **Bystander intervention**: training community members to intervene when they witness coercive behaviour.
- **Consent education**: teaching affirmative consent norms.
- **Risk factor reduction**: addressing gender inequality, rape-supportive attitudes, alcohol availability.
- **Survivor support**: accessible reporting, trauma-informed services.

# Chapter 8: Sexually Transmitted Infections

## 8.1 Biological and Epidemiological Overview

<div class="definition">
<strong>Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)</strong> are infections transmitted primarily through sexual contact (vaginal, anal, oral). Formerly called sexually transmitted diseases (STDs); the term STI is preferred because many infections can be asymptomatic (thus "infection" is more accurate than "disease").
</div>

**Epidemiology**: STIs are among the most common infectious diseases globally. In Canada, rates of chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis have increased significantly over the past two decades. Young people (15–24) account for a disproportionate share of new diagnoses.

| STI | Pathogen | Transmission | Key features |
|-----|---------|-------------|--------------|
| **Chlamydia** | *Chlamydia trachomatis* (bacteria) | Vaginal, anal, oral contact | Most common bacterial STI; often asymptomatic; causes infertility if untreated |
| **Gonorrhea** | *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (bacteria) | Vaginal, anal, oral contact | Antibiotic-resistant strains increasing; can cause PID |
| **Syphilis** | *Treponema pallidum* (bacteria) | Skin/mucous membrane contact | Three stages; tertiary syphilis: serious organ damage; congenital syphilis increasing |
| **Genital herpes** | HSV-1, HSV-2 (virus) | Skin-to-skin contact; asymptomatic shedding | Lifelong; stigmatized; ~70% of people with HSV unaware |
| **HIV/AIDS** | HIV (retrovirus) | Blood, semen, vaginal fluids, breast milk | Destroys CD4+ T cells; AIDS = advanced disease; ART makes viral load undetectable and non-transmissible (U=U) |
| **HPV** | Human papillomavirus (virus) | Skin-to-skin contact | Most common STI; most infections clear spontaneously; high-risk strains → cervical/anal cancer; vaccine available |
| **Hepatitis B** | Hepatitis B virus | Sexual contact, blood, perinatal | Vaccine available; chronic infection → liver disease |
| **Trichomoniasis** | *Trichomonas vaginalis* (protozoan) | Vaginal intercourse | Most common curable STI globally; often asymptomatic in men |

## 8.2 Psychosocial Aspects of STIs

**Stigma**: STIs carry substantial social stigma, which reduces testing-seeking behaviour, disclosure to partners, and treatment uptake. Stigma disproportionately affects marginalized communities.

**Disclosure**: People with STIs face complex decisions about when and how to disclose their status to sexual partners. Most jurisdictions have legal requirements for disclosure of HIV status. Supportive counselling helps people navigate disclosure.

**Risk perception and behaviour**: People systematically underestimate their personal STI risk (**optimistic bias**). Partner's perceived trustworthiness is often used as a proxy for STI risk (inaccurately — most people who transmit STIs don't know they are infected).

## 8.3 Prevention and Treatment

**Barrier methods**: Consistent and correct condom use reduces transmission of most STIs by 70–95%.

**Biomedical prevention**:
- **Vaccination**: HPV vaccine (Gardasil 9) protects against 9 strains including high-risk cancer-causing strains; most effective when given before sexual debut.
- **PrEP** (Pre-exposure prophylaxis): antiretroviral combination (tenofovir/emtricitabine) taken daily or on-demand reduces HIV acquisition risk by ~99% in high-adherence users.
- **PEP** (Post-exposure prophylaxis): 28-day antiretroviral course begun within 72 hours of potential HIV exposure.
- **Treatment as prevention (TasP)**: ART suppressing HIV to undetectable levels makes the person non-infectious (Undetectable = Untransmittable, U=U).

**Regular testing** is the cornerstone of STI control. Many STIs are asymptomatic; testing every 3–6 months is recommended for people with multiple partners.
