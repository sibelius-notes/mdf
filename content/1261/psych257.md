## Chapter 1: History & Definition of Psychopathology

### What Is Abnormal Behaviour?

<div class="definition">
<strong>Psychopathology</strong> is the scientific study of the origins, symptoms, and development of psychological disorders. A <strong>psychological disorder</strong> is a pattern of behavioural, cognitive, or emotional symptoms that causes significant distress or impairment and deviates from cultural norms.
</div>

The **4 Ds** framework helps operationalize abnormality:

| Criterion | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Deviance** | Behaviour departs markedly from cultural or statistical norms |
| **Dysfunction** | Impairs occupational, social, or daily functioning |
| **Distress** | Causes subjective suffering to the individual |
| **Danger** | Poses risk of harm to self or others |

No single criterion is sufficient; clinicians weigh all four in context.

### Historical Perspectives

<div class="remark">
Early explanations were <strong>supernatural</strong>: ancient cultures attributed mental illness to demonic possession, requiring exorcism or trephination (boring holes in the skull to release spirits).
</div>

The **Greek-Roman** era introduced naturalistic models. Hippocrates proposed that mental illness stemmed from imbalances among four **humours** — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile — anticipating biological psychiatry.

The Middle Ages saw regression toward demonology and witch trials. By the 18th century, **asylums** warehoused the mentally ill under horrific conditions. **Philippe Pinel** (Paris, 1793) and **William Tuke** (York Retreat, 1796) championed **moral treatment** — humane care, fresh air, and structured activity.

The 19th–20th centuries brought competing paradigms:
- **Biological/medical model** (Kraepelin): mental illness as brain disease with distinct categories and prognosis.
- **Psychoanalytic model** (Freud): unconscious conflict as root cause.
- **Behavioural model** (Watson, Skinner): learned maladaptive responses.
- **Cognitive model** (Beck, Ellis): distorted thinking drives disorder.
- **Humanistic model** (Rogers): blocked self-actualization causes distress.

### The Biopsychosocial Model

Modern psychopathology uses a **diathesis–stress model**: a biological or psychological **diathesis** (predisposition) interacts with environmental **stressors** to produce disorder. The broader **biopsychosocial model** integrates:

- *Biological*: genetics, neurochemistry, brain structure
- *Psychological*: cognitions, emotions, coping styles
- *Social/cultural*: relationships, trauma, socioeconomic status

<div class="example">
A person with a genetic predisposition to depression (diathesis) may remain well until a major job loss (stressor) triggers a depressive episode.
</div>

### Cultural Context

**Culture-bound syndromes** (e.g., *ataque de nervios*, *koro*) illustrate that abnormality is partly socially constructed. The DSM-5-TR includes a Cultural Formulation Interview to account for this.

## Chapter 2: Assessment & Diagnosis

### Goals of Clinical Assessment

<div class="definition">
<strong>Clinical assessment</strong> is the systematic collection of information to understand an individual's psychological functioning, determine diagnosis, and guide treatment planning. <strong>Reliability</strong> (consistency) and <strong>validity</strong> (accuracy) are the two pillars of sound assessment.
</div>

### Assessment Methods

**Clinical interviews** range from unstructured (open-ended) to fully structured (scripted questions with fixed responses). The **Structured Clinical Interview for DSM (SCID)** is the gold standard for research.

**Psychological testing** includes:

| Category | Example | What It Measures |
|----------|---------|-----------------|
| Intelligence | WAIS-IV | Cognitive abilities |
| Personality (objective) | MMPI-3 | Psychopathology dimensions |
| Personality (projective) | Rorschach, TAT | Unconscious processes |
| Neuropsychological | Halstead–Reitan | Brain–behaviour relationships |
| Behavioural | ABC functional analysis | Antecedents, behaviours, consequences |

**Self-report measures** (e.g., Beck Depression Inventory, GAD-7) provide quantified symptom severity. **Behavioural observation** captures naturalistic behaviour; **psychophysiological measures** (EEG, skin conductance) index biological reactivity.

### The DSM System

The **DSM-5-TR** (2022) uses a **categorical** approach: a disorder is present if a minimum number of criteria are met for a specified duration. It abandoned multiaxial coding in DSM-5 but retains dimensional severity ratings.

<div class="remark">
Critics note high <strong>comorbidity</strong> rates challenge categorical boundaries; dimensional models (e.g., the HiTOP hierarchy) may better reflect psychopathology's structure.
</div>

**Diagnostic reliability** is quantified with Cohen's kappa (\(\kappa\)):

\[
\kappa = \frac{P_o - P_e}{1 - P_e}
\]

where \(P_o\) = observed agreement, \(P_e\) = expected agreement by chance. Values \(\kappa > 0.60\) are considered acceptable.

### Classification Issues

- **Labelling effects**: diagnosis can stigmatize and create self-fulfilling prophecies (Rosenhan, 1973).
- **Cultural bias**: norms embedded in diagnostic criteria may pathologize minority experiences.
- **Overdiagnosis**: broadened criteria (e.g., ADHD, ASD) raise prevalence concerns.

Despite limitations, diagnosis serves communication, research, and insurance functions that make it practically indispensable.

## Chapter 3: Research Methods in Psychopathology

### Why Research Methods Matter

<div class="definition">
<strong>Empirically supported treatments (ESTs)</strong> require rigorous research evidence. Understanding methodology helps clinicians evaluate whether a treatment claim is trustworthy.
</div>

### Research Designs

**Case studies** provide rich idiographic data but cannot establish causation or generalize. They generate hypotheses for larger studies.

**Correlational research** measures the statistical association between variables. The Pearson correlation \(r\) ranges from \(-1\) to \(+1\):

\[
r = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{(n-1) s_X s_Y}
\]

Correlation does not imply causation — a third variable (**confound**) may explain the relationship.

**Experimental research** randomly assigns participants to conditions, allowing causal inference. The **randomized controlled trial (RCT)** is the gold standard for treatment efficacy.

| Design | Causal Inference | Generalizability |
|--------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Case study | No | Low |
| Correlational | No | Moderate–High |
| Quasi-experiment | Limited | Moderate |
| RCT | Yes | Moderate |
| Meta-analysis | Synthesizes RCTs | High |

### Special Designs in Psychopathology

**Longitudinal studies** track the same individuals over time, revealing developmental trajectories. **Cross-sectional studies** compare age groups at one time point — faster but confounded by cohort effects.

**Twin studies** partition variance into genetic (**heritability**, \(h^2\)) and environmental components. **Adoption studies** separate genetic from rearing influences. **Molecular genetics** (GWAS) identify specific risk alleles.

<div class="example">
The heritability of schizophrenia is estimated at \(h^2 \approx 0.80\), meaning ~80% of variance in liability is attributable to genetic differences in the studied population.
</div>

<div class="remark">
Heritability is not destiny — it describes population variance under current environmental conditions, not immutability.
</div>

### Analogue, Single-Case, and Epidemiological Designs

**Analogue experiments** induce mild states (e.g., experimentally induced anxiety) in non-clinical samples, allowing experimental control. **Single-case experimental designs** (ABAB reversal, multiple baseline) rigorously test interventions in individuals. **Epidemiological research** measures **prevalence** (proportion currently affected) and **incidence** (new cases per period) in populations.

### Ethics in Research

Participants must provide **informed consent**, researchers must minimize harm, and data must be kept confidential. The **Tuskegee study** remains a landmark cautionary case driving modern IRB oversight.

## Chapter 4: Anxiety Disorders

### Overview of Anxiety

<div class="definition">
<strong>Anxiety</strong> is a future-oriented emotional state of tension and apprehension. <strong>Fear</strong> is an immediate alarm response to perceived threat. <strong>Anxiety disorders</strong> occur when fear/anxiety is disproportionate, persistent, and impairing.
</div>

Anxiety involves three response systems: **cognitive** (worry, catastrophizing), **physiological** (autonomic arousal, HPA axis activation), and **behavioural** (avoidance, escape).

### Specific Phobia

Intense, irrational fear of a specific object or situation (animals, blood-injection-injury, situational, natural environment, other). Lifetime prevalence ~12%. Avoidance maintains the phobia via **negative reinforcement**. **Exposure therapy** (systematic desensitization, in vivo flooding) is highly effective.

### Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD)

<div class="definition">
<strong>Social anxiety disorder</strong> is marked fear of social or performance situations where scrutiny may occur, driven by fear of embarrassment or negative evaluation.
</div>

Prevalence ~7%. **Clark and Wells' (1995) cognitive model** highlights self-focused attention and post-event processing as maintaining factors. Treatment: **CBT** with exposure + cognitive restructuring; SSRIs/SNRIs for pharmacotherapy.

### Panic Disorder and Agoraphobia

A **panic attack** is a sudden surge of intense fear with somatic symptoms (palpitations, dizziness, dyspnea, derealization) peaking within minutes. **Panic disorder** requires recurrent unexpected attacks plus persistent concern about further attacks or maladaptive behavioural change.

**Clark's cognitive model**: interoceptive cues → **catastrophic misinterpretation** → fear → more symptoms (vicious cycle).

<div class="example">
A person notices heart racing → interprets it as a heart attack → anxiety escalates → further physiological arousal confirms the feared catastrophe.
</div>

**Agoraphobia** = fear/avoidance of situations where escape is difficult or help unavailable. Can occur with or without panic disorder.

### Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Chronic, excessive, uncontrollable worry about multiple domains for ≥6 months, with somatic symptoms (muscle tension, fatigue, insomnia). **Borkovec's avoidance model**: worry is verbal-linguistic, suppresses emotional processing, and is negatively reinforced. **Dugas' intolerance of uncertainty model** is also influential. Treatment: CBT, relaxation, mindfulness; SSRIs/SNRIs/buspirone.

### Biological Bases

The **fear circuit** (amygdala → locus coeruleus → hypothalamus) is hyperreactive in anxiety disorders. GABA deficiency, norepinephrine dysregulation, and serotonin hypofunction are implicated. Twin studies show moderate heritability (\(h^2 \approx 0.30{-}0.50\)).

## Chapter 5: Obsessive–Compulsive and Related Disorders

### OCD

<div class="definition">
<strong>Obsessions</strong> are recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges causing marked anxiety. <strong>Compulsions</strong> are repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to neutralize obsessional distress or prevent dreaded outcomes.
</div>

Prevalence ~2%. Ego-dystonic quality distinguishes obsessions from overvalued ideas. Common themes: contamination, harm, symmetry/order, forbidden thoughts (sexual, religious, aggressive).

**Salkovskis' cognitive model**: intrusive thoughts are universal, but people with OCD appraise them as personally significant and morally blameworthy, leading to neutralization (compulsions) that paradoxically increase intrusion frequency.

| OCD Subtype | Example Obsession | Compulsion |
|-------------|-------------------|------------|
| Contamination | "I will spread germs" | Repeated handwashing |
| Harm | "I might stab a loved one" | Checking, avoidance of knives |
| Symmetry | "Things must be even" | Arranging, counting |
| Forbidden thoughts | Sexual/blasphemous images | Mental neutralizing, prayer |

Treatment: **ERP (Exposure and Response Prevention)** — gold-standard behavioural intervention. Pharmacotherapy: SSRIs (high doses) or clomipramine.

### OCD-Spectrum Disorders

The DSM-5 groups related conditions in the OCD chapter:

- **Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD)**: preoccupation with imagined or slight defect in appearance; mirror-checking/avoidance compulsions.
- **Hoarding Disorder**: persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of value; distinct neural correlates from OCD.
- **Trichotillomania**: recurrent hair pulling resulting in loss; **Excoriation Disorder**: skin picking. Both are **body-focused repetitive behaviours (BFRBs)**.

<div class="remark">
OCD-spectrum disorders share the feature of repetitive behaviours, but differ in phenomenology and partially in neurobiology. The OFC–striatum–thalamus loop shows hyperactivity in OCD; hoarding shows distinct prefrontal deficits.
</div>

## Chapter 6: Mood Disorders

### Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

<div class="definition">
<strong>MDD</strong> requires ≥5 symptoms for ≥2 weeks, including depressed mood or <strong>anhedonia</strong> (loss of pleasure), plus changes in weight/sleep/psychomotor activity, fatigue, worthlessness/guilt, concentration difficulties, and suicidal ideation.
</div>

12-month prevalence ~7%; 2:1 female-to-male ratio. **Specifiers** include: with anxious distress, melancholic features, psychotic features, peripartum onset, seasonal pattern.

**Biological factors**: monoamine hypothesis (↓ serotonin, NE, dopamine); HPA axis dysregulation (hypercortisolism); structural changes (↓ hippocampal volume); genetic heritability \(h^2 \approx 0.37\).

**Psychological theories**:
- *Beck's cognitive triad*: negative views of self, world, and future; maintained by cognitive distortions.
- *Hopelessness theory* (Abramson): stable, global, internal attribution for negative events → hopelessness → depression.
- *Behavioural activation model*: reduced reinforcement → withdrawal → further reduced reinforcement.

Treatment: CBT, behavioural activation, IPT; SSRIs/SNRIs; severe cases → ECT or TMS.

### Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia)

Depressed mood more days than not for ≥2 years (adults), with ≥2 additional symptoms. Less severe but more chronic than MDD; high rates of **double depression** (MDD superimposed on PDD).

### Bipolar Disorders

<div class="definition">
<strong>Bipolar I</strong>: ≥1 <strong>manic episode</strong> (elevated/irritable mood + ↑ energy for ≥7 days; 3+ of: grandiosity, decreased sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, distractibility, goal-directed activity, risky behaviour).
<strong>Bipolar II</strong>: hypomanic episodes + MDD; no full mania.
<strong>Cyclothymia</strong>: hypomanic and depressive symptoms for ≥2 years without meeting full criteria.
</div>

Heritability \(h^2 \approx 0.75{-}0.93\) — among the highest in psychiatry. Kindling model: early episodes sensitize the brain to future episodes. **Lithium** remains first-line mood stabilizer; valproate, lamotrigine, and atypical antipsychotics are alternatives. Psychoeducation and CBT reduce relapse.

### Suicide

Risk factors: prior attempt, hopelessness, social isolation, substance use, male sex, access to lethal means. **Means restriction** (e.g., bridge barriers, medication packaging limits) reduces suicide rates at the population level. Clinical assessment uses structured tools (Columbia Protocol).

## Chapter 7: Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders

### PTSD

<div class="definition">
<strong>Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)</strong> develops after exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence, and involves four symptom clusters: intrusion, avoidance, negative cognitions/mood, and hyperarousal — lasting >1 month and causing significant impairment.
</div>

Lifetime prevalence ~6.8% general population; much higher in combat veterans, sexual assault survivors, and first responders. Only ~20% of trauma-exposed individuals develop PTSD — resilience is the norm.

**Symptom Clusters (DSM-5)**:

| Cluster | Examples |
|---------|---------|
| Intrusion | Flashbacks, nightmares, psychological distress at cues |
| Avoidance | Avoiding trauma-related thoughts, people, places |
| Negative cognitions/mood | Persistent blame, emotional numbing, anhedonia |
| Hyperarousal | Hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, sleep disturbance, irritability |

**Biological**: amygdala hyperreactivity, medial PFC hypoactivity, hippocampal atrophy, low cortisol (contrast with depression). **Ehlers & Clark's cognitive model**: the trauma memory is poorly elaborated/contextualized, leading to a sense of current threat; idiosyncratic appraisals of the trauma maintain PTSD.

**Treatments**: **Prolonged Exposure (PE)** and **Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)** have the strongest evidence. **EMDR** is effective (mechanism debated). Pharmacotherapy: SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine are FDA-approved).

### Acute Stress Disorder

Same symptom profile as PTSD but duration is 3 days to 1 month post-trauma. Dissociative symptoms are more prominent. Strong predictor of subsequent PTSD.

### Adjustment Disorders

Emotional/behavioural symptoms in response to an identifiable stressor, disproportionate to its severity, within 3 months of onset. Does not meet criteria for another disorder. Often self-limiting; short-term psychotherapy is effective.

### Reactive Attachment Disorder / Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder

Develop in children following severe neglect; reflect disruption of attachment systems. RAD = inhibited, withdrawn affect; DSED = indiscriminate sociability with strangers.

## Chapter 8: Eating Disorders & Sleep–Wake Disorders

### Anorexia Nervosa (AN)

<div class="definition">
<strong>AN</strong> is characterized by restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of weight gain, and distorted body image or denial of the seriousness of low weight.
</div>

Prevalence ~0.9% in women; female-to-male ~10:1. Highest mortality of any psychiatric disorder (\(\approx 5{-}10\%\) per decade); causes include starvation complications and suicide. Subtypes: **restricting** vs. **binge-eating/purging**.

### Bulimia Nervosa (BN)

Recurrent episodes of **binge eating** (large amounts + loss of control) followed by **compensatory behaviours** (purging, laxatives, fasting, exercise) ≥1×/week for ≥3 months. Self-evaluation unduly influenced by weight/shape. Prevalence ~1.5% in women. Russell's sign (knuckle abrasions), parotid gland enlargement, dental erosion are physical signs.

### Binge Eating Disorder (BED)

Recurrent binges without regular compensatory behaviours. Most prevalent eating disorder (~3.5% women, ~2% men). Associated with obesity but distinct from it. **CBT** is the first-line treatment for BN and BED; **FBT (Family-Based Treatment)** is preferred for adolescent AN.

### Biological and Psychological Factors

Serotonin dysfunction implicated in AN and BN. **Restraint theory** (Herman & Polivy): dietary restraint → disinhibition → bingeing. **Thin-ideal internalization** and media exposure increase risk; sociocultural pressures explain sex differences. Heritability: AN \(h^2 \approx 0.48{-}0.74\), BN \(h^2 \approx 0.55\).

### Sleep–Wake Disorders

<div class="definition">
<strong>Insomnia disorder</strong>: difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or early awakening, ≥3 nights/week for ≥3 months, with daytime impairment. <strong>Hypersomnolence disorder</strong>: excessive sleepiness despite adequate sleep. <strong>Narcolepsy</strong>: recurrent lapses into sleep, often with <strong>cataplexy</strong> (sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion), caused by hypocretin/orexin deficiency.
</div>

**Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)**: repeated upper airway collapse during sleep → arousals → daytime somnolence; treated with CPAP.

**Circadian rhythm disorders** (e.g., delayed sleep phase) reflect misalignment of internal clock with environmental demands.

**Parasomnias**: NREM disorders (sleepwalking, night terrors) vs. REM behaviour disorder (acting out dreams; associated with Parkinson's and other synucleinopathies).

**CBT-I (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia)** is the first-line treatment for insomnia, superior to pharmacotherapy long-term. Techniques: sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring of sleep beliefs.

## Chapter 9: Personality Disorders

### Overview

<div class="definition">
A <strong>personality disorder</strong> is an enduring pattern of inner experience and behaviour that deviates markedly from cultural expectations, is pervasive and inflexible, is stable across time, and causes distress or impairment.
</div>

DSM-5 lists 10 PDs in three **clusters**:

| Cluster | Descriptor | Disorders |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| A | Odd/eccentric | Paranoid, Schizoid, Schizotypal |
| B | Dramatic/erratic | Antisocial, Borderline, Histrionic, Narcissistic |
| C | Anxious/fearful | Avoidant, Dependent, Obsessive-Compulsive |

### Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)

Pervasive instability of interpersonal relationships, self-image, affect, and impulse control. Core features: **frantic efforts to avoid abandonment**, splitting (idealization/devaluation), identity disturbance, impulsivity, self-harm/suicidality, affective instability, chronic emptiness, inappropriate anger, dissociation.

**Linehan's biosocial model**: biologically based emotional sensitivity × **invalidating environment** → failure to develop emotion regulation skills. Treatment: **DBT (Dialectical Behaviour Therapy)** — skills training (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness) + individual therapy.

### Antisocial Personality Disorder (ASPD) & Psychopathy

ASPD requires conduct disorder before age 15 plus adult pattern of disregard for/violation of others' rights. **Psychopathy** (Hare's PCL-R) adds affective features: shallow affect, callousness, lack of remorse, grandiosity. Prevalence: ASPD ~3% men, 1% women. Heritability moderate; environmental factors (abuse, poverty) contribute. Notoriously difficult to treat; no established evidence-based therapy.

### Other Notable PDs

- **Schizotypal PD**: odd beliefs, magical thinking, perceptual distortions; on schizophrenia spectrum genetically.
- **Narcissistic PD**: grandiosity, need for admiration, lack of empathy; distinguished from healthy self-esteem by fragility.
- **Avoidant PD**: social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy, hypersensitivity to criticism; overlaps with generalized SAD.

### Alternative DSM-5 Model

The DSM-5 Section III proposes a **dimensional–categorical hybrid** model rating personality functioning and five pathological trait domains (OCEAN-adjacent). Moves toward trait-based description favoured by research.

## Chapter 10: Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders

### Definitions and Classification

<div class="definition">
<strong>Substance use disorder (SUD)</strong> is a cluster of cognitive, behavioural, and physiological symptoms indicating continued use despite significant problems. Severity is determined by the number of 11 criteria met: mild (2–3), moderate (4–5), severe (≥6).
</div>

Key criteria include: taking more than intended, failed attempts to cut down, craving, tolerance (**\(\uparrow\) dose needed for same effect**), withdrawal (**physiological syndrome on cessation**), and continued use despite negative consequences.

### Substance Classes and Mechanisms

| Class | Examples | Primary Mechanism |
|-------|---------|------------------|
| Alcohol | Ethanol | GABA agonism, NMDA antagonism |
| Stimulants | Cocaine, amphetamine, caffeine | Dopamine/NE reuptake inhibition or release |
| Opioids | Heroin, oxycodone | μ-opioid receptor agonism |
| Cannabis | THC | CB1 receptor agonism |
| Hallucinogens | LSD, psilocybin | 5-HT2A agonism |
| Sedatives | Benzos, barbiturates | GABA-A potentiation |
| Inhalants | Solvents | CNS depression, NMDA antagonism |

### Neurobiological Model of Addiction

The **reward pathway** (VTA → nucleus accumbens, prefrontal cortex) is central. Dopamine surge during acute use → neuroadaptation → tolerance. **Incentive salience theory** (Berridge): wanting (dopaminergic) dissociates from liking; cue-triggered wanting persists even after liking diminishes. **Allostatic model** (Koob): chronic use shifts hedonic set point; negative reinforcement (relief from withdrawal) drives continued use.

### Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD)

Most prevalent SUD globally. Comorbid with depression, anxiety, and PTSD. Acute alcohol withdrawal can be life-threatening (**delirium tremens**, seizures). Chronic use → Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome (thiamine deficiency → anterograde amnesia).

Treatment: detox (benzodiazepines), then pharmacotherapy (naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram) + CBT/motivational interviewing. **Motivational Interviewing (MI)** addresses ambivalence about change using reflective listening and OARS principles.

### Gambling Disorder

Only **behavioural addiction** in DSM-5. Shares neurobiological features with substance use (reward circuitry dysregulation). Criteria mirror SUD: preoccupation, tolerance (needing to gamble with more money), withdrawal-like irritability, failed attempts to cut down, chasing losses.

## Chapter 11: Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders

### Schizophrenia

<div class="definition">
<strong>Schizophrenia</strong> requires ≥2 of the following for ≥1 month (≥1 must be from the first three): <strong>delusions</strong>, <strong>hallucinations</strong>, <strong>disorganized speech</strong>, grossly disorganized or catatonic behaviour, <strong>negative symptoms</strong> — with continuous signs for ≥6 months.
</div>

Prevalence ~1% worldwide, similar across cultures. Onset typically late adolescence/early adulthood; earlier in males (18–25) than females (25–35).

**Positive symptoms** (excess/distortion of normal function):
- *Delusions*: fixed false beliefs (persecutory, referential, grandiose, somatic)
- *Hallucinations*: most commonly **auditory** (voices commenting, conversing)
- *Disorganized thinking*: loose associations, tangentiality, word salad
- *Catatonia*: motoric unresponsiveness or excitement

**Negative symptoms** (diminution of normal function): **avolition**, **alogia**, **anhedonia**, **affective flattening**, asociality. More treatment-resistant and predictive of functional outcome.

### Neurobiology

**Dopamine hypothesis**: positive symptoms result from hyperdopaminergia in the **mesolimbic pathway**; negative/cognitive symptoms from hypodopaminergia in the **mesocortical pathway** (PFC).

**Glutamate hypothesis**: NMDA receptor hypofunction explains broader symptom profile; ketamine/PCP model. Structural: enlarged ventricles, reduced grey matter in PFC, temporal lobes, hippocampus. Heritability \(h^2 \approx 0.79{-}0.85\); monozygotic twin concordance ~48% (environmental contribution).

**Neurodevelopmental model**: prenatal insults (infection, malnutrition, obstetric complications) + genetic vulnerability → aberrant neural circuitry → psychosis triggered by adolescent stress.

### Treatment

**Typical antipsychotics** (D2 blockers, e.g., haloperidol): treat positive symptoms; side effects include **EPS** and **tardive dyskinesia**. **Atypical antipsychotics** (e.g., clozapine, risperidone, quetiapine): broader receptor profile; less EPS; clozapine is most effective for treatment-resistant cases (monitor for agranulocytosis).

**Psychosocial**: CBTp (CBT for psychosis), family therapy (reduce **expressed emotion/EE**), supported employment. High EE (hostility, critical comments, overinvolvement) in family → ↑ relapse rate.

### Spectrum Disorders

- **Schizophreniform**: same criteria, 1–6 months duration.
- **Schizoaffective**: concurrent mood disorder + schizophrenia symptoms.
- **Delusional disorder**: non-bizarre delusions only; no other psychotic features.
- **Brief psychotic disorder**: 1 day–1 month, often stress-precipitated.

## Chapter 12: Sexual Dysfunctions, Gender Dysphoria, and Paraphilic Disorders

### Sexual Dysfunctions

<div class="definition">
<strong>Sexual dysfunctions</strong> are clinically significant disturbances in sexual response or pleasure causing distress or interpersonal difficulty.
</div>

| Dysfunction | Description |
|-------------|-------------|
| Male hypoactive sexual desire disorder | Absent/reduced sexual thoughts and desire |
| Erectile disorder | Difficulty obtaining/maintaining erection |
| Female sexual interest/arousal disorder | Reduced interest and/or arousal |
| Female orgasmic disorder | Delay/absence of orgasm |
| Genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder | Pain with intercourse or penetration; pelvic floor tension |
| Premature (early) ejaculation | Ejaculation within ~1 minute, before desired |
| Delayed ejaculation | Marked delay or inability to ejaculate |

Biopsychosocial factors include: vascular disease, hormonal imbalance, medications (SSRIs cause sexual dysfunction), anxiety/depression, relationship conflict, cultural shame. **Sensate focus** (Masters & Johnson) and CBT are mainstay psychological treatments; phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (e.g., sildenafil) for erectile disorder.

### Gender Dysphoria

<div class="definition">
<strong>Gender dysphoria</strong> is the distress arising from incongruence between one's experienced/expressed gender and assigned gender at birth, lasting ≥6 months.
</div>

Separate criteria for children vs. adolescents/adults. Treatment: gender-affirming care (social transition, hormone therapy, surgery), psychotherapy for co-occurring distress. High rates of comorbid depression and anxiety; affirmative care significantly reduces suicide risk.

### Paraphilic Disorders

A **paraphilia** is an atypical sexual interest; it becomes a **paraphilic disorder** only when it causes significant distress/impairment to the individual or involves harm to others (non-consenting persons or children).

| Disorder | Focus |
|----------|-------|
| Voyeuristic | Observing unsuspecting people undressing/having sex |
| Exhibitionistic | Exposing genitals to unsuspecting person |
| Frotteuristic | Touching/rubbing against non-consenting person |
| Pedophilic | Sexual attraction to prepubescent children |
| Sexual masochism/sadism | Humiliation/pain (receiving/inflicting) |
| Fetishistic | Non-living objects or non-genital body parts |
| Transvestic | Cross-dressing (associated with sexual arousal) |

Treatments: cognitive-behavioural relapse prevention, anti-androgen pharmacotherapy for high-risk offenders.

## Chapter 13: Neurodevelopmental Disorders (ADHD & ASD)

### Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

<div class="definition">
<strong>ADHD</strong> is characterized by persistent patterns of <strong>inattention</strong> and/or <strong>hyperactivity–impulsivity</strong> that interfere with functioning across two or more settings, with several symptoms present before age 12.
</div>

Three presentations: *primarily inattentive*, *primarily hyperactive–impulsive*, *combined*. Prevalence ~7% children, ~4% adults; 3:1 male-to-female ratio (but females underdiagnosed). Heritability \(h^2 \approx 0.76\); genes implicated include **DRD4**, **DAT1**.

**Barkley's executive function model**: core deficit is in **behavioural inhibition**, impairing self-regulation across time. **Prefrontal cortex** and striatum are structurally/functionally atypical; delayed cortical maturation.

**Treatment**: **stimulant medications** (methylphenidate, amphetamine salts) are highly effective — increase dopamine/NE in PFC via DAT/NET blockade. Non-stimulants: atomoxetine, guanfacine. Psychosocial: parent management training, school accommodations, organizational skills training.

### Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

<div class="definition">
<strong>ASD</strong> is defined by persistent deficits in <strong>social communication and interaction</strong> across multiple contexts, plus restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities — present from early development.
</div>

Prevalence ~1 in 36 (US CDC 2023). Male-to-female ratio ~4:1; females often present with more camouflaging, leading to underdiagnosis. DSM-5 collapsed previous subtypes (Autistic Disorder, Asperger's, PDD-NOS) into a single spectrum with severity levels.

**Social–communication deficits**: reduced reciprocity, abnormal eye contact/nonverbal communication, difficulty developing/maintaining relationships.

**Restricted/repetitive behaviours (RRBs)**: stereotypies, insistence on sameness, highly restricted fixated interests, hyper/hyposensitivity to sensory input.

Heritability \(h^2 > 0.80\); hundreds of risk genes identified — no single "autism gene." **Extreme male brain theory** (Baron-Cohen): systemizing > empathizing cognitive style. **Gut–brain axis** and immune dysregulation hypotheses under active research. Prenatal factors (advanced paternal age, valproate exposure) increase risk.

**Treatment**: **Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA)** has the most evidence for skill acquisition. Speech-language therapy, occupational therapy, social skills training. No pharmacological treatment for core ASD; medications target comorbidities (anxiety, ADHD, irritability).

### Other Neurodevelopmental Disorders

- **Intellectual Developmental Disorder (IDD)**: deficits in intellectual (\(IQ < 70\)) and adaptive functioning, onset in developmental period. Severity: mild, moderate, severe, profound.
- **Specific Learning Disorder**: dyslexia (reading), dysgraphia (writing), dyscalculia (math).
- **Developmental Coordination Disorder**: impaired motor skill acquisition.
- **Communication Disorders**: language disorder, speech sound disorder, childhood-onset fluency disorder (stuttering), social communication disorder.
- **Tic Disorders / Tourette's**: motor and/or vocal tics; **Tourette's** = ≥2 motor + ≥1 vocal tic for ≥1 year.

## Chapter 14: Neurocognitive Disorders

### Overview

<div class="definition">
<strong>Neurocognitive disorders (NCDs)</strong> are characterized by acquired decline in cognitive function from a previous level of performance, affecting one or more cognitive domains: complex attention, executive function, learning/memory, language, perceptual-motor function, social cognition.
</div>

**Major NCD (Dementia)**: significant cognitive decline impairing independence in daily activities.
**Mild NCD (Mild Cognitive Impairment, MCI)**: modest decline; independence preserved with compensatory strategies.
**Delirium**: acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with cognitive deficits; not a chronic condition.

### Delirium

Rapid onset (hours–days), fluctuating course, disturbance in attention and awareness, additional cognitive disturbance. Causes: medical illness, medications, substance intoxication/withdrawal, post-surgical states. Especially common in hospitalized elderly. **Hyperactive** (agitated), **hypoactive** (somnolent, easily missed), or **mixed** presentations. Management: identify and treat underlying cause; environmental reorientation; antipsychotics for severe agitation.

### Alzheimer's Disease (AD)

Most common dementia (~60–80% of cases). Pathology: **amyloid-β plaques** and **neurofibrillary tau tangles** → neuronal death, beginning in the entorhinal cortex/hippocampus → progressive cortical spread.

**Clinical course**: memory impairment (episodic first), language difficulties (aphasia), apraxia, agnosia, executive dysfunction, BPSD (behavioural and psychological symptoms). Mean survival 8–10 years after diagnosis.

Risk factors: age, APOE-ε4 allele (↑ risk 3–8×), family history, TBI, cardiovascular risk factors. **Cholinergic hypothesis**: loss of ACh neurons in nucleus basalis of Meynert. Treatments: **acetylcholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine) and **memantine** (NMDA antagonist) — modest symptomatic benefit, not disease-modifying. Novel disease-modifying agents (lecanemab, donanemab) targeting amyloid approved in 2023–2024.

### Other Major NCDs

| Aetiology | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Vascular NCD** | Stepwise decline; focal neurological signs; executive dysfunction prominent |
| **Lewy Body NCD** | Core: fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism; REM sleep behaviour disorder |
| **Frontotemporal NCD (FTD)** | Personality/behaviour change or language impairment; tau/TDP-43 pathology; younger onset (50s–60s) |
| **Parkinson's disease NCD** | Dementia emerging ≥1 year after motor Parkinson's onset |
| **TBI-related NCD** | Cognitive deficits following head injury; CTE from repeated trauma |
| **HIV-related NCD** | Subcortical pattern; prevented by antiretroviral therapy |
| **Prion disease (CJD)** | Rapidly progressive; myoclonus; invariably fatal |

### Neuropsychological Assessment

Screening: **MMSE** (max 30; <24 suggests impairment) and **MoCA** (more sensitive to MCI). Comprehensive neuropsychological battery assesses all cognitive domains. Biomarkers: CSF amyloid/tau ratios, amyloid/tau PET imaging, plasma p-tau217 (emerging).

### Caregiving and Ethics

Dementia places enormous burden on caregivers: depression, social isolation, financial strain. **Caregiver burnout** is a major concern. Ethical issues include: decision-making capacity, advance directives, use of restraints, truth-telling about diagnosis.

<div class="remark">
Early diagnosis enables advance care planning and access to support services — benefits that outweigh potential harms of disclosure in most cases. Shared decision-making with patients and families is central to ethical dementia care.
</div>
