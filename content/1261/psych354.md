# Chapter 1: A Conceptual Framework for Intimate Relationships

## 1.1 Defining Intimate Relationships

<div class="definition">
An <strong>intimate relationship</strong> is characterized by high <strong>interdependence</strong> — partners have a substantial mutual influence on each other's thoughts, feelings, and behaviours across many domains and over extended time. Miller (2022) emphasizes: knowledge, caring, interdependence, mutuality, trust, and commitment as the key defining features.
</div>

**Why study relationships scientifically?** Relationships profoundly affect physical health (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010: social isolation is as hazardous as smoking), mental health, economic outcomes, and even mortality. The scientific study of relationships — using reliable measurement and theory — yields more robust insights than intuition or folk wisdom.

## 1.2 The Elements of Intimacy

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Knowledge** | Partners possess detailed, private knowledge of each other's histories, preferences, values, fears |
| **Caring** | Partners experience and express genuine concern for each other's welfare |
| **Interdependence** | Partners' outcomes are frequently, strongly, and diversely intertwined |
| **Mutuality** | Partners think of themselves as a unit ("we"), not solely as individuals |
| **Trust** | Partners believe the other will not exploit their vulnerability |
| **Commitment** | Partners intend to continue the relationship and make it work |

## 1.3 Types of Relationships

Intimate relationships include romantic partnerships, close friendships, and family ties. This course focuses primarily on **romantic relationships** because they typically involve the highest interdependence, the strongest passions, and the most complex dynamics.

**Attachment, caregiving, and sexuality** (Hazan & Shaver, 1987) are three distinct behavioural systems that converge in romantic partnerships — each evolved for different adaptive functions but becomes organized around the same person in adult love.

## 1.4 The Building Blocks: Social Exchange Theory

<div class="definition">
<strong>Social exchange theory</strong> (Thibaut & Kelley, 1959) proposes that relationship partners evaluate their relationship in terms of its <strong>rewards</strong> (benefits obtained) and <strong>costs</strong> (negative outcomes), comparing the outcome to two standards:
<ul>
<li><strong>Comparison Level (CL)</strong>: the outcome the person believes they deserve, based on past experience and social norms. If current outcome > CL → satisfaction.</li>
<li><strong>Comparison Level for Alternatives (CLalt)</strong>: the outcome available from the best alternative (including being alone). If current outcome > CLalt → the person stays; if not → leaves.</li>
</ul>
</div>

Satisfaction and commitment are partially independent: a person can be satisfied but leave (if better alternatives exist) or unhappy but stay (if CLalt is low).

**Investment model** (Rusbult, 1980): Commitment = function of (Satisfaction) + (Investment size) − (Quality of alternatives). People stay in relationships not only because they are satisfied but because they have invested heavily (time, self-disclosure, shared friends, children) and have poor alternatives.

# Chapter 2: Research Methods in Relationship Science

## 2.1 The Special Challenges of Relationship Research

Relationships are **dyadic** phenomena — they exist between, not within, individuals. This creates special methodological challenges:
- **Non-independence**: partners' responses are correlated; standard individual-level statistics are inappropriate.
- **Reactivity**: observing a couple changes their behaviour.
- **Self-report biases**: people may not have accurate access to their own motivations or memories.
- **Selection effects**: cross-sectional samples cannot establish causation.

## 2.2 Research Designs

| Design | Description | Strengths | Limitations |
|--------|-------------|----------|-------------|
| **Cross-sectional survey** | Measure relationship variables at one time point | Quick, large samples | Cannot establish causation; selection effects |
| **Longitudinal study** | Follow couples over time (months, years) | Can track change; causal inference stronger | Attrition; expensive; slow |
| **Experience sampling (ESM/EMA)** | Repeated assessments in daily life (phones, diaries) | Ecological validity; reduces retrospective bias | Burden on participants; compliance |
| **Experimental** | Random assignment to conditions | Causal inference | Limited to manipulable variables; artificial situations |
| **Observational** | Code actual couple interactions in lab | Behavioural validity | Lab effects; expensive coding |

## 2.3 The Actor-Partner Interdependence Model (APIM)

The **APIM** (Kenny, 2000) is the standard statistical model for dyadic data. It simultaneously estimates:
- **Actor effect**: how Partner A's predictor affects Partner A's outcome.
- **Partner effect**: how Partner A's predictor affects Partner B's outcome.

This handles the non-independence of dyadic data and reveals how each partner influences both themselves and their partner.

## 2.4 Measurement Issues

**Self-report** scales (e.g., Relationship Assessment Scale, Investment Model Scale) are the workhorse of relationship research — efficient and face-valid, but subject to social desirability and recall biases.

**Behavioural coding**: Gottman's Specific Affect Coding System (SPAFF) codes facial expressions and vocal tone during conflict — predicts divorce with ~90% accuracy from a 15-minute conversation.

**Physiological measures**: heart rate, cortisol, skin conductance during conflict interactions index stress reactivity; predict relationship outcomes.

# Chapter 3: Attraction — Initial Liking and Mate Selection

## 3.1 Proximity and Familiarity

**Proximity** is one of the strongest predictors of who we become friends with and date. Festinger, Schachter, & Back (1950): residents of an apartment building were most likely to befriend people nearest their own apartment.

**Mere exposure effect** (Zajonc, 1968): repeated exposure to any stimulus increases liking, even without conscious recognition — simply encountering someone frequently increases attraction.

## 3.2 Physical Attractiveness

Physical attractiveness has a large and consistent effect on initial attraction and is more important for mate choice than most people believe themselves.

**Evolutionary perspective**: attractive features (symmetry, clear skin, sex-typical features) signal health and genetic quality. **Waist-to-hip ratio** (WHR ~0.7 in women) is valued cross-culturally, associated with hormonal markers of fertility.

**Halo effect**: attractive people are rated as more intelligent, warm, socially skilled, and competent ("what is beautiful is good"). Self-fulfilling prophecy: better treatment throughout life creates actual differences.

**Matching hypothesis**: people tend to pair with partners of similar attractiveness level, partly because those better-looking than oneself are less accessible, partly because of anticipated rejection.

## 3.3 Similarity

**Birds of a feather flock together**: the dominant finding in attraction research. Similarity in attitudes, values, personality, and background predicts liking. **Byrne's law of attraction**: liking scales linearly with the proportion of attitudes on which two people agree.

Why similarity attracts?
- Validates our own beliefs and worldview.
- Eases interaction (shared knowledge, shared activities).
- Evolutionary: similar individuals are more likely to share genetic interests.

**Complementarity** ("opposites attract") has little empirical support as a general principle, though it may operate for specific dimensions (dominant/submissive) within established relationships.

## 3.4 Reciprocity

We tend to like people who like us. Reciprocal liking triggers positive affect, reduced anxiety, and self-disclosure, which further deepen attraction.

## 3.5 Mate Selection — Evolutionary and Social Perspectives

**Parental investment theory** (Trivers, 1972): the sex investing more in offspring will be more selective about mating. Human females invest more (gestation, lactation) → more selective about mate quality; males invest less → more interested in quantity (across cultures, this asymmetry is observed but not universal).

**Human mate preferences** (Buss, 1989, cross-cultural): women more than men value resource acquisition (wealth, ambition); men more than women value youth and physical attractiveness (cues to fertility). Both sexes highly value kindness, intelligence, and good character.

**Social role theory** (Eagly & Wood): gender differences in mate preferences partially reflect traditional social roles (men as providers, women as homemakers) rather than evolutionary programming. As gender equality increases, mate preference differences decrease.

# Chapter 4: Models of Self — Self-Concept and Self-Esteem in Relationships

## 4.1 The Self in Relationships

Our sense of who we are is profoundly shaped by our close relationships. **Self-expansion model** (Aron & Aron, 1986): romantic relationships motivate inclusion of the partner's resources, perspectives, and identities into one's own self-concept. Initial stages of romantic love produce rapid self-expansion (excitement, vitality); long-term relationships sustain a slow expansion.

## 4.2 Self-Esteem and Relationship Quality

**Self-esteem** is the overall evaluative attitude toward the self. High self-esteem predicts better relationship functioning:
- Higher willingness to risk self-disclosure.
- Less anxiety about rejection.
- Less dependency and jealousy.
- Greater satisfaction and stability.

**Sociometer theory** (Leary et al.): self-esteem is a gauge of social acceptance — it tracks the degree to which we are valued by others. Threats to relationships lower self-esteem; being valued in a relationship raises it.

## 4.3 Working Models and Internal Working Models

**Bowlby's internal working models**: cognitive-affective schemas of self (am I worthy of love?) and others (are others available and responsive?) built from early attachment experiences. These models guide expectations, information processing, and behaviour in adult relationships.

| Model | Self view | Other view | Pattern |
|-------|----------|-----------|---------|
| Secure | Worthy | Responsive | Comfortable with intimacy and autonomy |
| Anxious-preoccupied | Unworthy | Responsive (wanted) | Craves closeness; fears abandonment |
| Dismissive-avoidant | Worthy | Unresponsive | Values independence; uncomfortable with intimacy |
| Fearful-avoidant | Unworthy | Unresponsive | Desires closeness but fears rejection; avoids |

# Chapter 5: Communication in Relationships

## 5.1 Verbal and Nonverbal Communication

Effective communication involves:
- **Content** (what is said) transmitted verbally.
- **Relational messages** (how partners define their relationship) transmitted predominantly nonverbally (tone, facial expression, posture, touch).

When verbal and nonverbal messages contradict, the nonverbal channel is generally given more weight.

## 5.2 Gottman's Research on Communication

John Gottman's laboratory observations of couple conflict have identified communication patterns that predict divorce with remarkable accuracy.

**The Four Horsemen** (predictors of relationship dissolution):
1. **Criticism**: attacking a partner's character ("You never think of me").
2. **Contempt**: disrespect, mockery, eye-rolling — the single strongest predictor of divorce. Signals disgust for the partner.
3. **Defensiveness**: denying responsibility, counter-attacking.
4. **Stonewalling**: withdrawing, shutting down, silent treatment — typically male response to emotional flooding.

**Positive-to-negative ratio**: Gottman found that stable couples maintain a ratio of ~5 positive interactions for every negative one. Couples heading for divorce show ratios closer to 1:1.

## 5.3 Self-Disclosure

<div class="definition">
<strong>Self-disclosure</strong> is the voluntary sharing of personal information with another person. It is both a cause and consequence of intimacy.
</div>

**Social penetration theory** (Altman & Taylor, 1973): relationships develop through gradual exchange of increasingly personal information — moving from breadth (many topics, shallow) to depth (fewer topics, more personal). Disclosure typically follows **reciprocity norms**: we tend to match the intimacy level of the other's disclosure.

**Privacy regulation**: Relationships require balancing disclosure (intimacy) and privacy (autonomy). Both excessive disclosure (boundary invasion) and insufficient disclosure (emotional distance) impair relationship quality.

## 5.4 Demand-Withdraw Pattern

One of the most reliably observed patterns in couple conflict: one partner **demands** (raises concern, criticizes, presses for discussion) and the other **withdraws** (avoids discussion, becomes quiet, leaves). Typically: more often female demands, male withdraws — partly reflecting power asymmetries and differential comfort with conflict.

Demand-withdraw correlates strongly with dissatisfaction for both partners and predicts deterioration over time.

# Chapter 6: Commitment

## 6.1 Components of Commitment

<div class="definition">
<strong>Commitment</strong> is the intention to maintain a relationship and persist through adversity. It has both cognitive components (decision to stay) and motivational components (desire to stay).
</div>

**Investment model** (Rusbult): Commitment = satisfaction + investments − quality of alternatives. Investments can be intrinsic (time, emotional energy) or extrinsic (shared social networks, finances, children).

**Tricomponent model** (Johnson): distinguishes:
- **Personal commitment** (want to stay — desire-based).
- **Moral commitment** (ought to stay — sense of obligation, values).
- **Structural constraint** (have to stay — barriers to leaving: financial, children, social stigma).

## 6.2 Commitment-Maintaining Behaviours

Committed partners engage in behaviours that protect the relationship:
- **Derogation of alternatives**: perceive attractive others as less appealing or available.
- **Positive illusions**: idealize partners (see them as better than they objectively are, or better than partners see themselves).
- **Accommodation**: respond to partner provocations constructively rather than destructively.
- **Willingness to sacrifice**: forego personal interests for the partner's or relationship's benefit.

**Michelangelo phenomenon** (Drigotas): committed partners "sculpt" each other toward their ideal selves through behavioural confirmation — treating the partner as if they already embody the ideal → partner comes to resemble the ideal more over time.

# Chapter 7: Developmental Beginnings — Attachment and Early Relationships

## 7.1 Bowlby's Attachment Theory

<div class="definition">
<strong>Attachment theory</strong> (Bowlby, 1969–1982) proposes that humans have an evolved <strong>attachment behavioural system</strong> that motivates proximity-seeking to a stronger/wiser caregiver under conditions of threat or distress. The attachment system evolved because it increased survival in the environment of evolutionary adaptedness.
</div>

The **secure base** function: a responsive caregiver provides a safe haven (comfort when distressed) and a secure base from which the child can explore the environment. Without security, exploration is inhibited.

## 7.2 Patterns of Attachment (Ainsworth)

**Strange Situation** (Ainsworth et al., 1978): observational procedure assessing infants' (12–18 months) responses to separations and reunions with their caregiver.

| Pattern | Percentage | Behaviour on reunion | Likely caregiver style |
|---------|-----------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Secure** | ~65% | Greets caregiver; quickly comforted; returns to play | Sensitive and responsive |
| **Anxious-ambivalent** | ~10–15% | Clingy; hard to soothe; alternates approach/resistance | Inconsistently responsive |
| **Avoidant** | ~20–25% | Ignores caregiver; little distress | Consistently rejecting |
| **Disorganized** | ~5–15% | Contradictory, confused behaviour | Frightened or frightening |

## 7.3 Adult Attachment

Hazan & Shaver (1987) extended attachment theory to adult romantic relationships. Adults classified as **secure** (comfortable with intimacy and autonomy), **anxious/preoccupied** (craves closeness, fears abandonment), or **avoidant** (uncomfortable with closeness, values independence).

**Bartholomew & Horowitz (1991)**: Two-dimensional model (positivity of model of self × positivity of model of others) yields four categories: secure, preoccupied (anxious), dismissing (avoidant), and fearful.

Adult attachment style predicts:
- Relationship satisfaction and stability.
- Communication patterns in conflict.
- Sexual behaviour and intimacy.
- Response to relationship stress (particularly evident in caregiving).

# Chapter 8: Theories of Love

## 8.1 Defining Love

Love is not a single emotion but a **family of experiences and behaviours**. Different theoretical accounts emphasize different components.

## 8.2 Sternberg's Triangular Theory

<div class="definition">
<strong>Sternberg's triangular theory</strong> (1986) proposes that love has three components:
<ul>
<li><strong>Intimacy</strong>: feelings of closeness, connectedness, warmth.</li>
<li><strong>Passion</strong>: romance, physical attraction, sexual consummation.</li>
<li><strong>Commitment</strong>: decision to love and maintain the relationship.</li>
</ul>
Different combinations yield different love types.
</div>

| Love type | Intimacy | Passion | Commitment |
|----------|---------|--------|-----------|
| Nonlove | — | — | — |
| Liking | ✓ | — | — |
| Infatuated | — | ✓ | — |
| Empty | — | — | ✓ |
| Romantic | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| Companionate | ✓ | — | ✓ |
| Fatuous | — | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Consummate** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

**Consummate love** is the ideal but difficult to sustain — passion typically declines over time while intimacy and commitment may grow.

## 8.3 Lee's Colours of Love (Love Styles)

Lee (1973) proposed that love can be characterized by different styles (later operationalized by Hendrick & Hendrick):
- **Eros**: passionate, idealized love.
- **Ludus**: game-playing love; multiple partners; low commitment.
- **Storge**: friendship-based love; gradual development.
- **Pragma**: practical, logical love; compatible partner criteria.
- **Mania**: possessive, dependent, jealous love.
- **Agape**: altruistic, selfless love.

## 8.4 Passionate vs. Companionate Love

<div class="definition">
<strong>Passionate love</strong> (Hatfield & Sprecher): intense longing for another; absorption in the loved one; physiological arousal. Characterized by high positive affect when things go well and intense negative affect (anxiety, jealousy) when they don't.

<strong>Companionate love</strong>: deep affection and care for a long-term partner; based on intimacy, shared history, and interdependence. More stable and longer-lasting than passionate love.
</div>

**Trajectory**: Passionate love is intense at relationship onset and typically declines over the first 1–2 years (even in happy couples). Companionate love can grow over the long term.

**Neuroscience of love**: early-stage romantic love activates dopaminergic reward circuits (VTA, caudate nucleus) — similar to drug reward. Long-term attachment activates oxytocin and vasopressin systems. This two-system model explains the trajectory from passion to companionate bond.

## 8.5 Hate

Sternberg (2003) proposed a **triangular theory of hate** parallel to love:
- **Negation of intimacy** (repulsion, disgust).
- **Passion** (anger, fear).
- **Commitment** (devaluation, diminution of the hated person).

Complete hate (all three components) can co-exist with strong romantic attachment — explaining the intensity of emotion in relationship dissolution and domestic violence.

# Chapter 9: Theories of Emotion in Relationships

## 9.1 Why Emotion Matters in Relationships

Emotions serve **communicative functions** in relationships: signalling needs, intentions, and states to partners. They also motivate approach and avoidance behaviours. Close relationships are among the most potent elicitors of emotion.

## 9.2 Appraisal Theory of Emotion

**Appraisal theory** (Lazarus, 1991): emotions arise from cognitive evaluations (appraisals) of events in relation to the person's goals and concerns. The same event (partner arrives late) generates different emotions depending on appraisal (deliberate disrespect → anger; concern for safety → worry; pleasant surprise → relief).

Relationship-relevant appraisal dimensions include:
- Goal relevance (does this matter to me?).
- Goal congruence (is this event consistent or inconsistent with my goals?).
- Attribution (who is responsible?).
- Controllability (can I influence this?).

## 9.3 Emotion Regulation in Relationships

**Co-regulation**: partners regulate each other's emotional states — a responsive partner can soothe distress (secure base), but a hostile partner can amplify it. The degree to which partners effectively co-regulate predicts relationship quality.

**Emotional flooding** (Gottman): autonomic arousal during conflict (heart rate >100 bpm) impairs information processing and communication. Flooding leads to stonewalling — the physiological shutdown that prevents productive discussion. The antidote: calling a time-out and returning to discussion after arousal subsides.

# Chapter 10: Trust

## 10.1 The Nature of Trust

<div class="definition">
<strong>Trust</strong> (Rempel, Holmes, & Zanna, 1985) is the willingness to rely on a partner in the knowledge that vulnerability could be exploited. It involves confident positive expectations about a partner's future behaviour and motivations in the face of uncertainty.
</div>

Trust is not naïve optimism — it is a willingness to remain open and vulnerable in the face of risk, based on evidence about the partner's past behaviour and underlying character.

## 10.2 The Development of Trust (Rempel et al., 1985)

Rempel, Holmes, and Zanna proposed three components of trust that develop progressively over the course of a relationship:

1. **Predictability**: the partner's behaviour is consistent and reliable; they do what they say they will do. Based on specific behavioural evidence.
2. **Dependability**: the partner can be counted on more generally; they are reliable across novel situations. Based on perceived character traits (honesty, good will).
3. **Faith**: a confident belief in the partner's goodness and love, transcending specific evidence. Involves a leap beyond available evidence; makes the relationship feel secure even in the face of imperfect behaviour.

Faith-level trust is the most important for relationship quality and is associated with the greatest satisfaction and the greatest vulnerability (betrayal of faith-level trust is most devastating).

## 10.3 Trust and Risk

**Trust dilemma**: trusting involves accepting vulnerability (the risk that the partner will exploit the dependency). Not trusting avoids vulnerability but forecloses intimacy. People manage this dilemma through:
- Incremental trust-building (testing small risks before large ones).
- Attribution patterns (benign vs. malicious attributions for ambiguous partner behaviour).

**Betrayal** dramatically reduces trust, especially faith-level trust. Whether trust can be rebuilt depends on: the severity of the betrayal, the partner's accountability and remorse, and the pre-betrayal trust level.

# Chapter 11: Conflict

## 11.1 Sources of Conflict

Conflict is inevitable in intimate relationships because partners are different people with different preferences, histories, and needs. Key sources:
- **Specific issues**: household tasks, finances, parenting, sex, in-laws.
- **Personality differences**: incompatible traits that create chronic friction.
- **External stressors**: work, finances, health — stress spillover.

## 11.2 Conflict Styles

| Style | Description | Outcome |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Validators** | Mutual respect; acknowledge partner's feelings even in disagreement | Stable marriages; mutual satisfaction |
| **Volatile** | Passionate, heated; high positivity ratio | Stable if positivity compensates |
| **Conflict avoiders** | Minimize disagreements; agree to disagree | Stable if partners have low overlap in irritants |
| **Hostile** | Criticism and defensiveness; low positivity | Unstable |
| **Hostile-detached** | Contempt and emotional withdrawal | Divorce predictive |

Gottman's key insight: no single style is optimal — what matters is the ratio of positive to negative affect and whether partners share a compatible style.

## 11.3 Constructive vs. Destructive Conflict

**Constructive conflict** behaviours:
- Raising concerns calmly (softened start-up).
- Using "I" statements rather than "you" accusations.
- Active listening and validation.
- Physiological self-regulation (taking breaks if flooded).
- Repair attempts (humour, apology, de-escalation).

**Destructive patterns**: escalation (each exchange more negative than the previous), invalidation, negative interpretations, and withdrawal.

## 11.4 Perpetual vs. Solvable Problems

Gottman found that ~69% of couple conflicts are **perpetual** — rooted in fundamental personality differences or lifestyle preferences that will never be resolved. Only ~31% of conflicts are solvable in principle. Happy couples learn to manage perpetual problems through dialogue and accommodation rather than seeking resolution. Unhappy couples get gridlocked on perpetual problems.

# Chapter 12: Power in Relationships

## 12.1 Defining Relational Power

<div class="definition">
<strong>Power</strong> in relationships is the capacity to influence a partner's behaviour, attitudes, or emotions. It flows from having resources the partner values or from being less dependent on the relationship than the partner is.
</div>

**Principle of least interest** (Waller, 1938): the partner who is less interested in continuing the relationship holds more power — they can threaten to leave at less cost.

## 12.2 Sources of Relational Power

| Source | Example |
|--------|---------|
| **Resource control** | Income, attractiveness, social connections |
| **Dependency asymmetry** | The more invested partner has less power |
| **Alternative availability** | Partners with good alternatives have more power |
| **Expertise** | Knowledge the partner lacks |
| **Emotional control** | The less emotionally expressive partner often appears more powerful |

## 12.3 Power Dynamics and Equity

**Social exchange**: power imbalances create inequitable exchanges, which reduce satisfaction especially for the underbenefited (though over-benefited partners also show some distress).

**Equality vs. equity**: in modern Western couples, equality (50/50 outcomes) is valued over equity (outcomes proportional to inputs). Egalitarian relationships show higher relationship quality on average.

Gender and power: despite increasing equality, gender differences in power persist — men often have more financial power; women often have more emotional management responsibility. These asymmetries predict demand-withdraw conflict patterns and differential satisfaction.

# Chapter 13: Relationship Dissolution, Maintenance, and Repair

## 13.1 Why Relationships End

Predictors of dissolution:
- **Communication**: contempt, criticism, stonewalling — Gottman's Four Horsemen.
- **Satisfaction decline**: gradual erosion of positive sentiment.
- **Investment changes**: reduced investment (time, energy, shared plans).
- **Alternative quality improvement**: attractive alternatives become available.
- **Personality factors**: neuroticism most consistently predicts relationship instability.
- **External stressors**: economic hardship, relocation, major life changes.

**Lee's (1984) model of dissolution**: awareness (realizing dissatisfaction) → exposure (airing concerns) → negotiation → resolution attempts → termination (if unresolved). Many relationships cycle through early stages without reaching termination.

## 13.2 Consequences of Dissolution

Relationship dissolution, especially of long-term partnerships, is among the most stressful life events:
- Health consequences: disrupted sleep, immune function decline, elevated cortisol.
- Mental health: increased depression, anxiety, loneliness.
- **Grieving the loss**: even unwanted relationships involve grief.

**Who suffers more?** The partner who did not initiate the dissolution typically suffers more acutely; the initiator may have grieved the relationship before ending it (**emotional divorce** before legal/formal dissolution).

## 13.3 Relationship Maintenance

Relationships do not maintain themselves — active behaviours sustain them:
- **Positivity**: cheerfulness, being pleasant.
- **Openness**: self-disclosure.
- **Assurances**: expressing love and commitment.
- **Network involvement**: maintaining shared social networks.
- **Task sharing**: equitable division of household tasks.

**Gratitude**: expressing and feeling gratitude for partner's contributions is a powerful maintenance behaviour — increases partner satisfaction and sense of being valued.

## 13.4 Relationship Repair

After conflict, betrayal, or periods of distance, relationships can be repaired through:
- **Apology**: acknowledging harm, taking responsibility, expressing remorse.
- **Forgiveness**: releasing resentment without necessarily condoning the behaviour; benefits the forgiver's health as well as the relationship.
- **Recommitment**: deliberately choosing to reinvest in the relationship.
- **Interventions**: couple therapy (Gottman Method, Emotionally Focused Therapy) can rebuild trust and communication; ~50% success rate in meta-analyses.

**Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)** (Johnson): targets insecure attachment patterns underlying negative interaction cycles. Partners learn to express underlying attachment needs (fear of abandonment, need for reassurance) rather than defensive anger, enabling partners to respond to each other's vulnerability.
