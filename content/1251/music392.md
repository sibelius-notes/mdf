# Chapter 1: Hearing Bali Beyond Exoticism

## 1.1 What This Course Is Not

A course on Balinese music can easily go wrong if students approach it as a catalogue of unusual instruments and unfamiliar sounds. That kind of listening turns a living tradition into an exotic object. It reduces a complex musical culture to surface difference.

This course asks for something better. It asks students to hear Balinese music as a system of thought, practice, teaching, listening, and community. The brilliance of the sound is real and important, but it is only one part of a larger musical world.

## 1.2 Music as Community

One of the first ideas students encounter in Balinese music is that ensemble performance is deeply social. Music making is not only a matter of individual interpretation. It is a collective practice tied to rehearsal, ritual, local identity, mutual attentiveness, and community labor.

This is a major reason the tradition is so valuable pedagogically. It challenges assumptions inherited from some Western classical training, especially the assumption that music begins with a private score and then gets realized by performers. In much Balinese practice, music lives first in people, not on paper.

<div class="remark">
<strong>Guiding idea</strong>: to learn Balinese music is not just to learn pieces. It is to learn a different distribution of musical responsibility.
</div>

# Chapter 2: Gamelan, Instruments, and Sonic Structure

## 2.1 What Gamelan Means

The term **gamelan** refers broadly to an Indonesian ensemble tradition centered on metallophones, gongs, drums, and related instruments, though ensemble makeup varies by region and genre. In the Balinese context, the term often evokes brilliant bronze sonorities, interlocking patterns, and highly coordinated ensemble precision.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Gamelan</strong>: an Indonesian ensemble tradition organized around metallophones, gongs, drums, and related instruments, with performance structured through cyclical time, layered texture, and communal coordination.
</div>

Students should resist imagining the ensemble in the same terms they would use for a Western orchestra. The categories of melody, accompaniment, and harmony do not map neatly. Instruments often participate in layered structural roles rather than in a simple foreground-background hierarchy.

## 2.2 Tuning and Sonic Shimmer

One of the most striking features of Balinese gamelan is the shimmering acoustic effect created by paired instruments tuned slightly apart. This beating phenomenon is not an accident to be corrected. It is part of the sound ideal. The result is that timbre and tuning are inseparable.

This is worth emphasizing because students raised in equal-tempered traditions often hear tuning first as a matter of correctness. In Balinese music, tuning is also a matter of energy, brightness, and ensemble identity.

## 2.3 Colotomic Time

Many gamelan traditions organize time cyclically, with large gongs and related instruments articulating structural points in recurring patterns. Instead of hearing music as a linear march toward a single final cadence, one often hears rotation, return, and nested cycles.

This cyclical hearing is one of the major conceptual shifts students must make. Time in the music is not formless. It is highly organized, but the organization is not the same as later Western goal-directed harmonic time.

# Chapter 3: Core Melody, Elaboration, and Kotekan

## 3.1 Layered Texture

Balinese gamelan texture is often stratified. Some instruments articulate a central melodic framework. Others elaborate it, punctuate it, densify it, or energize it rhythmically. This layered approach is one reason the music feels so alive: no single line tells the whole story.

A useful way to listen is to ask what each layer is responsible for. Which instruments mark structure? Which fill in rapid motion? Which stabilize pulse? Which cue transitions? Once these questions become habitual, the apparent complexity becomes intelligible.

## 3.2 Kotekan

Among the most famous techniques in Balinese music is **kotekan**, the interlocking distribution of a rapid composite figure across players or parts. No one player necessarily performs the entire line alone. The pattern emerges through coordination.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Kotekan</strong>: an interlocking texture in which two or more parts alternate to produce a rapid composite rhythmic-melodic pattern.
</div>

Kotekan is musically important for more than its brilliance. It embodies a social idea. Virtuosity is collective. Precision is distributed. The line exists not in one body but between bodies.

## 3.3 Listening Through Interdependence

This is one of the most educationally powerful dimensions of the tradition. In some musical environments, excellence is measured by how independently an individual can dominate a line. In kotekan-based ensemble practice, excellence also depends on how accurately one contributes a fragment whose meaning only fully appears in relation to others.

# Chapter 4: Learning, Teaching, and Oral Transmission

## 4.1 Music Without Heavy Reliance on Notation

Balinese music is often taught through demonstration, imitation, repetition, and embodied rehearsal rather than through detailed written scores. That does not imply informality or lack of sophistication. It implies that musical knowledge is carried differently.

For students trained primarily through notation, this can be both unsettling and liberating. Without the page as a constant intermediary, attention shifts toward listening, memory, gesture, and the social cues of rehearsal.

## 4.2 Repetition and Internalization

Repetition in this context is not mindless drilling. It is a way of letting structure move from the outside into the body. Pattern, timing, cueing, and phrase shape become habitual through repeated shared action. This is how the ensemble achieves the extraordinary collective precision for which Balinese performance is often admired.

## 4.3 Teaching as Performance

In many Balinese contexts, teaching itself is musical. A leader may demonstrate, sing, clap, cue physically, and shape group response in real time. Authority resides not only in explanation, but in the ability to make the music learnable through clear embodied communication.

# Chapter 5: Composition, Borrowing, and New Creation

## 5.1 Composition as Social Process

The course description for this subject emphasizes new music creation, and that emphasis is deeply revealing. In this setting, composition is not imagined solely as a solitary act in which a complete score is privately produced and later handed to performers. It can emerge through trying ideas on instruments, listening, adapting, remembering, and teaching.

This does not mean the music lacks structure. It means structure may emerge collaboratively and orally before it becomes textual.

## 5.2 Borrowing and Transformation

Balinese compositional practice often treats borrowing, adaptation, and transformation as natural musical behavior. A composer listens widely, absorbs material, reshapes patterns, and recombines techniques. This differs from romantic myths of absolute originality. Creativity often lies in how traditions are activated and reworked rather than in the fantasy of making something from nothing.

## 5.3 Creating Within a Tradition

Students composing in a Balinese-inspired environment have to think carefully about what it means to work responsibly within a style that is not their own. The challenge is not to imitate the surface only. The challenge is to understand the structural and social principles that make the music what it is: cyclical organization, interlocking texture, ensemble timing, oral teachability, and leadership through rehearsal.

# Chapter 6: Performance, Leadership, and Ensemble Responsibility

## 6.1 The Role of the Leader

Because much of the music is transmitted and refined in rehearsal, leadership becomes crucial. The leader is not simply a conductor in the Western orchestral sense. The leader may also be composer, teacher, demonstrator, and performer. Direction occurs through cueing, modeling, and close participation.

## 6.2 Teaching the Ensemble

When students are asked not only to compose but to teach their works orally to the ensemble, the course is making an important claim: understanding music includes understanding how to communicate it. It is not enough to have an idea. One must be able to lead others into the idea clearly and musically.

That requirement turns composition into a public and practical art rather than an abstract exercise.

# Chapter 7: Notation After the Fact

## 7.1 Score as Record Rather Than Origin

One of the most revealing moments in courses like this is when notation appears after rehearsal and performance rather than before. In such cases, the score does not function as the sole source of authority. It documents, clarifies, or preserves what has already been worked out in sound.

This reversal is philosophically important. It reminds students that notation is one musical technology among many, not the definition of seriousness or legitimacy.

## 7.2 Multiple Systems of Representation

If a piece can be taught by singing, gesture, and repetition, and only later represented in cipher notation, staff notation, or another system, then musical literacy itself becomes plural. Students begin to see that notation systems are tools shaped by the needs of specific traditions.

# Chapter 8: Ethical Listening and the Value of the Course

## 8.1 Study Without Reduction

Studying Balinese music outside Bali requires intellectual and ethical care. One must avoid flattening the tradition into generalized ideas about spirituality, community, or exotic color. It is better to stay concrete: particular instruments, particular techniques, particular ensemble behaviors, particular histories of teaching and making.

## 8.2 Why This Course Matters

This course matters because it enlarges the student's idea of what musicianship can be. It shows that musical rigor can be oral, collective, cyclical, embodied, and community-centered. It shows that virtuosity may belong to relationships among players as much as to any one performer. It shows that composition may grow from listening and shared practice rather than from notation alone.

In that sense, Bali is not simply one more style to add to a list. It is a challenge to narrow assumptions about music itself. That is precisely why it belongs in a serious curriculum.

# Chapter 9: Broader Pedagogical and Musical Context

## 9.1 Why Ensemble-Based World Music Courses Matter

Courses on Balinese music often overlap with ensemble pedagogy more than with standard lecture history. This is not a weakness. It is one of their greatest strengths. Students do not only learn *about* the music; they learn how musical understanding changes when it is distributed across listening, repetition, gesture, and collaborative rehearsal.

This is one reason world music ensemble traditions at other universities, including long-running gamelan programs, have been so influential in curriculum design. They demonstrate that performance itself can be a form of scholarship.

## 9.2 Balinese Music and Comparative Listening

Balinese music also helps students hear more clearly by contrast. Concepts that seem natural in Western theory classrooms, such as harmony-driven form or notation-first composition, suddenly appear as culturally specific solutions rather than universal laws. This comparative insight is intellectually valuable even for students who do not continue performing the repertory.

## 9.3 Ethical Intercultural Creation

Because this course includes new composition, it must also include ethical reflection. Creating music inspired by Balinese practice requires more than borrowing timbre or fast interlocking rhythms. It requires respect for structure, teaching methods, and cultural context. The student should ask not only, "Can I use this sound?" but "Do I understand how this musical idea functions in its own tradition?"

## 9.4 Why the Notes Need More Than Definitions

Short notes on a course like this can easily collapse into a glossary of gamelan terms. That is not enough. The student also needs a sense of rehearsal culture, transmission, community structure, and the difference between learning a style from the outside and participating in it responsibly. Textbook-scale notes help make those relations visible.

# Chapter 10: Walkthrough of the Course's Creative Practice

## 10.1 Listening and Aural Internalization

The outline's emphasis on listening tasks, aural skills, and melodic elaboration is important because it places hearing before concept. Students are first asked to internalize, not merely define.

## 10.2 Compose, Teach, Lead

The requirement to compose short studies and teach them orally to the ensemble is one of the course's strongest design choices. It ensures that composition is tested in social performance reality.

## 10.3 Final Piece and Score

The final score requirement after performance elegantly reverses notation-first assumptions. Students first make the music live, then document and reflect on it. That reversal is one of the deepest lessons in the course.

# Chapter 11: Listening and Reflection at Greater Depth

## 11.1 Hearing Ensemble Roles

One of the most useful listening exercises in gamelan is to isolate functional layers. First hear the larger structural gong cycle. Then attend to the core melodic layer. Then shift to the faster elaborating lines. Finally listen for how the whole ensemble coordinates. This layered listening is often what transforms the experience from dazzling surface to intelligible structure.

## 11.2 Why Interlocking Technique Matters Pedagogically

Kotekan is more than a signature sound. It teaches a philosophy of musicianship. The individual line is incomplete by design. Completion happens in relation. This is one reason ensemble world music courses can be so educationally powerful even for students trained in very different traditions. They make interdependence audible and unavoidable.

## 11.3 Reflection After Performance

A reflection written after hearing one's own piece performed by the ensemble can be one of the most valuable assignments in the whole course. It forces the student to compare intention with result, notation with embodiment, and compositional fantasy with collective practice. That comparison is where artistic maturity often begins.

# Chapter 12: Why This Course Needs Long Notes

## 12.1 Beyond Terminology

It is easy to reduce Balinese music to a short set of words: gamelan, kotekan, gong cycle, oral tradition. That reduction is pedagogically dangerous because it creates the illusion of understanding where almost none exists. Long notes are needed to connect the terms to musical practice, rehearsal culture, and historical imagination.

## 12.2 A Different Model of Musical Seriousness

These notes also need scale because the course challenges inherited assumptions about what serious music study looks like. If a student has mostly experienced seriousness as score analysis and written theory, a course centered on oral transmission, embodied repetition, and community performance requires a deeper explanatory bridge. That bridge is what long-form notes are for.
