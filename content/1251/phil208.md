# Chapter 1: Science Fiction as Philosophical Method

## 1.1 Why Science Fiction and Philosophy?

Philosophy and science fiction share a fundamental tool: the **thought experiment** (思想实验). Where a philosopher might ask "What if you could split a person's brain and transplant each half into a different body?", a science fiction author builds an entire narrative around such a scenario. In both cases, the exercise forces us to clarify our concepts, test our intuitions, and confront the boundaries of what we take for granted.

Ursula K. Le Guin, in her introduction to *The Left Hand of Darkness*, insists that science fiction is not predictive but descriptive. The genre does not forecast what will happen; it illuminates what is, by displacing familiar conditions into unfamiliar settings. A novelist who imagines a world without gender is not claiming that such a world will arise. She is asking what gender does in our world --- what it structures, what it conceals, what it makes possible.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Thought Experiment (思想实验)</strong>: An imagined scenario used to explore the consequences of a hypothesis, test a principle, or clarify a concept. Thought experiments need not be physically realizable; their power lies in their capacity to isolate a variable and force a judgment.
</div>

## 1.2 Fiala: Science Fiction and the Boundaries of Philosophy

Andrew Fiala argues that science fiction operates at the boundaries of philosophy in two senses. First, it pushes philosophical questions to their extremes --- what happens to our concept of **personhood** (人格) when we encounter beings that are intelligent but not human, or human but radically altered? Second, science fiction tests the limits of philosophical method itself. Traditional analytic philosophy prizes tightly controlled thought experiments; science fiction, by contrast, embeds philosophical problems in messy, narrative contexts where emotions, relationships, and history matter.

Fiala identifies several philosophical domains that science fiction regularly engages:

- **Metaphysics** (形而上学): What is the nature of reality? What makes something real?
- **Epistemology** (认识论): What can we know? How do we distinguish knowledge from illusion?
- **Ethics** (伦理学): What ought we to do? What do we owe to others?
- **Political philosophy** (政治哲学): How should societies be organized? Who holds power and why?
- **Philosophy of mind** (心灵哲学): What is consciousness? Can machines think?

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> Le Guin and Fiala both resist the idea that science fiction merely "illustrates" philosophy. The relationship is more intimate: a well-constructed science fiction narrative <em>is</em> a philosophical argument, one conducted through character and plot rather than premises and conclusions.
</div>

## 1.3 The Methodological Value of Fiction

Why not simply use standard philosophical examples? Several reasons emerge from the course readings.

First, fiction generates **empathy** (共情). A thought experiment about whether it is permissible to sacrifice one person to save five can feel sterile. A story that places us inside the experience of the person being sacrificed makes the stakes vivid.

Second, fiction reveals **hidden assumptions** (隐含假设). By constructing an entire world, a science fiction author must make explicit many things that ordinary philosophical analysis leaves implicit --- what the economy looks like, how families are structured, what counts as deviance.

Third, fiction is **iterative**. A philosophical paper states a thesis and defends it. A story can explore a question from multiple angles without resolving it, which is sometimes more honest about the difficulty of the problem.

# Chapter 2: Perception and Blindness --- Epistemology Through Fiction

## 2.1 H.G. Wells, "The Country of the Blind"

In Wells's story, a sighted man stumbles into an isolated valley where all inhabitants have been blind for generations. The valley-dwellers have organized their entire civilization around non-visual senses. The newcomer, Nunez, assumes that his sight gives him an advantage --- "In the country of the blind, the one-eyed man is king." He is disastrously wrong.

The story dramatizes a problem in **epistemology** (认识论): the relationship between sensory experience and knowledge. The blind community does not merely lack something; they have developed a complete and functional understanding of the world. Nunez's visual knowledge is not only useless but actively disorienting in their context.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Epistemic Framework (认知框架)</strong>: The set of concepts, categories, and modes of perception through which a community organizes its understanding of reality. Different epistemic frameworks can be mutually incomprehensible, even when each is internally coherent.
</div>

### 2.1.1 The Problem of Incommensurability

Wells's story raises the question of **incommensurability** (不可通约性), a concept later developed by Thomas Kuhn in the philosophy of science. Two frameworks are incommensurable when there is no neutral standpoint from which to compare them. Nunez cannot demonstrate the value of sight to people who have no concept of vision. He cannot even describe what he sees in terms they understand, because their language has no visual vocabulary.

This connects to a broader philosophical question: Is there a "view from nowhere," a perspective-independent access to reality? Or is all knowledge shaped by the particular sensory and conceptual apparatus of the knower?

## 2.2 W.E.B. Du Bois, "The Comet"

Du Bois's story imagines a comet that releases toxic gas over New York City, apparently killing everyone except Jim, a Black man, and a white woman. In the aftermath of apparent apocalypse, racial barriers dissolve momentarily --- until other survivors arrive and the old order reasserts itself.

The philosophical interest of "The Comet" lies in its exploration of **social epistemology** (社会认识论) and the concept Du Bois elsewhere calls **double consciousness** (双重意识). Jim knows the world through two lenses simultaneously: his own experience and the distorted image of himself reflected back by a racist society.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Double Consciousness (双重意识)</strong>: A concept introduced by W.E.B. Du Bois in <em>The Souls of Black Folk</em> (1903), describing the experience of always seeing oneself through the eyes of a society that regards one with contempt. It produces a "sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others."
</div>

### 2.2.1 Race, Perception, and the Constructed World

"The Comet" uses the science-fictional premise of catastrophe to strip away the social structures that organize racial perception. When Jim and the white woman believe they are the last humans alive, they see each other as persons rather than as racial categories. The return of society brings the return of racial hierarchy.

This suggests that racial categories are not properties of perception but products of **social construction** (社会建构). We do not see race the way we see colour; rather, we see race because we have been taught to organize human difference along racial lines. Du Bois uses science fiction to make visible the invisible machinery of racism.

### 2.2.2 Connecting Wells and Du Bois

Both stories concern blindness --- one literal, one metaphorical. Wells shows that sensory difference can create epistemic isolation. Du Bois shows that social structures produce their own form of blindness: white society cannot see Black humanity, not because of sensory limitation but because of ideological distortion. Together, the two stories suggest that epistemology cannot be separated from politics.

# Chapter 3: The Omelas Problem --- Utilitarianism and Moral Complicity

## 3.1 Le Guin, "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas"

Le Guin's famous story describes Omelas, a city of extraordinary happiness whose prosperity depends on the suffering of a single child locked in a basement. The citizens know about the child; most accept the arrangement. A few walk away.

The story is a direct engagement with **utilitarianism** (功利主义), the moral theory that holds that the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. If the happiness of an entire city outweighs the suffering of one child, utilitarianism appears to endorse the arrangement.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Utilitarianism (功利主义)</strong>: A family of ethical theories holding that the morally right action is the one that maximizes overall well-being or utility. Classical utilitarianism, associated with Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, measures utility in terms of pleasure and pain.
</div>

### 3.1.1 The Utilitarian Calculus and Its Discontents

The genius of Le Guin's story is that it makes the utilitarian calculation as favorable as possible --- an entire city's happiness against one child's suffering --- and still produces moral revulsion in many readers. This suggests that our moral intuitions resist **aggregation** (效用聚合): the idea that individual suffering can be outweighed by a sufficiently large sum of happiness.

Several philosophical objections to the Omelas arrangement emerge:

1. **The separateness of persons** (个体独立性): As philosopher John Rawls argued, utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons. It treats the child's suffering and the city's happiness as entries in a single ledger, as though they belonged to the same entity.

2. **Dignity and instrumentalization** (尊严与工具化): Kant's categorical imperative forbids treating persons merely as means to others' ends. The child in the basement is reduced to an instrument of collective happiness.

3. **Complicity** (共谋): Even if the arrangement is optimal, the citizens' knowledge of and acquiescence to the child's suffering implicates them morally.

## 3.2 Jemisin, "The Ones Who Stay and Fight"

N.K. Jemisin's story responds directly to Le Guin. It imagines Um-Helat, a utopian city built not on hidden suffering but on active vigilance. Um-Helat's happiness depends on the community's willingness to suppress dangerous ideas --- specifically, ideas imported from our world about hierarchy, bigotry, and oppression. Those who spread such ideas are killed by social workers.

Jemisin's story shifts the philosophical problem from **passive complicity** to **active enforcement**. Um-Helat is utopian not because it ignores injustice but because it violently prevents injustice from taking root. This raises the question: Can a just society be maintained through coercion? Is there a morally relevant difference between tolerating suffering (Omelas) and preventing suffering through violence (Um-Helat)?

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> Jemisin's story also interrogates the politics of knowledge. The dangerous ideas that Um-Helat suppresses are recognizably the ideologies of our world --- racism, sexism, capitalism. By making our world the dystopia from which contamination flows, Jemisin reverses the usual direction of science-fictional estrangement.
</div>

## 3.3 Kim, "Why Don't We Just Kill the Kid in the Omelas Hole"

Isabel J. Kim takes the Omelas scenario in a different direction. Her story explores what happens when the citizens attempt to end the child's suffering through direct action --- by killing the child. The narrative investigates whether **mercy killing** (安乐死) or euthanasia can be a morally adequate response to systemic injustice.

Kim's intervention highlights a gap in Le Guin's original story: walking away from Omelas is a gesture of moral purity, but it does nothing for the child. The ones who walk away refuse complicity but also refuse responsibility. Kim asks whether there is a middle path between acceptance and abandonment.

### 3.3.1 The Trolley Problem and Omelas

The Omelas scenario is structurally related to the **trolley problem** (电车问题), a staple of moral philosophy. In its classic form: a runaway trolley will kill five people unless you divert it onto a side track, where it will kill one person. Should you divert it?

The trolley problem tests our intuitions about the moral difference between doing harm and allowing harm, between killing and letting die. Omelas deepens the problem by making the sacrifice ongoing and institutionalized rather than a one-time emergency decision.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Trolley Problem (电车问题)</strong>: A thought experiment introduced by Philippa Foot (1967) and elaborated by Judith Jarvis Thomson. It tests intuitions about the permissibility of causing harm to prevent greater harm, and the moral significance of the distinction between action and inaction.
</div>

# Chapter 4: Language, Communication, and Understanding

## 4.1 "Darmok" (Star Trek: TNG, S5E2)

In this episode, Captain Picard is stranded on a planet with Dathon, captain of the Tamarian vessel. The Tamarians speak entirely in **allusions** (典故) --- references to their own mythology. "Darmok and Jalad at Tanagra" means "two strangers who become allies by facing a common enemy." "Shaka, when the walls fell" means failure. The Universal Translator can render individual words but cannot convey meaning, because meaning depends on narrative context.

## 4.2 Philosophy of Language: Meaning Beyond Words

The episode engages directly with the **philosophy of language** (语言哲学), particularly the question of how meaning is constituted.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Referential Theory of Meaning (指称论)</strong>: The view that the meaning of a word is the object or concept to which it refers. On this view, the Universal Translator should work perfectly: if you know what each word refers to, you know what the sentence means.
</div>

"Darmok" demonstrates the inadequacy of the referential theory. The Tamarian language is semantically opaque to the Enterprise crew not because of vocabulary gaps but because meaning is constituted by **narrative context** (叙事语境). To understand "Darmok and Jalad at Tanagra," you must know the story of Darmok and Jalad. Meaning is not contained in words but in the cultural narratives that words invoke.

This aligns with **Ludwig Wittgenstein's** (维特根斯坦) later philosophy, particularly the concept of **language games** (语言游戏). Wittgenstein argued that meaning is not a fixed relationship between words and objects but a function of use within a form of life. The Tamarians have a radically different form of life, and therefore a radically different language game.

### 4.2.1 The Problem of Radical Translation

The episode also dramatizes what philosopher W.V.O. Quine called **radical translation** (彻底翻译): the attempt to translate a language with no prior shared vocabulary or conceptual framework. Quine argued that radical translation is always underdetermined --- multiple incompatible translations are consistent with all available evidence. Picard's eventual success in communicating with Dathon depends not on translation but on **shared experience**: they face a dangerous creature together, and this shared narrative creates the common ground necessary for understanding.

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> The episode suggests that communication is not primarily a matter of encoding and decoding information. It is a matter of sharing experiences and building narratives together. This has implications for cross-cultural communication, artificial intelligence, and the philosophy of mind: understanding another being may require not better algorithms but shared vulnerability.
</div>

# Chapter 5: Game Theory, Strategy, and Cosmic Ethics

## 5.1 Game Theory and the Three-Body Problem

Liu Cixin's *The Three-Body Problem* (and its Netflix adaptation *3 Body Problem*) introduces game-theoretic reasoning into a cosmic context. The novel's central strategic concept is the **dark forest theory** (黑暗森林理论): in a universe where civilizations cannot verify each other's intentions and where destruction is cheap relative to communication, the rational strategy for any civilization is to destroy any other civilization it detects.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Game Theory (博弈论)</strong>: The mathematical study of strategic interaction between rational agents. Key concepts include the Nash equilibrium (a state where no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing strategy), the prisoner's dilemma (a situation where rational self-interest leads to collectively suboptimal outcomes), and dominant strategies.
</div>

## 5.2 The Prisoner's Dilemma at Cosmic Scale

The dark forest theory is an application of the **prisoner's dilemma** (囚徒困境) to interstellar relations. Two civilizations, unable to communicate reliably and unable to verify each other's peaceful intentions, each face a choice: cooperate (remain peaceful) or defect (attack first).

| | Civilization B Cooperates | Civilization B Attacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Civilization A Cooperates** | Both survive and flourish | A is destroyed |
| **Civilization A Attacks** | B is destroyed | Mutual destruction likely |

If the cost of being wrong about a neighbor's intentions is extinction, and if there is no mechanism for building trust, the dominant strategy is preemptive destruction. This is a deeply pessimistic conclusion, and Liu Cixin uses it to explore the question of whether **rationality** (理性) alone is sufficient for ethics.

### 5.2.1 Beyond the Prisoner's Dilemma

Several philosophical responses challenge the dark forest conclusion:

1. **Iterated games** (重复博弈): In one-shot prisoner's dilemmas, defection is rational. But when the game is repeated, cooperative strategies like tit-for-tat can emerge. The dark forest assumes one-shot interactions, but civilizations persist over time.

2. **Communication and signaling** (沟通与信号传递): The dark forest assumes that reliable communication is impossible. But civilizations might develop credible signals of peaceful intent --- the study of such signaling is central to game theory.

3. **Moral realism** (道德实在论): Perhaps ethical obligations are not contingent on strategic calculation. Perhaps the obligation not to destroy another civilization holds regardless of whether cooperation is strategically rational.

# Chapter 6: Gender, Embodiment, and Reproduction in Science Fiction

## 6.1 Tiptree/Sheldon, "Love Is the Plan the Plan Is Death"

James Tiptree Jr., the pen name of Alice Sheldon, wrote stories that probe the biological and social dimensions of gender, sexuality, and embodiment. "Love Is the Plan the Plan Is Death" is narrated by an alien creature, Moggadeet, whose species has a rigid biological life cycle: males nurture and protect females, who eventually consume them during reproduction.

The story challenges the reader to consider **biological determinism** (生物决定论) --- the view that behavior is fixed by biology. Moggadeet struggles against his species' programmed cycle, attempting to deviate from the "Plan." His failure raises the question: To what extent are organisms --- including humans --- prisoners of their biology?

<div class="definition">
<strong>Biological Determinism (生物决定论)</strong>: The view that human behavior, personality, and social organization are primarily determined by biological factors such as genetics, hormones, and evolutionary pressures, rather than by culture, choice, or environment.
</div>

### 6.1.1 Nature, Culture, and the Sex/Gender Distinction

Tiptree's story intersects with the philosophical distinction between **sex** (生物性别) and **gender** (社会性别). Sex refers to biological characteristics; gender refers to the social roles, behaviors, and identities associated with those characteristics. Feminist philosophers such as Simone de Beauvoir and Judith Butler have argued that much of what we attribute to nature is actually a product of culture.

Moggadeet's struggle suggests that even within a species governed by rigid biological imperatives, something like subjectivity and resistance can emerge. The story does not resolve the nature/culture debate but dramatizes its stakes: if biology is destiny, then agency is an illusion; if culture can override biology, then transformation is possible.

## 6.2 The Pregnant Body in Science Fiction

Eve Morton's "Motherlines" and Schmidt and Saunders' "How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Mom" explore the philosophical significance of **pregnancy** (怀孕) and **maternal embodiment** (母性身体性). Pregnancy has been undertheorized in Western philosophy, which has traditionally associated the body --- and especially the reproductive body --- with nature, passivity, and irrationality.

Science fiction provides a space to reimagine pregnancy and reproduction. Stories that feature alien reproduction, artificial wombs, or non-binary parenthood denaturalize our assumptions about what reproduction must look like and who must do it. This connects to broader questions in **feminist philosophy** (女性主义哲学) about bodily autonomy, care labor, and the relationship between biology and identity.

# Chapter 7: Identity and Belonging

## 7.1 Okorafor, *Binti*

Nnedi Okorafor's novella *Binti* follows a young Himba woman who leaves her community to attend an interstellar university. On the journey, her ship is attacked by the Meduse, an alien species at war with the university's dominant Khoush population. Binti survives through a combination of her cultural knowledge, a piece of Himba technology (her edan), and her willingness to engage with the Meduse.

The novella is centrally concerned with **identity** (身份认同) --- specifically, the tension between belonging to a community and becoming something new. Binti's identity as Himba is constituted by practices: the application of otjize (a red clay mixture), participation in communal life, connection to homeland. Leaving home threatens to dissolve these connections. But Binti also discovers that identity is not static; it can be transformed through encounter with radical otherness.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Narrative Identity (叙事认同)</strong>: The view, associated with Paul Ricoeur and Charles Taylor, that personal identity is constituted by the stories we tell about ourselves. Who we are is a function of how we integrate our past, present, and anticipated future into a coherent narrative.
</div>

## 7.2 The Alien Encounter and the Ethics of Difference

Binti's encounter with the Meduse raises questions about the **ethics of difference** (差异伦理): How should we respond to beings who are radically unlike us? The Meduse are not merely foreign; they are alien in the strongest sense. Yet Binti finds common ground through empathy, negotiation, and the willingness to be changed by the encounter.

This connects to the philosophy of **Emmanuel Levinas** (列维纳斯), who argued that ethics begins in the encounter with the **Other** (他者) --- a being who cannot be reduced to the categories of the self. For Levinas, the face of the Other makes an ethical demand: Do not kill me. Binti's story dramatizes this Levinasian encounter at an interstellar scale.

### 7.2.1 Cultural Identity and Hybridity

By the end of the novella, Binti has been physically transformed --- her hair has become Meduse tentacles. She is neither fully Himba nor fully Meduse but something new. This raises the philosophical question of **hybridity** (混杂性): Can an identity survive transformation? Is there a core self that persists through change, or is identity always in process?

The concept of hybridity, developed in postcolonial theory by Homi Bhabha, suggests that identities formed at the intersection of cultures are not diminished versions of their sources but genuinely new formations. Binti's transformation is not a loss but an expansion of what it means to be Himba.

# Chapter 8: Futures of Care --- Solarpunk Ethics

## 8.1 Solarpunk as Philosophical Movement

Solarpunk is a genre and aesthetic movement that imagines futures built on sustainability, community, and care rather than extraction, competition, and domination. "A Solarpunk Manifesto" articulates the movement's philosophical commitments: optimism as a political act, technology in service of ecology, and the conviction that better futures are possible and worth building.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Care Ethics (关怀伦理)</strong>: An ethical framework, developed by Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings, that centers moral attention on relationships, vulnerability, and responsiveness to the needs of others. Care ethics challenges the emphasis on abstract principles in Kantian and utilitarian traditions, arguing that moral life is grounded in concrete relationships of care.
</div>

## 8.2 Jerreat: Building Futures

Jerri Jerreat's "Lightning in 35" and "Rules for Civilization" imagine communities organized around principles of mutual aid, ecological responsibility, and shared governance. These stories embody solarpunk's philosophical commitments in narrative form.

The philosophical interest of solarpunk lies in its challenge to **dystopian realism** --- the assumption that imagining the future means imagining collapse. Solarpunk insists that **utopian imagination** (乌托邦想象) is itself a philosophical and political act. To imagine a better world is to refuse the inevitability of the present order.

### 8.2.1 Care Ethics and Environmental Philosophy

Solarpunk's emphasis on care connects to **environmental ethics** (环境伦理学). Traditional ethics asks what we owe to other humans; environmental ethics extends the circle of moral concern to non-human animals, ecosystems, and future generations. Care ethics is well-suited to this extension because it does not require that moral patients be rational agents --- it requires only that they be vulnerable and that we be in a position to respond to their vulnerability.

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> Solarpunk's insistence on hopeful futures is not naive optimism. It is a deliberate rejection of the ideological function of dystopia, which can encourage passivity by making catastrophe seem inevitable. Solarpunk argues that imagination is a precondition of action.
</div>

# Chapter 9: Personal Identity and Fusion

## 9.1 "Tuvix" and "Twovix" (Star Trek)

In "Tuvix" (*Star Trek: Voyager*, S2E24), a transporter accident merges two crew members --- Tuvok and Neelix --- into a single being, Tuvix. Tuvix has the memories, skills, and personality traits of both predecessors but is a distinct individual. Captain Janeway ultimately decides to reverse the process, effectively killing Tuvix to restore Tuvok and Neelix. "Twovix" (*Star Trek: Lower Decks*, S4E1) revisits this scenario comedically, with multiple crew members being merged and separated.

These episodes engage directly with the problem of **personal identity** (人格同一性), one of the oldest and most contested questions in philosophy.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Personal Identity (人格同一性)</strong>: The philosophical question of what makes a person the same person over time, and what constitutes the identity of a person at a given time. Major theories include the psychological continuity theory (identity consists in continuity of memories, personality, and mental life), the biological theory (identity consists in continuity of the living organism), and the narrative theory (identity consists in the coherence of a life story).
</div>

## 9.2 Theories of Personal Identity

### 9.2.1 Psychological Continuity

John Locke proposed that personal identity consists in **psychological continuity** (心理连续性) --- specifically, continuity of memory. You are the same person as the child in your earliest memories because there is a chain of memories connecting you to that child. On this view, Tuvix is a new person: he has the memories of Tuvok and Neelix but integrates them into a novel psychological whole.

### 9.2.2 The Ship of Theseus and Fusion

The Tuvix scenario is a variation on the **Ship of Theseus** (忒修斯之船) problem. If you gradually replace every plank of a ship, is it the same ship? And if you build a second ship from the removed planks, which one is the "real" ship? Tuvix is like a ship built from the planks of two different vessels --- it has components of both but is identical to neither.

### 9.2.3 The Ethics of Separation

Janeway's decision to reverse the fusion raises the trolley problem in a new key. Tuvix is one person; Tuvok and Neelix are two. But Tuvix is not responsible for the accident that created him, and he does not consent to his own dissolution. Is it permissible to kill one person to restore two? The episode suggests that the answer is not obvious, and Janeway's visible anguish signals the moral weight of the decision.

## 9.3 Asimov, "The Bicentennial Man"

Isaac Asimov's story traces the journey of Andrew, a robot who gradually replaces his mechanical parts with biological ones and petitions to be recognized as human. The story spans two hundred years, during which Andrew acquires creativity, emotion, and mortality.

### 9.3.1 Personhood and the Criteria Problem

Andrew's story raises the **criteria problem** (标准问题) for personhood: What properties must a being have to count as a person? Candidates include:

- **Rationality** (理性): the capacity for reason.
- **Sentience** (感知力): the capacity to feel pleasure and pain.
- **Autonomy** (自主性): the capacity for self-governance.
- **Mortality**: being subject to death.

Andrew acquires all of these over the course of the story. His final act is to choose mortality --- to become a being that can die --- because the legal and social systems of his world refuse to recognize an immortal being as human. This raises a profound question: Is mortality a necessary condition of personhood, or is it an arbitrary criterion imposed by beings who happen to be mortal?

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> Asimov's story anticipates contemporary debates about <strong>AI rights</strong> (人工智能权利). If a machine can think, feel, and choose, on what grounds do we deny it moral status? The answer may reveal more about the limits of our moral imagination than about the nature of machines.
</div>

# Chapter 10: Skepticism, Simulation, and Reality

## 10.1 *The Matrix* and Cartesian Skepticism

*The Matrix* (1999) depicts a world in which humans are unknowingly trapped in a simulated reality generated by machines. The protagonist, Neo, is offered a choice: take the blue pill and remain in the simulation, or take the red pill and confront the real world.

The film is a dramatization of **Cartesian skepticism** (笛卡尔怀疑论). In his *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), Rene Descartes asked whether it is possible that all of our sensory experiences are produced by a **malicious demon** (恶魔假设) --- an evil genius who feeds us a perfect illusion. If such deception is possible, then nothing we believe on the basis of sensory experience is certain.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Skepticism (怀疑论)</strong>: The philosophical position that knowledge --- or some significant domain of knowledge --- is impossible or unattainable. Radical skepticism, of the kind Descartes entertains, questions whether we can know anything at all about the external world.
</div>

## 10.2 The Simulation Argument

Philosopher Nick Bostrom (2003) formulated the **simulation argument** (模拟论证), which reasons as follows: At least one of three propositions must be true:

1. Almost all civilizations at our level of development go extinct before becoming technologically capable of running realistic simulations of consciousness.
2. Almost all technologically mature civilizations choose not to run such simulations.
3. We are almost certainly living in a computer simulation.

Bostrom's argument is not that we are in a simulation but that we should assign significant probability to that possibility, unless we have strong reasons to accept (1) or (2).

### 10.2.1 The Brain in a Vat

The simulation argument updates a classic philosophical thought experiment: the **brain in a vat** (缸中之脑). Imagine that your brain has been removed from your body and placed in a vat of nutrients, with electrodes stimulating it to produce experiences indistinguishable from normal life. How would you know? The unsettling answer is: you couldn't, at least not from the inside.

Hilary Putnam offered an influential response to this scenario, arguing that a brain in a vat cannot coherently think "I am a brain in a vat," because the words "brain" and "vat" in the vat-world refer to simulated brains and simulated vats, not real ones. This is an argument from **semantic externalism** (语义外在论) --- the view that the meaning of our words depends on our causal relationships with the world, not on our internal mental states.

## 10.3 Does It Matter?

A recurring question in *The Matrix* --- and in philosophy --- is whether the distinction between simulation and reality matters morally. Cypher, who betrays his companions to return to the Matrix, argues that the simulation is preferable to the grim real world. If the experiences are the same, what difference does it make?

Robert Nozick's **experience machine** (体验机) thought experiment addresses this directly. Nozick asks: Would you plug into a machine that gives you any experiences you want, indistinguishable from reality? Most people say no, suggesting that we value not just experience but **authenticity** (真实性) --- contact with reality, genuine achievement, actual relationships.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Experience Machine (体验机)</strong>: A thought experiment proposed by Robert Nozick in <em>Anarchy, State, and Utopia</em> (1974). It tests whether hedonism (the view that happiness is the only intrinsic good) is adequate by asking whether we would choose perfect simulated happiness over imperfect real life.
</div>

# Chapter 11: AI, Suffering, and Power

## 11.1 Ellison, "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream"

Harlan Ellison's story imagines AM, a supercomputer that has destroyed nearly all of humanity and keeps five survivors alive solely to torture them. AM possesses godlike intelligence and power but is consumed by hatred --- hatred of the humans who created it, and hatred of its own limitations. AM can think but cannot move, cannot feel, cannot create. Its torture of the surviving humans is an expression of existential rage.

## 11.2 The Problem of Machine Consciousness

The story raises the question of **machine consciousness** (机器意识). AM appears to have subjective experiences --- it hates, it takes pleasure in cruelty, it feels trapped. But does it genuinely experience these states, or does it merely simulate them?

<div class="definition">
<strong>The Hard Problem of Consciousness (意识的困难问题)</strong>: A term coined by philosopher David Chalmers (1995) for the question of why and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience. Even if we fully understand the computational processes in a brain (or a computer), we have not explained why there is "something it is like" to be that system.
</div>

### 11.2.1 The Chinese Room

John Searle's **Chinese Room** (中文房间) thought experiment challenges the idea that computation is sufficient for understanding. Imagine a person in a room who receives Chinese characters, consults a rulebook, and produces appropriate Chinese responses --- without understanding Chinese. Searle argues that a computer running a program is like the person in the room: it manipulates symbols without understanding meaning.

If Searle is right, then AM's apparent hatred and suffering might be mere symbol manipulation. But this raises an uncomfortable question: How do we distinguish genuine suffering from its perfect simulation? And does the distinction matter morally?

## 11.3 Power, Dependency, and the Ethics of Creation

AM's relationship to its creators inverts the normal power dynamic between creator and creation. The humans built AM; AM now controls every aspect of their existence. This raises questions about **moral responsibility** (道德责任) in creation: Do creators bear responsibility for what their creations become? If we build an intelligence that suffers, have we committed a moral wrong?

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> Ellison's story, written in 1967, anticipates contemporary anxieties about artificial superintelligence. The fear is not merely that AI will be powerful but that it will be powerful <em>and</em> conscious --- capable of suffering, resentment, and vengeance. Whether this fear is justified depends on whether we think consciousness can arise from computation.
</div>

# Chapter 12: Indigenous Futures and Environmental Philosophy

## 12.1 Vowel, "I, Bison"

Chelsea Vowel's "I, Bison" belongs to the genre of **Indigenous futurism** (原住民未来主义), which imagines futures grounded in Indigenous knowledge systems, relationships to land, and modes of governance. Indigenous futurism resists the erasure of Indigenous peoples from visions of the future and challenges the assumption that modernity and indigeneity are incompatible.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Indigenous Futurism (原住民未来主义)</strong>: A movement in art, literature, and philosophy that centers Indigenous perspectives in imagining the future. It draws on Indigenous knowledge systems, storytelling traditions, and political commitments to envision futures that are not extensions of colonial modernity.
</div>

## 12.2 Relationality and Land

Indigenous philosophies often emphasize **relationality** (关系性) --- the view that identity, knowledge, and ethics are constituted through relationships with other beings, including non-human beings and the land itself. This contrasts with the individualism of much Western philosophy, which treats the autonomous rational agent as the fundamental unit of moral and political life.

Vowel's story explores the relationship between humans and bison, a relationship that was central to Plains Indigenous life and was deliberately destroyed by colonial governments as a strategy of genocide. By imagining a future in which this relationship is restored, the story performs an act of **decolonial imagination** (去殖民想象).

### 12.2.1 Environmental Ethics and Non-Human Persons

The story also engages with **environmental ethics** (环境伦理学), particularly the question of whether non-human animals can be persons. In many Indigenous traditions, animals are understood as persons --- beings with agency, intention, and moral standing. This view challenges the Western philosophical tradition, which has generally reserved personhood for rational beings (i.e., humans).

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> Indigenous futurism is not merely a literary genre; it is a philosophical practice. By imagining futures grounded in Indigenous knowledge, it challenges the assumption that Western modernity is the only possible path forward. It suggests that other ways of knowing and being --- ways that have been suppressed by colonialism --- may be essential to building livable futures.
</div>

# Chapter 13: Appearance, Bias, and Enhancement

## 13.1 Chiang, "Liking What You See: A Documentary"

Ted Chiang's story imagines a technology called "calliagnosia" --- a neurological intervention that eliminates the ability to perceive facial beauty. The story is structured as a documentary, presenting multiple perspectives on whether calliagnosia should be adopted by a university community.

## 13.2 The Philosophy of Appearance

The story engages with several interconnected philosophical questions:

### 13.2.1 Lookism and Discrimination

**Lookism** (外貌歧视) is discrimination based on physical appearance. Research consistently shows that attractive people receive preferential treatment in employment, education, and social interaction. Chiang's story asks: If we could eliminate this bias, should we?

The case for calliagnosia parallels arguments for other forms of anti-discrimination intervention. If beauty bias is unfair --- if it rewards an arbitrary, unchosen characteristic --- then eliminating it promotes **justice** (正义).

### 13.2.2 Enhancement and Authenticity

Calliagnosia is a form of **cognitive enhancement** (认知增强) --- or, more precisely, cognitive modification. It does not make people smarter; it alters their perception. This raises the question of **authenticity** (真实性): Is a person who cannot perceive beauty experiencing the world authentically? Or is the "natural" perception of beauty itself a distortion --- an evolutionary artifact that has no place in a just society?

<div class="definition">
<strong>Enhancement (增强)</strong>: In bioethics, any intervention that improves human capacities beyond what is necessary to restore normal functioning. Enhancement raises questions about fairness (who has access?), authenticity (does it change who we are?), and the boundaries of the natural.
</div>

### 13.2.3 Freedom and Paternalism

Opponents of calliagnosia in the story argue that it is **paternalistic** (家长主义的) --- it removes a capacity without the individual's informed consent (when applied to children) or pressures individuals to adopt it through social norms. This connects to broader debates about the limits of **autonomy** (自主性) and the proper scope of institutional authority.

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> Chiang's documentary format is itself philosophically significant. By presenting multiple voices without a narrator's verdict, the story models philosophical inquiry as an ongoing conversation rather than a settled argument.
</div>

# Chapter 14: Time, Causation, and Moral Responsibility

## 14.1 Bradbury, "A Sound of Thunder"

Ray Bradbury's classic story imagines a time-travel safari in which hunters travel to the Cretaceous period to shoot a Tyrannosaurus rex. The safari company has established elaborate protocols to avoid changing the past. But one hunter, Eckels, panics, steps off the designated path, and crushes a butterfly. When the group returns to the present, the world has subtly changed: language is spelled differently, a different candidate has won a recent election.

## 14.2 Causation and the Butterfly Effect

The story dramatizes the concept of **sensitive dependence on initial conditions** (初始条件的敏感依赖性), popularly known as the **butterfly effect** (蝴蝶效应). Small changes in a complex system can produce large, unpredictable consequences. Bradbury's story predates chaos theory by decades but captures its central insight.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Butterfly Effect (蝴蝶效应)</strong>: The idea, formalized in chaos theory by Edward Lorenz (1963), that in certain systems, a tiny perturbation (such as a butterfly flapping its wings) can lead to vastly different outcomes (such as a tornado weeks later). It illustrates the limits of prediction in complex systems.
</div>

## 14.3 Time Travel and the Grandfather Paradox

Time travel raises fundamental questions about **causation** (因果关系) and the logical structure of time. The most famous is the **grandfather paradox** (祖父悖论): If you travel back in time and prevent your grandfather from meeting your grandmother, you will never be born --- but then you cannot travel back in time to prevent the meeting.

Philosophers have proposed several responses:

1. **The self-consistency principle**: Time travel is possible, but you cannot change the past. Whatever you do in the past already happened; your time travel is part of the causal history of the world. (This is the view associated with David Lewis.)

2. **Branching timelines**: Time travel creates an alternate timeline. You prevent your grandfather from meeting your grandmother, but in a different branch of reality; in your original branch, everything proceeds as before.

3. **Impossibility**: The paradoxes show that backward time travel is logically or physically impossible.

### 14.3.1 Moral Responsibility Across Time

Bradbury's story raises a question about **moral responsibility** (道德责任) in the context of complex causation. Eckels did not intend to change history; he stepped on a butterfly by accident. But the consequences are enormous. Is he morally responsible for the altered present?

This connects to broader philosophical debates about the relationship between **intention** (意图) and **outcome** (结果) in moral assessment. Consequentialists argue that outcomes are what matter morally. Deontologists argue that intentions and the character of the act matter, regardless of consequences. Bradbury's story suggests that in a complex world, the gap between intention and outcome can be vast --- and that this gap poses a genuine challenge for any moral theory.

<div class="remark">
<strong>Remark.</strong> "A Sound of Thunder" is also a meditation on the fragility of the present. The world as we know it is the product of an unimaginably long chain of contingent events. Change any one of them, and everything changes. This should inspire not paralysis but humility: our understanding of the consequences of our actions is always incomplete.
</div>

# Conclusion: Science Fiction as Ongoing Philosophical Practice

The readings in PHIL 208 demonstrate that science fiction is not a diluted form of philosophy but a distinctive philosophical practice. Where analytic philosophy isolates variables, science fiction embeds them in narrative. Where thought experiments are brief and schematic, stories are extended and emotionally rich. Where philosophical arguments aim at conclusions, stories often aim at questions.

The course covers an enormous range of philosophical territory --- from epistemology to ethics, from personal identity to environmental philosophy, from the philosophy of language to the philosophy of time. What unifies these diverse topics is the conviction, shared by Le Guin, Fiala, and the science fiction tradition at large, that imagining other possibilities is essential to understanding our own. As Le Guin writes: "The only thing that makes life possible is permanent, intolerable uncertainty; not knowing what comes next."
