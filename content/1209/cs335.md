## Lecture 1: Option Pricing and the Two-State Tree

### European Options

A **European option** is a financial contract that grants its holder the right — but not the obligation — to buy or sell an underlying asset at a prescribed **strike price** <span>&#92;(K&#92;)</span> at a fixed future time <span>&#92;(T&#92;)</span>, called the **expiry** or **exercise time**.

- A **call option** gives the holder the right to *buy* the asset at <span>&#92;(K&#92;)</span>.
- A **put option** gives the holder the right to *sell* the asset at <span>&#92;(K&#92;)</span>.

Let <span>&#92;(S(t)&#92;)</span> denote the time-varying price of the underlying **risky asset** (e.g., a stock). At expiry <span>&#92;(t = T&#92;)</span>, the **payoff** — i.e., the value of the option — is:

- **Call payoff:** <span>&#92;(\max(S(T) - K,\, 0)&#92;)</span>
- **Put payoff:** <span>&#92;(\max(K - S(T),\, 0)&#92;)</span>

For a call, if <span>&#92;(S(T) > K&#92;)</span> we exercise: buy the asset at <span>&#92;(K&#92;)</span> from the issuer and immediately sell at the market price <span>&#92;(S(T)&#92;)</span>, pocketing the difference. If <span>&#92;(S(T) \le K&#92;)</span>, we simply let the option expire worthless.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

S = np.linspace(0, 150, 500)
K = 100
call_payoff = np.maximum(S - K, 0)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))

axes[1].axvline(K, color='gray', linestyle='--', alpha=0.6)
```

### The Central Problem: Option Pricing

The **central question** of the course is: *What is the fair value of an option at times <span>&#92;(t < T&#92;)</span>?* When an option is initially sold, what should the seller charge?

A naïve approach is the **expected discounted cash flow**: discount the expected payoff back to today. For our two-state example with <span>&#92;(S(0) = 20&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(K = 21&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(T = 3&#92;)</span> months:

- Up: <span>&#92;(S(T) = 22&#92;)</span> with probability <span>&#92;(p = 0.1&#92;)</span>, payoff = 1
- Down: <span>&#92;(S(T) = 18&#92;)</span> with probability <span>&#92;(q = 0.9&#92;)</span>, payoff = 0

Expected payoff = <span>&#92;(0.1 \times 1 + 0.9 \times 0 = 0.1&#92;)</span>. Discounted at <span>&#92;(r = 12\%&#92;)</span> annually:
<span>&#92;[ \text{Naïve price} = e^{-0.12 \times 0.25} \times 0.1 \approx \$0.097. &#92;]</span>

This turns out to be *wrong*. We will show the correct price is **$0.633** using no-arbitrage arguments.

### Arbitrage and the No-Arbitrage Principle

**Definition.** An **arbitrage opportunity** exists if, starting with zero initial wealth, an investor can devise a strategy with zero probability of loss and positive probability of gain.

The key insight is to assemble a **hedging portfolio** <span>&#92;(\Pi = \delta S - V&#92;)</span> consisting of <span>&#92;(\delta&#92;)</span> shares of the underlying stock and a short position in the option. We choose <span>&#92;(\delta&#92;)</span> so that the portfolio has the *same* value in both the up and down states — making it **risk-free**.

Setting <span>&#92;(\Pi_\text{up} = \Pi_\text{down}&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \delta(22) - 1 = \delta(18) - 0 \implies \delta = 0.25. &#92;]</span>

Both cases give <span>&#92;(\Pi = 4.5&#92;)</span> at <span>&#92;(T&#92;)</span>. Since the portfolio is risk-free, by the **No-Arbitrage Principle** it must earn the risk-free rate:

> **No-Arbitrage Principle.** A portfolio that is risk-free must earn the risk-free rate of return.

(If it earned more, you could borrow at the risk-free rate and invest in this portfolio for guaranteed profit — an arbitrage. If it earned less, you could short the portfolio and invest the proceeds at the risk-free rate, again for guaranteed profit. Either case generates arbitrageurs who quickly push the price back to equilibrium.)

Discounting the final portfolio value back to today:
<span>&#92;[ \Pi_\text{today} = e^{-0.12 \times 0.25} \times 4.5 \approx 4.367. &#92;]</span>

Since <span>&#92;(\Pi = \delta S - V&#92;)</span>, we recover:
<span>&#92;[ V = \delta S - \Pi = 0.25 \times 20 - 4.367 = \$0.633. &#92;]</span>

This is the **no-arbitrage value** (or **fair value**) of the option. Notice that this is *independent of the actual probabilities* <span>&#92;(p&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(q&#92;)&#92;</span>! The real-world probabilities do not matter for option pricing under the no-arbitrage framework.

The two-state tree illustrates several deep ideas that will recur throughout the course:
- We model risky assets as following a **stochastic process** — random and unpredictable.
- We use **hedging** (combining the option and stock) to eliminate risk and thereby determine price.

## Lecture 2: Brownian Motion and Random Walks

### Modeling Uncertainty with Brownian Motion

Real stock prices have a full probability distribution of outcomes, not just two. To model this, we use **Brownian motion** (also called a **Wiener process**). Let <span>&#92;(X(t)&#92;)</span> be a random, time-dependent variable. Over a small time period <span>&#92;(dt&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> changes by:
<span>&#92;[ dX = \alpha\, dt + \sigma\, dZ, &#92;]</span>
where:
- <span>&#92;(\alpha\, dt&#92;)</span> is the **drift term** (deterministic);
- <span>&#92;(\sigma&#92;)</span> is the **volatility** (how much randomness there is);
- <span>&#92;(dZ = \phi\sqrt{dt}&#92;)</span> is the **random term**, with <span>&#92;(\phi \sim N(0,1)&#92;)</span> a standard normal random variable.

This is a **stochastic differential equation (SDE)**. There is no single solution — only a *probability density* of outcomes.

### Normal Distributions

A standard normal distribution <span>&#92;(N(0,1)&#92;)</span> has density:
<span>&#92;[ p(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-y^2/2}. &#92;]</span>

For <span>&#92;(y \sim N(\mu, \beta^2)&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ E[y] = \mu, \qquad \text{Var}[y] = \beta^2. &#92;]</span>

For our Brownian motion increment <span>&#92;(\phi \sim N(0,1)&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ E[\phi] = 0, \qquad E[\phi^2] = 1. &#92;]</span>

The mean and variance of <span>&#92;(dX&#92;)</span> follow directly:
<span>&#92;[ E[dX] = \alpha\, dt, \qquad \text{Var}[dX] = \sigma^2\, dt. &#92;]</span>

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))

# Left: Normal distribution
x = np.linspace(-4, 4, 400)

axes[0].plot(x, pdf, 'b', linewidth=2)
```

### The Discrete Lattice (Random Walk)

To build intuition, consider a **discrete random walk** on a lattice. At each time step <span>&#92;(\Delta t&#92;)</span>, the variable moves:
- **Up** by <span>&#92;(\Delta h&#92;)</span> with probability <span>&#92;(p&#92;)</span>;
- **Down** by <span>&#92;(\Delta h&#92;)</span> with probability <span>&#92;(q = 1-p&#92;)</span>.

After <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span> steps, the probability of <span>&#92;(j&#92;)</span> up-moves follows a **binomial distribution**:
<span>&#92;[ P(j \text{ up, } n{-}j \text{ down}) = \binom{n}{j} p^j q^{n-j}. &#92;]</span>

The expectation and variance of the total change are:
<span>&#92;[ E[X_n - X_0] = \frac{t}{\Delta t}(p-q)\Delta h, \qquad \text{Var}[X_n - X_0] = \frac{t}{\Delta t} 4pq(\Delta h)^2. &#92;]</span>

### Limiting Behavior: Connecting Lattice to Brownian Motion

As <span>&#92;(\Delta t \to 0&#92;)</span>, we want the lattice walk to converge to the Brownian motion SDE. For the variance to remain bounded and nonzero, we need <span>&#92;(\Delta h \propto \sqrt{\Delta t}&#92;)</span>. Setting:
<span>&#92;[ \Delta h = \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}, \qquad p - q = \frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}, &#92;]</span>
or equivalently:
<span>&#92;[ p = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(1 + \frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}\right), \qquad q = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}\right), &#92;]</span>
the lattice walk converges in distribution to <span>&#92;(dX = \alpha\, dt + \sigma\, dZ&#92;)</span>. In the limit, <span>&#92;(X(t) - X(0) \sim N(\alpha t,\, \sigma^2 t)&#92;)</span>.

### Non-Differentiability of Brownian Paths

A curious and important property of Brownian motion is that its paths are **non-differentiable** everywhere. As <span>&#92;(\Delta t \to 0&#92;)</span>, the total distance traveled in time <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span> grows without bound:
<span>&#92;[ n \cdot \Delta h = \frac{t}{\Delta t} \cdot \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} = \frac{\sigma t}{\sqrt{\Delta t}} \to \infty. &#92;]</span>

The "velocity" <span>&#92;(\Delta X / \Delta t = \pm \sigma/\sqrt{\Delta t} \to \pm\infty&#92;)</span>. Ordinary calculus derivatives cannot be applied to such paths, which motivates the development of **Ito (stochastic) calculus** in the next lecture.

## Lecture 3: Ito Calculus and Ito's Lemma

### Ito Stochastic Integral

Consider the SDE <span>&#92;(dX = a(X,t)\,dt + b(X,t)\,dZ&#92;)</span>. Integrated over <span>&#92;([0,t]&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ X(t) - X(0) = \int_0^t a(X(s),s)\,ds + \int_0^t b(X(s),s)\,dZ(s). &#92;]</span>

The stochastic integral is defined as the limit of a discrete sum, evaluated at the **left endpoint** of each interval:
<span>&#92;[ \int_0^t b(X(s),s)\,dZ(s) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} b(X(t_j), t_j)\,\Delta Z_j. &#92;]</span>

This is the **Ito convention** (evaluating at the left end). An alternative, the **Stratonovich convention**, uses the midpoint. In finance, we use Ito because we cannot "peek into the future" — we only know current prices, not future ones.

### Key Rule: <span>&#92;(dZ^2 \to dt&#92;)</span>

A fundamental result of Ito calculus is:
<span>&#92;[ dZ^2 = dt \quad \text{as } \Delta t \to 0. &#92;]</span>
Equivalently, <span>&#92;(\left(\int_0^t b\,dZ\right)^2 = \int_0^t b^2\,ds&#92;)</span>. This non-obvious identity is the heart of why stochastic calculus differs from ordinary calculus.

### Big-O Notation

For the Taylor-series derivation to follow, we use **Big-O notation**:
- <span>&#92;(p(x) = O(x^n)&#92;)</span> as <span>&#92;(x \to 0&#92;)</span>: there exist <span>&#92;(C, \delta > 0&#92;)</span> such that <span>&#92;(|p(x)| < C|x^n|&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(|x| < \delta&#92;)</span>.

Since <span>&#92;(dZ = \phi\sqrt{dt} = O(dt^{0.5})&#92;)</span>, and using <span>&#92;(dZ^2 = dt&#92;)</span>, we have:
<span>&#92;[ dX^2 = (a\,dt + b\,dZ)^2 = b^2\,dt + O(dt^{1.5}). &#92;]</span>

### Ito's Lemma

Suppose <span>&#92;(X&#92;)</span> follows <span>&#92;(dX = a(X,t)\,dt + b(X,t)\,dZ&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(F = F(X,t)&#92;)</span> is a smooth function. By Taylor series:
<span>&#92;[ dF = F_t\,dt + F_X\,dX + \tfrac{1}{2}F_{XX}\,dX^2 + \cdots &#92;]</span>

Substituting <span>&#92;(dX^2 = b^2\,dt + O(dt^{1.5})&#92;)</span> and collecting terms:

> **Ito's Lemma.** If <span>&#92;(dX = a\,dt + b\,dZ&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(F = F(X,t)&#92;)</span>, then:
> <span>&#92;[ dF = \left(aF_X + \tfrac{b^2}{2}F_{XX} + F_t\right)dt + bF_X\,dZ. &#92;]</span>

This is the stochastic analogue of the chain rule. The crucial extra term, <span>&#92;(\tfrac{b^2}{2}F_{XX}&#92;)</span>, arises from the <span>&#92;(dZ^2 = dt&#92;)</span> rule and has no counterpart in deterministic calculus.

### Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)

Stock prices are modeled by **geometric Brownian motion**:
<span>&#92;[ dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dZ. &#92;]</span>

Here <span>&#92;(\mu&#92;)</span> is the **drift rate** (expected return) and <span>&#92;(\sigma&#92;)</span> is the **volatility**. The coefficients depend on <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> itself, which is inconvenient. Applying Ito's lemma with <span>&#92;(X = \log S&#92;)</span> (so <span>&#92;(X_S = 1/S&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(X_{SS} = -1/S^2&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(X_t = 0&#92;)</span>):
<span>&#92;[ d(\log S) = \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)dt + \sigma\,dZ. &#92;]</span>

This is a **linear SDE** with *constant* coefficients — much easier to work with. The log price follows ordinary Brownian motion, so:
<span>&#92;[ \log S(T) - \log S(0) \sim N\!\left(\left(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T,\; \sigma^2 T\right). &#92;]</span>

### GBM Lattice

The no-arbitrage lattice for stock prices uses:
<span>&#92;[ S_{j+1}^{n+1} = S_j^n\, e^{+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad S_j^{n+1} = S_j^n\, e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}. &#92;]</span>

Next we will see how to price options backwards through this lattice.

## Lecture 4: Lattice Pricing and Dynamic Programming

### No-Arbitrage Lattice Option Pricing

From the no-arbitrage argument applied to the GBM lattice, the option value at each node satisfies:
<span>&#92;[ V_j^n = e^{-r\Delta t}\!\left(p^* V_{j+1}^{n+1} + q^* V_j^{n+1}\right), &#92;]</span>
where the **risk-neutral probabilities** are:
<span>&#92;[ p^* = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d}, \qquad q^* = 1 - p^*, \qquad u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}. &#92;]</span>

This expression is the **discounted expected value** of the option at the next time step, under the artificial "risk-neutral" probabilities <span>&#92;(p^*, q^*&#92;)</span>. The real-world probabilities of stock up/down moves play no role.

### Algorithm: European Call Option

1. Choose <span>&#92;(N&#92;)</span> steps, <span>&#92;(\Delta t = T/N&#92;)</span>.
2. Compute all stock prices: <span>&#92;(S_j^N = S_0\, e^{(2j-N)\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}&#92;)</span> at expiry.
3. Set terminal option values: <span>&#92;(V_j^N = \max(S_j^N - K, 0)&#92;)</span>.
4. **Backward sweep**: for <span>&#92;(n = N-1, \ldots, 0&#92;)</span>, compute:
<span>&#92;[ V_j^n = e^{-r\Delta t}\bigl(p^* V_{j+1}^{n+1} + (1-p^*) V_j^{n+1}\bigr). &#92;]</span>
5. Output <span>&#92;(V_0^0&#92;)</span> as the current option price.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def lattice_european_call(S0, K, T, r, sigma, N):
    dt = T / N
    u = np.exp(sigma * np.sqrt(dt))
    d = 1 / u
    p_star = (np.exp(r * dt) - d) / (u - d)
    discount = np.exp(-r * dt)

    # Terminal stock prices
    j = np.arange(N + 1)
    S_T = S0 * u**j * d**(N - j)
    V = np.maximum(S_T - K, 0)

    # Backward recursion
    for n in range(N - 1, -1, -1):
        V = discount * (p_star * V[1:n+2] + (1 - p_star) * V[0:n+1])
    return V[0]

# Example: S=100, K=100, T=1yr, r=5%, sigma=20%, N=200
price = lattice_european_call(100, 100, 1.0, 0.05, 0.20, 200)
print(f"European call price: ${price:.4f}")

# Convergence plot
Ns = [10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800]
prices = [lattice_european_call(100, 100, 1.0, 0.05, 0.20, N) for N in Ns]
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
plt.semilogx(Ns, prices, 'b-o', markersize=5)
plt.axhline(prices[-1], color='gray', linestyle='--', alpha=0.5, label='N=800 value')
plt.xlabel('Number of time steps N')
plt.ylabel('Option price ($)')
plt.title('Lattice Convergence: European Call')
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('lattice_convergence.png', dpi=120, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()
```

### American Options: Early Exercise

An **American option** can be exercised at *any* time in <span>&#92;([0,T]&#92;)</span>. At each lattice node we now have two choices: exercise immediately (take the payoff) or continue to hold. We should do whichever maximizes value.

Define the **continuation value**:
<span>&#92;[ V_j^{c,n} = e^{-r\Delta t}\!\left(p^* V_{j+1}^{n+1} + (1-p^*)V_j^{n+1}\right). &#92;]</span>
The American option value at each node is:
<span>&#92;[ V_j^n = \max\!\left(V_j^{c,n},\; \max(S_j^n - K, 0)\right). &#92;]</span>

This is the key modification: simply replace the European recursion with this max. No more complex algorithm is needed. For a put option, replace payoff with <span>&#92;(\max(K - S_j^n, 0)&#92;)</span>.

### Convergence and the Smoothed Payoff

The lattice method has first-order accuracy in time (<span>&#92;(O(\Delta t)&#92;)</span>) in general. However, accuracy degrades near the **kink** in the option payoff at <span>&#92;(S = K&#92;)</span>, because the finite-difference delta approximation is inaccurate across a non-smooth point.

A fix is to **smooth the payoff** by replacing each terminal node value with its integral-average over a neighborhood of size <span>&#92;(2\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ \tilde{g}(X_j^N) = \frac{1}{2\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} \int_{X_j^N - \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}^{X_j^N + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}} g(x)\,dx, &#92;]</span>
where <span>&#92;(g(X) = \max(e^X - K, 0)&#92;)</span> for a call (and <span>&#92;(X = \log S&#92;)</span>). This restores second-order convergence.

### Dynamic Programming: The Marriage Problem

The backward recursion underlying American option pricing is an example of **dynamic programming**, which exploits the **principle of optimality**: any sub-path of an optimal trajectory is itself optimal for the corresponding sub-problem.

To illustrate, consider the "marriage problem": you meet <span>&#92;(N&#92;)</span> potential partners sequentially; each has a wealth drawn uniformly from <span>&#92;([0,1]&#92;)</span>. You must decide at each step whether to marry the current person or move on. How do you maximize the expected wealth of your partner?

Working backwards from step <span>&#92;(N&#92;)</span>:
- <span>&#92;(V_N = 0.5&#92;)</span> (expected wealth of the last person, forced to take).
- At step <span>&#92;(k&#92;)</span>: marry if current person's wealth > <span>&#92;(V_{k+1}&#92;)</span>, else continue.

In general:
<span>&#92;[ V_k = p_k \bar{W}_k + (1-p_k)V_{k+1}, &#92;]</span>
where <span>&#92;(p_k = P(W_k > V_{k+1}) = 1 - V_{k+1}&#92;)</span> is the probability of stopping, and <span>&#92;(\bar{W}_k = E[W_k \mid W_k > V_{k+1}] = \tfrac{1}{2}(1+V_{k+1})&#92;)</span> is the expected wealth conditional on stopping. For <span>&#92;(N=10&#92;)</span>, this gives <span>&#92;(V_1 \approx 0.861&#92;)</span>.

## Lecture 5: The Black-Scholes Equation and Floating Point Numbers

### Deriving the Black-Scholes PDE

Under the assumptions:
- Stock follows GBM: <span>&#92;(dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dZ&#92;)</span>;
- Risk-free rate <span>&#92;(r&#92;)</span> is known;
- Short selling allowed, no transaction costs;
- Volatility <span>&#92;(\sigma&#92;)</span> is known.

Construct a hedging portfolio <span>&#92;(\Pi = V - \delta S&#92;)</span>. Applying Ito's lemma to <span>&#92;(dV&#92;)</span> and setting the random term of <span>&#92;(d\Pi&#92;)</span> to zero (to make the portfolio risk-free) requires <span>&#92;(\delta = V_S \equiv \partial V/\partial S&#92;)</span>. Then:
<span>&#92;[ d\Pi = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} + V_t\right)dt. &#92;]</span>

Since <span>&#92;(\Pi&#92;)</span> is now risk-free, <span>&#92;(d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt = r(V - V_S S)\,dt&#92;)</span>. Equating the two:

> **Black-Scholes PDE** (1973):
> <span>&#92;[ V_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} + rS V_S - rV = 0. &#92;]</span>

The drift rate <span>&#92;(\mu&#92;)</span> of the stock does *not* appear — it cancels through the hedging argument. Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published this in 1973; Robert Merton shared the 1997 Nobel Prize (Black died in 1995).

The **option delta** <span>&#92;(\delta = \partial V / \partial S&#92;)</span> is the number of shares to hold in the hedge. It changes continuously as <span>&#92;(S&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span> evolve, which is why we need **continuous rebalancing** (the **dynamic hedge**). The hedge is **self-financing**: given the initial proceeds from selling the option, no further cash injection is required.

### Floating Point Numbers

Computers represent real numbers using **floating point** — a finite approximation. The real number line is infinite both in extent and density; computers can represent neither.

A **floating point system** <span>&#92;(F = (\beta, t, L, U)&#92;)</span> represents numbers of the form:
<span>&#92;[ \pm 0.d_1 d_2 \cdots d_t \times \beta^p, \quad L \le p \le U,\quad d_1 \ne 0, &#92;]</span>
where <span>&#92;(\beta&#92;)</span> is the base, <span>&#92;(t&#92;)</span> the number of significant digits, and <span>&#92;([L, U]&#92;)</span> the exponent range.

Key properties:
- **Overflow**: <span>&#92;(p > U&#92;)</span> → result is <span>&#92;(\pm\infty&#92;)</span>.
- **Underflow**: <span>&#92;(p < L&#92;)</span> → result rounds to 0.
- **NaN**: invalid operations like <span>&#92;(0/0&#92;)</span>.

Unlike fixed-point numbers, floating point numbers are **not evenly spaced** — they are denser near zero and sparser far from zero.

### Machine Epsilon

The **machine epsilon** (or unit round-off) <span>&#92;(\varepsilon_\text{mach}&#92;)</span> is the smallest value such that <span>&#92;(\text{fl}(1 + \varepsilon_\text{mach}) > 1&#92;)</span>. For any real number <span>&#92;(x&#92;)</span>, its floating point representation <span>&#92;(\text{fl}(x)&#92;)</span> satisfies:
<span>&#92;[ \text{fl}(x) = x(1 + \delta), \quad |\delta| \le \varepsilon_\text{mach}. &#92;]</span>

Formulas for <span>&#92;(\varepsilon_\text{mach}&#92;)</span>:
- **Rounding**: <span>&#92;(\varepsilon_\text{mach} = \tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{1-t}&#92;)</span>.
- **Truncation/chopping**: <span>&#92;(\varepsilon_\text{mach} = \beta^{1-t}&#92;)</span>.

For double-precision IEEE 754 (<span>&#92;(\beta=2&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(t=53&#92;)</span>, rounding): <span>&#92;(\varepsilon_\text{mach} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-16}&#92;)</span>.

### Catastrophic Cancellation

The relative error of adding two floating point numbers <span>&#92;(x, y&#92;)</span> is approximately:
<span>&#92;[ E_\text{rel} \approx \frac{|x| + |y|}{|x + y|} \cdot 2\varepsilon_\text{mach}. &#92;]</span>

- Same signs: <span>&#92;(|x+y| \approx |x| + |y|&#92;)</span>, relative error <span>&#92;(\approx 2\varepsilon_\text{mach}&#92;)</span> — small.
- **Opposite signs** (subtraction of nearly equal numbers): <span>&#92;(|x+y| \ll |x| + |y|&#92;)</span>, relative error can be catastrophically large.

This phenomenon is called **catastrophic cancellation** and is a major source of numerical errors.

**Example.** Evaluating <span>&#92;(e^{-5.5}&#92;)</span> using a Taylor series centered at <span>&#92;(a=0&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(x = -5.5&#92;)</span> requires summing many alternating large terms, leading to catastrophic cancellation. Using <span>&#92;(e^{-x} = 1/e^x&#92;)</span> instead avoids this.

**Floating point arithmetic is not associative**: <span>&#92;((a \oplus b) \oplus c \ne a \oplus (b \oplus c)&#92;)</span> in general, because each operation introduces a small rounding error and the order affects how these errors compound.

Notable disasters caused by floating point errors:
- 1982: Vancouver Stock Exchange index miscalculated by factor of 2.
- 1996: Ariane 5 rocket exploded (integer overflow: converting 64-bit float to 16-bit integer).
- 1991: US Patriot missile system failed; cumulative rounding errors caused a 0.34-second timing error.

## Lecture 6: Numerical Stability and Solving Non-Linear Equations

### Conditioning and Stability

Before examining specific algorithms, two key concepts govern the quality of numerical computations:

**Conditioning** is a property of the *mathematical problem* itself. A problem is **well-conditioned** if small changes in input produce small changes in output. It is **ill-conditioned** (high condition number) if small input perturbations cause large output changes. Conditioning is independent of the numerical method.

**Stability** is a property of the *numerical algorithm*. An algorithm is **stable** if it does not amplify rounding errors beyond what the problem's conditioning requires. A stable algorithm applied to a well-conditioned problem gives accurate results; even a stable algorithm applied to an ill-conditioned problem may give large errors (but that is the problem's fault, not the algorithm's).

### The Root-Finding Problem

Many financial computations require solving **non-linear equations**. A canonical example is **implied volatility**: given an observed option price <span>&#92;(P_\text{obs}&#92;)</span> and parameters <span>&#92;((S, T, r)&#92;)</span>, find the volatility <span>&#92;(\sigma_\text{imp}&#92;)</span> such that:
<span>&#92;[ P_\text{obs} - f(S, T, \sigma_\text{imp}, r) = 0, &#92;]</span>
where <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> is the Black-Scholes formula. This is a non-linear equation in <span>&#92;(\sigma_\text{imp}&#92;)</span>.

**General root-finding problem**: given <span>&#92;(f(x)&#92;)</span>, find <span>&#92;(x^*&#92;)</span> such that <span>&#92;(f(x^*) = 0&#92;)</span>.

**Intermediate Value Theorem**: If <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> is continuous on <span>&#92;([a,b]&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0&#92;)</span> (opposite signs), then there exists <span>&#92;(x^* \in (a,b)&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(f(x^*) = 0&#92;)</span>.

### Bisection Method

Given a bracket <span>&#92;([a_0, b_0]&#92;)</span> with <span>&#92;(f(a_0)f(b_0) < 0&#92;)</span>:

1. Set <span>&#92;(x_{k+1} = (a_k + b_k)/2&#92;)</span>.
2. If <span>&#92;(f(a_k) \cdot f(x_{k+1}) < 0&#92;)</span>: new bracket is <span>&#92;([a_k, x_{k+1}]&#92;)</span>.
3. Else: new bracket is <span>&#92;([x_{k+1}, b_k]&#92;)</span>.

After <span>&#92;(n&#92;)</span> steps, the bracket length is <span>&#92;(b_n - a_n = 2^{-n}(b_0 - a_0)&#92;)</span>, and the error satisfies <span>&#92;(|x_n - x^*| < b_n - a_n&#92;)</span>. To achieve tolerance <span>&#92;(\text{tol}&#92;)</span>, we need at most:
<span>&#92;[ n > \log_2\!\left(\frac{b_0 - a_0}{\text{tol}}\right) &#92;]</span>
iterations. **Bisection always works** (provided <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> is continuous and we have a valid bracket), and always gives a guaranteed error bound.

### Fixed Point Iteration

Rewrite <span>&#92;(f(x) = 0&#92;)</span> as <span>&#92;(x = g(x)&#92;)</span>. The iteration is:
<span>&#92;[ x_{k+1} = g(x_k). &#92;]</span>

Multiple choices of <span>&#92;(g&#92;)</span> are possible; not all will converge. For example, <span>&#92;(f(x) = x^2 - x - 2&#92;)</span> (root at <span>&#92;(x^*=2&#92;)</span>) admits:
- <span>&#92;(g(x) = x^2 - 2&#92;)</span>: <span>&#92;(g'(2) = 4 > 1&#92;)</span> — diverges.
- <span>&#92;(g(x) = 1 + 2/x&#92;)</span>: <span>&#92;(g'(2) = -1/2&#92;)</span> — converges.

### Newton's Method

Approximate <span>&#92;(f(x^*)&#92;)</span> by its Taylor series at <span>&#92;(x_k&#92;)</span> and set it to zero:
<span>&#92;[ x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}. &#92;]</span>

Geometrically, this follows the tangent line at <span>&#92;((x_k, f(x_k))&#92;)</span> to where it intersects the <span>&#92;(x&#92;)</span>-axis.

### Secant Method

When <span>&#92;(f'(x)&#92;)</span> is unavailable (e.g., <span>&#92;(f&#92;)</span> is a black-box pricer), approximate the derivative using previous iterates:
<span>&#92;[ f'(x_k) \approx \frac{f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})}{x_k - x_{k-1}}, &#92;]</span>
giving the **secant iteration**:
<span>&#92;[ x_{k+1} = x_k - f(x_k)\,\frac{x_k - x_{k-1}}{f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})}. &#92;]</span>
Requires two starting values; converges slower than Newton but faster than bisection.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Visualize Newton's method for f(x) = x^2 - x - 2
def f(x):  return x**2 - x - 2
def df(x): return 2*x - 1

x = np.linspace(-1, 3.5, 400)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 5))
ax.plot(x, f(x), 'b', linewidth=2, label='f(x) = x² - x - 2')
ax.axhline(0, color='k', linewidth=0.8)
ax.axvline(2, color='gray', linestyle=':', alpha=0.6, label='root x*=2')

x0 = 3.2
colors = ['red', 'orange', 'green']
for i in range(3):
    x1 = x0 - f(x0)/df(x0)
    # Draw tangent line
    t = np.linspace(x0 - 0.8, x0 + 0.2, 50)
    ax.plot(t, f(x0) + df(x0)*(t - x0), color=colors[i],
            linewidth=1.2, linestyle='--', alpha=0.8,
            label=f'iter {i+1}: x={x1:.3f}')
    ax.plot(x0, f(x0), 'o', color=colors[i], markersize=6)
    x0 = x1

ax.set_ylim(-4, 8)
ax.set_xlabel('x')
ax.set_ylabel('f(x)')
ax.set_title("Newton's Method: 3 iterations")
ax.legend(fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('newtons_method.png', dpi=120, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()
```

### Stopping Criteria

All iterative root-finders need a stopping rule. Common choices:
1. **Maximum iterations**: prevent infinite loops.
2. **Tolerance on correction**: <span>&#92;(|x_{k+1} - x_k| < \text{tol}&#92;)</span>.
3. **Tolerance on function value**: <span>&#92;(|f(x_k)| < \text{tol}&#92;)</span>.

For bisection, criterion (2) gives a **guaranteed error bound** since <span>&#92;(|x_{k+1} - x_k| = (b_k - a_k)/2&#92;)</span> and we know <span>&#92;(|x_k - x^*| < b_k - a_k&#92;)</span>. This guarantee does *not* hold in general for Newton or secant methods.

## Lecture 7: Convergence Theory for Root-Finding

### Definition of Convergence Order

Let <span>&#92;(e_k = |x_k - x^*|&#92;)</span>. The sequence <span>&#92;(\{x_k\}&#92;)</span> converges to <span>&#92;(x^*&#92;)</span> with **order** <span>&#92;(q&#92;)</span> if:
<span>&#92;[ \lim_{k\to\infty} \frac{e_{k+1}}{(e_k)^q} = \mu = \text{constant} \quad (< 1 \text{ if } q=1). &#92;]</span>

- <span>&#92;(q = 1&#92;)</span>: **linear convergence** — error halves each iteration (roughly).
- <span>&#92;(q = 2&#92;)</span>: **quadratic convergence** — error squares each iteration (very fast).

### Bisection Convergence

Bisection has linear convergence: <span>&#92;(e_{k+1} \le e_k/2&#92;)</span> on average. Each step halves the bracket, so <span>&#92;(|x_k - x^*| < (b_0-a_0)/2^k \to 0&#92;)</span>. However, the actual error oscillates — on any given step, the new iterate may be closer or farther than the previous one even though the bracket has shrunk.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def bisection(f, a, b, tol=1e-12):
    errors = []
    x_star = 2.0  # known root
    while (b - a) > tol:
        c = (a + b) / 2
        errors.append(abs(c - x_star))
        if f(a) * f(c) < 0:
            b = c
        else:
            a = c
    return errors

def f(x): return x**2 - x - 2

errors = bisection(f, 0, 3)
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
plt.semilogy(errors, 'b-o', markersize=3)
plt.xlabel('Iteration k')
plt.ylabel('|xₖ - x*| (log scale)')
plt.title('Bisection Convergence (oscillating, but overall ½ per step)')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('bisection_convergence.png', dpi=120, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()
```

### Fixed Point Convergence Theorem

**Theorem.** Let <span>&#92;(g(x)&#92;)</span> be continuous and map <span>&#92;([a,b]&#92;)</span> into itself. If <span>&#92;(g&#92;)</span> is a **contraction** on <span>&#92;([a,b]&#92;)</span> — i.e., <span>&#92;(\exists\,\lambda \in [0,1)&#92;)</span> such that <span>&#92;(|g(x) - g(y)| \le \lambda|x-y|&#92;)</span> for all <span>&#92;(x,y \in [a,b]&#92;)</span> — then:
1. A unique fixed point <span>&#92;(x^*&#92;)</span> exists in <span>&#92;([a,b]&#92;)</span>.
2. The iteration <span>&#92;(x_{k+1} = g(x_k)&#92;)</span> converges to <span>&#92;(x^*&#92;)</span> for any starting <span>&#92;(x_0 \in [a,b]&#92;)</span>.

The contraction condition is essentially <span>&#92;(|g'(x^*)| < 1&#92;)</span>. The **convergence rate** depends on <span>&#92;(|g'(x^*)|&#92;)</span>:
- <span>&#92;(0 \le g'(x^*) < 1&#92;)</span>: converges from one side.
- <span>&#92;(-1 < g'(x^*) < 0&#92;)</span>: converges in an oscillating spiral.
- <span>&#92;(|g'(x^*)| > 1&#92;)</span>: diverges.

### Newton Convergence (Quadratic)

For Newton's method, assuming <span>&#92;(f'(x^*) \ne 0&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(f''&#92;)</span> bounded, one can show:
<span>&#92;[ e_{k+1} \approx \frac{f''(x^*)}{2f'(x^*)}\,e_k^2. &#92;]</span>
This is **quadratic convergence** — once we are close, the number of correct decimal digits roughly doubles each iteration. Newton is much faster than bisection in practice, but requires:
- An initial guess reasonably close to the root.
- <span>&#92;(f'(x^*)&#92;)</span> not too small (ill-conditioned near a double root).
- Evaluating <span>&#92;(f'(x)&#92;)</span> at each step.

### Secant Convergence

The secant method achieves **superlinear convergence** with order <span>&#92;(q \approx 1.618&#92;)</span> (the golden ratio), between linear and quadratic. It is more expensive per step than fixed point but cheaper than Newton (no derivative needed).

### Summary

| Method | Order | Cost/step | Needs bracket? | Needs <span>&#92;(f'&#92;)</span>? |
|--------|-------|-----------|---------------|---------|
| Bisection | linear (≈0.5) | 1 eval | Yes | No |
| Fixed point | linear | 1 eval | No | No |
| Secant | ≈1.618 | 1 eval | No | No |
| Newton | quadratic (2) | 2 evals | No | Yes |

## Lecture 8: Newton's Method — Convergence Derivation

### Newton Convergence Proof

Let <span>&#92;(e_k = x_k - x^*&#92;)</span> (signed error). By Taylor series at <span>&#92;(x^*&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ f(x_k) = f(x^*) + e_k f'(x^*) + \tfrac{1}{2}e_k^2 f''(x^*) + O(e_k^3) = e_k f'(x^*) + \tfrac{1}{2}e_k^2 f''(x^*) + O(e_k^3). &#92;]</span>
<span>&#92;[ f'(x_k) = f'(x^*) + e_k f''(x^*) + O(e_k^2). &#92;]</span>

The Newton update gives:
<span>&#92;[ e_{k+1} = x_{k+1} - x^* = x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)} - x^* = e_k - \frac{e_k f'(x^*) + \tfrac{1}{2}e_k^2 f''(x^*)}{f'(x^*) + e_k f''(x^*)} + O(e_k^3). &#92;]</span>

Dividing numerator and denominator by <span>&#92;(f'(x^*)&#92;)</span> and using <span>&#92;((1+\epsilon)^{-1} \approx 1 - \epsilon&#92;)</span> for small <span>&#92;(\epsilon&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ e_{k+1} \approx \frac{f''(x^*)}{2 f'(x^*)}\, e_k^2. &#92;]</span>

This confirms quadratic convergence. The constant <span>&#92;(|f''(x^*)| / |2f'(x^*)|&#92;)</span> governs how quickly the quadratic convergence kicks in.

### Richardson Extrapolation

If a numerical scheme has error <span>&#92;(V_{\Delta t} = V_\text{exact} + C\Delta t^p + O(\Delta t^{p+1})&#92;)</span>, we can compute two solutions at step sizes <span>&#92;(\Delta t&#92;)</span> and <span>&#92;(\Delta t/2&#92;)</span> and eliminate the leading error term:
<span>&#92;[ V_\text{extrap} = \frac{2^p V_{\Delta t/2} - V_{\Delta t}}{2^p - 1} = V_\text{exact} + O(\Delta t^{p+1}). &#92;]</span>

Richardson extrapolation is cheap (just two runs) and boosts accuracy by one order. It is widely used to improve the convergence of the lattice option pricer.

## Lecture 9: Monte Carlo Options Pricing

### The Risk-Neutral World

We showed that the Black-Scholes PDE for the no-arbitrage option value <span>&#92;(V&#92;)</span> is:
<span>&#92;[ V_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} + rSV_S - rV = 0. &#92;]</span>
The drift rate <span>&#92;(\mu&#92;)</span> does not appear. Compared to the PDE for the expected (unhedged) option value (which contains <span>&#92;(\mu&#92;)</span>), the equations coincide *exactly* when <span>&#92;(\mu = r&#92;)</span> and the discount rate equals <span>&#92;(r&#92;)</span>.

**Conclusion**: the no-arbitrage option price equals the discounted expected payoff, *provided* we pretend that the asset grows at the risk-free rate:
<span>&#92;[ V_\text{no-arb} = e^{-r(T-t)} E_Q[\text{payoff}], &#92;]</span>
where <span>&#92;(E_Q&#92;)</span> denotes expectation in the **Q-measure** (risk-neutral world), where <span>&#92;(dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dZ&#92;)</span>.

### Monte Carlo Simulation Algorithm

Instead of solving the PDE backward, we can **simulate** stock paths forward under the Q-measure.

**Discretization** (forward Euler):
<span>&#92;[ S_{n+1} = S_n + S_n\!\left(r\,\Delta t + \sigma\phi_n\sqrt{\Delta t}\right), \quad \phi_n \sim N(0,1). &#92;]</span>

**Algorithm**:
1. Simulate <span>&#92;(M&#92;)</span> independent paths from <span>&#92;(t=0&#92;)</span> to <span>&#92;(t=T&#92;)</span>, each of <span>&#92;(N = T/\Delta t&#92;)</span> steps.
2. For each path <span>&#92;(m&#92;)</span>, compute the payoff: <span>&#92;(\text{payoff}_m = \max(S_N^{(m)} - K, 0)&#92;)</span>.
3. Estimate the option value:
<span>&#92;[ V \approx e^{-rT} \cdot \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M \text{payoff}_m. &#92;]</span>

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def monte_carlo_call(S0, K, T, r, sigma, N, M, seed=42):
    np.random.seed(seed)
    dt = T / N
    S = np.full(M, S0, dtype=float)
    for _ in range(N):
        phi = np.random.randn(M)
        S = S * (1 + r*dt + sigma*phi*np.sqrt(dt))
    payoff = np.maximum(S - K, 0)
    V = np.exp(-r*T) * np.mean(payoff)
    std = np.exp(-r*T) * np.std(payoff) / np.sqrt(M)
    return V, std

V, std = monte_carlo_call(100, 100, 1.0, 0.05, 0.20, 252, 100_000)
print(f"MC estimate: ${V:.4f} ± {1.96*std:.4f} (95% CI)")

# Plot sample paths
np.random.seed(0)
S0, r, sigma, T, N = 100, 0.05, 0.20, 1.0, 252
dt = T/N
t = np.linspace(0, T, N+1)
plt.figure(figsize=(9, 4))
for _ in range(20):
    S = [S0]
    for _ in range(N):
        S.append(S[-1] * (1 + r*dt + sigma*np.random.randn()*np.sqrt(dt)))
    plt.plot(t, S, linewidth=0.7, alpha=0.6)
plt.xlabel('t (years)')
plt.ylabel('S(t)')
plt.title('Monte Carlo: 20 risk-neutral stock paths (S₀=100, r=5%, σ=20%)')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('mc_paths.png', dpi=120, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()
```

### Monte Carlo Error Analysis

Two sources of error affect Monte Carlo:

**1. Time-stepping error.** The forward Euler scheme introduces a **weak error** (error in expectations):
<span>&#92;[ |E[f(S(T))] - E[f(S^{\Delta t}(T))]| \le C\,\Delta t. &#92;]</span>
Forward Euler has **weak order 1**. (The weak order concerns accuracy of expectations, not individual path accuracy.)

**2. Sampling error.** Estimating an expectation by averaging <span>&#92;(M&#92;)</span> samples gives:
<span>&#92;[ \text{Error}_\text{sampling} = O(1/\sqrt{M}). &#92;]</span>

Combined:
<span>&#92;[ \text{Error}_\text{MC} = O(\Delta t) + O(1/\sqrt{M}). &#92;]</span>

To balance the two terms: set <span>&#92;(M = C/\Delta t^2&#92;)</span>, so both errors are <span>&#92;(O(\Delta t)&#92;)</span>. Then:
<span>&#92;[ \text{Complexity} \propto N \times M = \frac{1}{\Delta t} \times \frac{C}{\Delta t^2} = \frac{C}{\Delta t^3} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Error}^3}. &#92;]</span>
Reducing the error by 10× requires increasing computation by **1000×**. This is MC's main inefficiency.

### MC Error Confidence Interval

Let <span>&#92;(\hat{\mu}&#92;)</span> be the sample mean and <span>&#92;(w&#92;)</span> the sample standard deviation of the discounted payoffs. The true option value <span>&#92;(V&#92;)</span> satisfies:
<span>&#92;[ \hat{\mu} - \frac{x^* w}{\sqrt{M}} \le V \le \hat{\mu} + \frac{x^* w}{\sqrt{M}} &#92;]</span>
at confidence level <span>&#92;(1-\alpha&#92;)</span>, where <span>&#92;(x^* = 1.96&#92;)</span> for 95% and <span>&#92;(x^* = 2.58&#92;)</span> for 99%.

### American Options via MC (Nested Simulation)

American options require comparing the early-exercise payoff against the continuation value at each step. For a single exercise time <span>&#92;(t_1&#92;)</span> (a **Bermuda** option):

1. Simulate <span>&#92;(M&#92;)</span> paths from <span>&#92;(0&#92;)</span> to <span>&#92;(t_1&#92;)</span>.
2. For each path ending at <span>&#92;(S_A(t_1)&#92;)</span>, spawn more simulations from <span>&#92;(t_1&#92;)</span> to <span>&#92;(T&#92;)</span> to estimate the continuation value <span>&#92;(e^{-r(T-t_1)}E_Q[\text{payoff}(S(T))]&#92;)</span>.
3. Take <span>&#92;(V(t_1) = \max(\text{early payoff},\, \text{continuation value})&#92;)</span>.

This **nested simulation** (stochastic tree) requires <span>&#92;(M^2&#92;)</span> total paths and becomes exponentially expensive with more exercise dates. This is why the lattice method is preferred for American options.

**Monte Carlo pros and cons:**
- **Pros**: easy to code, naturally handles many assets and complex payoffs.
- **Cons**: slow convergence (<span>&#92;(O(1/\sqrt{M})&#92;)</span>), difficult for American options, hard to compute "Greeks" (hedging sensitivities like delta, gamma).

## Lecture 10: Discrete Hedging

### Continuous vs. Discrete Hedging

In Black-Scholes theory, a **perfectly hedged portfolio** requires **continuous rebalancing** of the delta <span>&#92;(\delta = \partial V / \partial S&#92;)</span>. In practice, we can only rebalance at **discrete intervals** <span>&#92;(\Delta t_\text{hedge}&#92;)</span>. This introduces **hedging error** — the portfolio value is no longer exactly zero.

As <span>&#92;(\Delta t_\text{hedge} \to 0&#92;)</span>, the hedging error vanishes; for finite intervals, there is a residual **Profit & Loss (P&L)**.

### The Delta-Hedge Simulation

Construct the portfolio: short one option, hold <span>&#92;(\alpha = \partial V / \partial S&#92;)</span> shares, with a bank account <span>&#92;(B&#92;)</span>.

**Initial setup** at <span>&#92;(t=0&#92;)</span>:
<span>&#92;[ B_0 = V_0 - \alpha_0 S_0, \qquad \Pi_0 = -V_0 + \alpha_0 S_0 + B_0 = 0. &#92;]</span>

**At each rebalancing date** <span>&#92;(t_i&#92;)</span>:
1. Update bank account with interest: <span>&#92;(B_{i-1} \to B_{i-1} e^{r\Delta t}&#92;)</span>.
2. Recompute delta: <span>&#92;(\alpha_i = \partial V / \partial S\,(S_i, t_i)&#92;)</span>.
3. Update bank (self-financing):
<span>&#92;[ B_i = B_{i-1} e^{r\Delta t} - (\alpha_i - \alpha_{i-1}) S_i. &#92;]</span>
(If <span>&#92;(\alpha_i > \alpha_{i-1}&#92;)</span>: buy more shares, draw from bank. If <span>&#92;(\alpha_i < \alpha_{i-1}&#92;)</span>: sell shares, deposit in bank.)

At expiry, liquidate everything. The **relative P&L** is:
<span>&#92;[ \text{P\&L} = \frac{e^{-rT}\,\Pi(T)}{V_0}. &#92;]</span>
If zero, the hedge was perfect.

### P&L Distribution and Risk Measures

Repeating the simulation many times (under the P-measure, i.e., real-world stock paths) yields a distribution of P&L outcomes. This distribution becomes **narrower** as <span>&#92;(\Delta t_\text{hedge}&#92;)</span> decreases.

**Value at Risk (VAR)**: "<span>&#92;(y\%&#92;)</span> of the time, we lose no more than <span>&#92;(x&#92;)</span>." Formally, <span>&#92;(\hat{x}&#92;)</span> is the <span>&#92;(y\%&#92;)</span>-VAR if:
<span>&#92;[ \int_{\hat{x}}^{\infty} p(x)\,dx = \frac{y}{100}, &#92;]</span>
so the area to the right of <span>&#92;(\hat{x}&#92;)</span> equals <span>&#92;(y/100&#92;)</span>. VAR is typically reported as a positive number (the size of the potential loss), with the negative sign implied.

**Conditional VAR (cVAR)**: the expected loss *given* that we are in the worst <span>&#92;((100-y)\%&#92;)</span> of outcomes:
<span>&#92;[ \text{cVAR} = \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\text{VAR}} x\,p(x)\,dx}{\int_{-\infty}^{\text{VAR}} p(x)\,dx}. &#92;]</span>

VAR is widely criticized because it is **not sub-additive**: combining two risky portfolios could give a VAR higher than the sum of their individual VARs, which is counterintuitive. cVAR *is* sub-additive and is preferred in modern risk management:
<span>&#92;[ \text{cVAR}(\Pi_1 + \Pi_2) \le \text{cVAR}(\Pi_1) + \text{cVAR}(\Pi_2). &#92;]</span>

## Lecture 11: Simulating Investment Strategies

### Forward Simulation and Backtesting

**Forward simulation**: given a stochastic model of asset prices (under the P-measure), generate many future scenarios and study the statistics of a strategy's return (mean, stdDev, VAR, cVAR).

**Backtesting**: evaluate how a strategy would have performed on *historical* data. Backtesting is misleading because the single observed historical path is most likely one where nothing catastrophic happened — it is structurally biased toward benign outcomes and ignores tail risk. Forward simulation is more intellectually honest.

### Measuring Investment Return

A natural measure of investment performance is the **log return** (continuously compounded return):
<span>&#92;[ R = \log\!\left(\frac{\Pi(T)}{\Pi(0)}\right). &#92;]</span>

For a sequence of portfolio multipliers <span>&#92;(X_i = \Pi_{i+1}/\Pi_i&#92;)</span> (i.i.d.), the expected average compound return is:
<span>&#92;[ E[A_\text{avg}] = E[\log X]. &#92;]</span>

### Volatility Pumping

Consider two assets:
- Asset **a**: doubles with prob 1/2, halves with prob 1/2 → <span>&#92;(E[\log X_a] = \tfrac{1}{2}\log 2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\log\tfrac{1}{2} = 0&#92;)</span>.
- Asset **b**: always stays the same → <span>&#92;(E[\log X_b] = 0&#92;)</span>.

Both have zero expected log return individually. Yet if we **dynamically rebalance** to keep 50% in each:

- Case A (prob 1/2): a doubles → total portfolio multiplier <span>&#92;(X = 3/2&#92;)</span>.
- Case B (prob 1/2): a halves → total portfolio multiplier <span>&#92;(X = 3/4&#92;)</span>.

Expected compound return:
<span>&#92;[ E[A_\text{avg}^\text{rebalanced}] = \tfrac{1}{2}\log\tfrac{3}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\log\tfrac{3}{4} \approx 0.059 > 0. &#92;]</span>

By buying low and selling high at each rebalancing, the **dynamic rebalancing strategy** generates a positive return from two zero-return assets! This is called **volatility pumping**. Unfortunately, the effect is weaker in realistic GBM scenarios.

### Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance (CPPI)

**CPPI** (proposed by Fischer Black) provides downside protection while allowing upside participation. Define:
- <span>&#92;(F_i&#92;)</span>: **floor** — a minimum acceptable portfolio value.
- <span>&#92;(M_i&#92;)</span>: **multiplier** — leverage factor (often > 1).
- **Cushion**: <span>&#92;(C_i = \max(0, \Pi_i^- - F_i)&#92;)</span> — how far above the floor the portfolio currently sits.

The CPPI strategy: at each rebalancing date, invest <span>&#92;(M \times \text{cushion}&#92;)</span> in the risky asset:
<span>&#92;[ \alpha_{i+1} = M_{i+1} \cdot \frac{\max(0,\,\Pi_{i+1}^- - F_{i+1})}{S_{i+1}}, &#92;]</span>
<span>&#92;[ B_{i+1} = B_i e^{r\Delta t} - (\alpha_{i+1} - \alpha_i) S_{i+1}. &#92;]</span>

**Behavior**:
- When <span>&#92;(\Pi \to F&#92;)</span> (portfolio approaches floor): cushion → 0, risky investment → 0, portfolio shifts entirely to risk-free assets for protection.
- When <span>&#92;(\Pi \gg F&#92;)</span>: large cushion → leverage invested in risky asset for growth.
- With <span>&#92;(M > 1&#92;)</span>: **leverage** — borrow to amplify gains.
- With <span>&#92;(F=0&#92;)</span>, <span>&#92;(M=1/2&#92;)</span>: reduces to the 50/50 rebalancing strategy (volatility pumping).

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def simulate_cppi(Pi0, F0, M, r, mu, sigma, T, N, n_paths=200, seed=42):
    np.random.seed(seed)
    dt = T / N
    returns = []

    for _ in range(n_paths):
        S = 100.0
        B = Pi0 - M * max(0, Pi0 - F0)
        alpha = M * max(0, Pi0 - F0) / S

        for i in range(N):
            # Simulate stock
            phi = np.random.randn()
            S_new = S * np.exp((mu - 0.5*sigma**2)*dt + sigma*phi*np.sqrt(dt))

            # Portfolio value before rebalance
            Pi_minus = B * np.exp(r*dt) + alpha * S_new

            # New floor (growing at risk-free rate for simplicity)
            F_new = F0 * np.exp(r * (i+1)*dt)

            # Rebalance
            cushion = max(0, Pi_minus - F_new)
            alpha_new = M * cushion / S_new
            B_new = Pi_minus - alpha_new * S_new

            S, B, alpha = S_new, B_new, alpha_new

        Pi_T = B + alpha * S
        returns.append(np.log(Pi_T / Pi0))

    return np.array(returns)

# Simulate CPPI with M=4, F=80% of initial wealth
log_rets = simulate_cppi(Pi0=100, F0=80, M=4, r=0.03, mu=0.08, sigma=0.20, T=1, N=252)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.hist(log_rets, bins=50, density=True, color='steelblue', alpha=0.75, edgecolor='none')
var_5  = np.percentile(log_rets, 5)
cvar_5 = log_rets[log_rets <= var_5].mean()
ax.axvline(var_5,  color='red',    linewidth=1.5, linestyle='--', label=f'95% VAR = {var_5:.3f}')
ax.axvline(cvar_5, color='orange', linewidth=1.5, linestyle='-',  label=f'cVAR = {cvar_5:.3f}')
ax.set_xlabel('Log return R = log(Π(T)/Π(0))')
ax.set_ylabel('Density')
ax.set_title('CPPI Strategy: Return Distribution (M=4, Floor=80%)')
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('cppi_returns.png', dpi=120, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()
```

### Covered Call Writing

**Covered call writing** is a popular income strategy: you own the underlying stock and sell a call option slightly out-of-the-money.

- At <span>&#92;(t=0&#92;)</span>: own stock at <span>&#92;(S_0 = 100&#92;)</span>, sell call at <span>&#92;(K = 105&#92;)</span>, receive premium <span>&#92;(V_0&#92;)</span>.
- **Case A** (<span>&#92;(S(T) > 105&#92;)</span>): stock "called away" at 105; profit = <span>&#92;(5 + V_0&#92;)</span> per share — capped upside.
- **Case B** (<span>&#92;(100 - V_0 \le S(T) \le 105&#92;)</span>): keep stock, positive return.
- **Case C** (<span>&#92;(S(T) < 100 - V_0&#92;)</span>): stock loss partially offset by premium collected, but still a net loss.

The forward-simulated return distribution is highly **left-skewed**: the strategy often earns small gains (cases A and B) but has a long tail of large losses (case C, if the stock crashes). Backtesting will appear to validate this strategy because the most frequently observed historical outcome is a positive one — but the tail risk is ignored.
