Algebraic graph theory is the study of graphs through the lens of algebra — primarily group theory and linear algebra. The central questions are: what symmetries does a graph possess, how can we classify graphs up to these symmetries, and what do the eigenvalues of natural matrix representations reveal about a graph's structure? This course develops the machinery to answer all three.

## Chapter 1: Graphs and Morphisms

### 1.1 Graphs and Isomorphisms

We work primarily with **finite simple graphs**. A graph \(X = (V, E)\) consists of a vertex set \(V\) and an edge set \(E \subseteq \binom{V}{2}\). Important special cases include complete graphs \(K_n\), cycles \(C_n\), bipartite graphs, and the null graph (\(V = \emptyset\)). We will occasionally need multigraphs when the combinatorics demands it, but simplicity is the default.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Isomorphism). An <em>isomorphism</em> between graphs \(X\) and \(Y\) is a bijection \(f : V(X) \to V(Y)\) such that \(uv \in E(X) \iff f(u)f(v) \in E(Y)\). If an isomorphism exists, we write \(X \cong Y\).</div>

Isomorphism is the equivalence relation for graphs: it captures when two graphs have identical combinatorial structure, regardless of labelling. A **subgraph** \(Y\) of \(X\) satisfies \(V(Y) \subseteq V(X)\) and \(E(Y) \subseteq E(X)\). A **spanning subgraph** keeps all vertices; an **induced subgraph** \(X[S]\) on \(S \subseteq V\) keeps all edges with both endpoints in \(S\).

### 1.2 Automorphisms

An **automorphism** is a self-isomorphism — a permutation of the vertices that preserves adjacency. Understanding the automorphism group of a graph is the central object of study for most of this course.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Automorphism). An <em>automorphism</em> of a graph \(X\) is an isomorphism \(f : X \to X\). The set of all automorphisms of \(X\) is denoted \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\) is a subgroup of \(\mathrm{Sym}(V(X))\), the symmetric group on the vertex set.</div>

We adopt the notation \(v^g := g(v)\) for the image of vertex \(v\) under \(g \in \mathrm{Aut}(X)\), and extend this to subgraphs: if \(Y\) is a subgraph of \(X\), then \(Y^g\) has \(V(Y^g) = V(Y)^g\) and \(E(Y^g) = \{u^g v^g : uv \in E(Y)\}\). Crucially, \(Y^g \cong Y\).

![Petersen graph with rotation automorphism arrows](/pics/co444/graph-automorphism.png)

*The Petersen graph, with arrows indicating one automorphism: a cyclic rotation of the outer ring.*

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> For \(g \in \mathrm{Aut}(X)\) and \(v \in V(X)\), \(\deg_X(v) = \deg_{X^g}(v^g)\). Thus automorphisms permute vertices of the same degree.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Let \(Y\) be the subgraph induced by \(N(v) \cup \{v\}\). The result follows because \(Y^g \cong Y\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> For \(g \in \mathrm{Aut}(X)\) and \(u, v \in V(X)\), \(d_X(u,v) = d_{X^g}(u^g, v^g)\). Automorphisms preserve distances.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Using \(g\) and \(g^{-1}\), there is a \(uv\)-path of length \(\ell\) in \(X\) if and only if there is a \(u^g v^g\)-path of length \(\ell\) in \(X^g\). \(\square\)</div>

These two lemmas encode a powerful constraint: automorphisms act as symmetries that are invisible to local graph invariants like degree and distance. Any graph property invariant under relabelling is automatically preserved by automorphisms.

### 1.3 Homomorphisms

A **homomorphism** relaxes the isomorphism condition from a two-way implication to a one-way implication: edges must map to edges, but non-edges may also map to edges. This makes homomorphisms a vastly richer class of maps.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Homomorphism). A <em>homomorphism</em> from \(X\) to \(Y\) is a function \(f : V(X) \to V(Y)\) such that \(xy \in E(X) \implies f(x)f(y) \in E(Y)\). We write \(X \to Y\) if a homomorphism exists.</div>

![Graph homomorphism: triangle maps to single edge](/pics/co444/homomorphism-def.png)

*Left: triangle \(K_3\). Right: single edge \(K_2\). The map \(a \mapsto x, b \mapsto y, c \mapsto x\) is a valid homomorphism, sending a triangle to an edge — a non-injective map.*

Homomorphisms are not required to be injective or surjective, and they need not reflect the edge relation. This flexibility makes them the right notion for graph colouring theory.

<div class="example"><strong>Example.</strong> Given any bipartite graph \(X\) with bipartition \(A \cup B\), the map sending all of \(A\) to one endpoint of \(K_2\) and all of \(B\) to the other is a homomorphism \(X \to K_2\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> The chromatic number \(\chi(X)\) equals the minimum integer \(r\) such that \(X \to K_r\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> A proper \(\chi(X)\)-colouring \(c : V(X) \to [\chi(X)]\) is exactly a homomorphism \(X \to K_{\chi(X)}\), so \(\chi(X) \geq r\). Conversely, any homomorphism \(f : X \to K_r\) gives an \(r\)-colouring by \(v \mapsto f(v)\), so \(\chi(X) \leq r\). \(\square\)</div>

This lemma is fundamental: graph colourings are exactly graph homomorphisms into complete graphs. The minimum such target is the chromatic number. A **retraction** is a special kind of homomorphism: a map \(f : X \to Y\) for a subgraph \(Y \subseteq X\) such that \(f|_Y = \mathrm{id}_Y\). We then call \(Y\) a **retract** of \(X\).

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Retraction). A <em>retraction</em> is a homomorphism \(f : X \to Y\) where \(Y\) is a subgraph of \(X\) and \(f\) restricts to the identity on \(Y\). If such \(f\) exists, \(Y\) is a <em>retract</em> of \(X\).</div>

## Chapter 2: Families of Graphs

This chapter surveys the three principal families of graphs that appear throughout algebraic graph theory: circulant graphs, Johnson graphs, and line graphs. Each arises naturally from algebraic or combinatorial constructions and possesses rich symmetry structure.

### 2.1 Circulant Graphs

The cycle \(C_n\) is the simplest example of a vertex-transitive graph, with automorphism group equal to the dihedral group \(D_{2n}\). Circulant graphs generalise cycles by allowing additional edge connections dictated by an arithmetic structure on \(\mathbb{Z}_n\).

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Circulant Graph). Let \(C \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_n \setminus \{0\}\) be closed under inverses (i.e., \(c \in C \implies -c \in C\)). The <em>circulant graph</em> \(X(\mathbb{Z}_n, C)\) has vertex set \(\mathbb{Z}_n\) and edge set \(E = \{ij : i - j \in C\}\).</div>

![Circulant graph C_8(1,3)](/pics/co444/circulant-graph.png)

*The circulant graph \(C_8(1,3)\): an 8-cycle (solid edges) augmented with "chord" connections of jump 3 (dashed red edges). The symmetry group acts by cyclic shifts and the reflection \(i \mapsto -i\).*

The cyclic shift \(g : i \mapsto i+1\) and the reflection \(h : i \mapsto -i\) both belong to \(\mathrm{Aut}(X(\mathbb{Z}_n, C))\). The rotation subgroup \(R = \langle g \rangle\) and the coset \(hR\) are distinct, giving \(|\mathrm{Aut}(X(\mathbb{Z}_n, C))| \geq 2n\).

### 2.2 Johnson Graphs

Johnson graphs arise from set intersections and constitute a fundamental family in algebraic combinatorics.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Johnson Graph). For integers \(v \geq k \geq i \geq 0\), the <em>Johnson graph</em> \(J(v,k,i)\) has vertex set \(\{S \subseteq [v] : |S| = k\}\) and edge set \(\{ST : |S \cap T| = i\}\).</div>

![Johnson graph J(4,2)](/pics/co444/johnson-graph.png)

*The Johnson graph \(J(4,2)\): vertices are 2-element subsets of \(\{1,2,3,4\}\), and two vertices are adjacent when their intersection has exactly 1 element. This is the octahedron graph.*

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> \(J(v,k,i)\) is \(d\)-regular for \(d = \binom{k}{i}\binom{v-k}{k-i}\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Fix a vertex \(S\). Choose a subset \(S' \subseteq S\) of size \(i\): there are \(\binom{k}{i}\) such choices. For each, there are \(\binom{v-k}{k-i}\) sets \(T\) with \(S \cap T = S'\). The product counts all neighbours of \(S\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> \(J(v,k,i) \cong J(v, v-k, v-2k+i)\).</div>

The isomorphism is given by complementation: \(S \mapsto \bar{S} = [v] \setminus S\). An important special case: the **Kneser graphs** \(J(v,k,0)\). In particular, \(J(5,2,0)\) is the **Petersen graph**, one of the most important graphs in combinatorics.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> \(\mathrm{Aut}(J(v,k,i))\) contains a subgroup isomorphic to \(\mathrm{Sym}(v)\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> For \(g \in \mathrm{Sym}(v)\), the map \(\sigma_g : S \mapsto S^g\) is an automorphism of \(J(v,k,i)\), since \(|S \cap T| = |S^g \cap T^g|\). The family \(\{\sigma_g : g \in \mathrm{Sym}(v)\}\) is a subgroup isomorphic to \(\mathrm{Sym}(v)\). \(\square\)</div>

Typically \(\mathrm{Aut}(J(v,k,i)) \cong \mathrm{Sym}(v)\), but there are exceptions.

### 2.3 Line Graphs

The **line graph** construction replaces edges by vertices and adjacency by shared endpoints.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Line Graph). The <em>line graph</em> \(L(X)\) of \(X\) has vertex set \(V(L(X)) = E(X)\) and edge set \(E(L(X)) = \{ef : e, f \text{ share an endpoint in } X\}\).</div>

![Graph G and its line graph L(G)](/pics/co444/line-graph.png)

*Left: a triangle with a pendant edge. Right: the corresponding line graph. Each edge of \(G\) becomes a vertex in \(L(G)\), with adjacencies reflecting shared endpoints.*

If \(X \cong Y\) then \(L(X) \cong L(Y)\), but the converse fails: \(L(C_3) \cong L(K_{1,3})\) yet \(C_3 \not\cong K_{1,3}\). The converse does hold when both graphs have minimum degree at least 4. Not every graph is a line graph: for instance, \(K_{1,3}\) is not, since any preimage would need an edge incident to three others at a common endpoint, leading to a contradiction.

Line graphs appear prominently in spectral theory: if \(B = B(X)\) is the incidence matrix, then \(B^T B = 2I + A(L(X))\), and the positive semidefiniteness of \(B^T B\) immediately implies \(\lambda_{\min}(L(X)) \geq -2\).

## Chapter 3: Group Actions on Graphs

Group actions provide the algebraic framework to study graph symmetry systematically. Instead of studying automorphisms of a specific graph in isolation, we study the action of abstract groups on sets and graphs.

### 3.1 Group Actions

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Group Action). Let \(G\) be a group and \(V\) a set. A <em>(left) action</em> of \(G\) on \(V\) is a homomorphism \(f : G \to \mathrm{Sym}(V)\). If \(f\) is injective (i.e., \(\ker f = \{1\}\)), the action is <em>faithful</em>.</div>

We write \(v^g\) for \(f(g)(v)\), the image of \(v \in V\) under \(g \in G\). For \(S \subseteq V\), \(S^g = \{v^g : v \in S\}\). A subset \(S\) is **\(G\)-invariant** if \(S^g = S\) for all \(g \in G\).

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Orbit and Stabilizer). The <em>orbit</em> of \(x \in V\) under \(G\) is \(x^G = \{x^g : g \in G\}\). The <em>stabilizer</em> of \(x\) is \(G_x = \{g \in G : x^g = x\}\).</div>

![Group action: orbits on a 6-element set](/pics/co444/group-action-orbit.png)

*Three orbits of a group action on \(V = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\). The group permutes elements within each coloured oval but never moves elements between ovals.*

The orbits partition \(V\) into disjoint \(G\)-invariant subsets. The stabilizer \(G_x\) is always a subgroup of \(G\).

### 3.2 The Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem

The Orbit-Stabilizer theorem is the cornerstone of enumeration in group theory. It relates the size of a group to the size of its orbits and stabilizers.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> Let \(G\) act on \(V\) and \(x \in V\). For any \(y \in x^G\), the set \(\{h \in G : x^h = y\}\) is a left coset of \(G_x\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma</strong> (Orbit-Stabilizer). Let \(G\) act on \(V\) and \(x \in V\). Then \[|G_x| \cdot |x^G| = |G|.\]</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Define \(f : x^G \to \{\text{left cosets of } G_x\}\) by \(f(y) = \{g \in G : x^g = y\}\). By the previous lemma, \(f\) is a bijection, so \(|x^G| = [G : G_x] = |G|/|G_x|\). \(\square\)</div>

![Orbit-Stabilizer theorem illustrated](/pics/co444/orbit-stabilizer.png)

*The Orbit-Stabilizer equation \(|G| = |G_x| \cdot |x^G|\): the group splits into cosets of the stabilizer, each coset corresponding to exactly one element of the orbit.*

A key consequence: for any \(g \in G\) and \(y = x^g\), the stabilizers \(G_x\) and \(G_y\) are conjugate: \(G_y = g G_x g^{-1}\). This follows from the computation \(y^{ghg^{-1}} = x^{ghg^{-1}} = \ldots = y\), showing conjugation by \(g\) maps \(G_x\) to \(G_y\).

### 3.3 Burnside's Lemma

Burnside's lemma counts the number of orbits of a group action by averaging the number of fixed points.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma</strong> (Burnside). Let \(G\) act on \(V\). Write \(\mathrm{fix}(g) = \{v \in V : v^g = v\}\). Then the number of orbits of \(G\) is \[\frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} |\mathrm{fix}(g)|.\]</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Let \(\Lambda = \{(g,x) : x \in \mathrm{fix}(g)\}\). Double-counting \(\Lambda\): summing over \(g\) gives \(\sum_g |\mathrm{fix}(g)|\). Summing over \(x\) gives \(\sum_x |G_x|\). By the Orbit-Stabilizer theorem, \(|G_x| = |G|/|x^G|\), so \(\sum_x |G_x| = |G| \cdot \#\text{orbits}\). \(\square\)</div>

![Burnside's lemma: 4 distinct 2-colorings of C₄ up to rotation](/pics/co444/burnside-example.png)

*Four rotationally distinct 2-colourings of \(C_4\). Burnside's lemma computes: \((16+0+4+0+4+0+16+0+\ldots)/8 = 6\) for necklaces, or \(4\) if only rotations are allowed.*

**Application to counting graph isomorphism classes.** Let \(G = \mathrm{Sym}(n)\) act on \(\mathcal{G}_n\) (all graphs on \([n]\)) by \(X \mapsto X^g\). The orbits are exactly the isomorphism classes. Burnside gives:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> The number of isomorphism classes of graphs on \([n]\) satisfies \[|H| = (1+o(1)) \frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}{n!}.\]</div>

The key insight is that the identity permutation dominates the Burnside sum, contributing \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\), while all other permutations contribute a negligible \(o(1)\) fraction.

### 3.4 Asymmetric Graphs

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Asymmetric). A graph \(X\) is <em>asymmetric</em> if \(\mathrm{Aut}(X) = \{1\}\) is trivial.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Asymptotically, almost all graphs are asymmetric: if \(X\) is chosen uniformly at random from \(\mathcal{G}_n\), then \(\Pr[X \text{ is asymmetric}] \to 1\) as \(n \to \infty\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> Among isomorphism classes in \(H\), let \(\rho\) be the proportion corresponding to asymmetric graphs. For an asymmetric class \(C\), \(|C| = n!\). For a non-asymmetric class, \(|C| \leq n!/2\). Using the formula for \(|H|\) and summing over classes:
\[2^{\binom{n}{2}} \leq \rho|H| \cdot n! + (1-\rho)|H| \cdot \frac{n!}{2} = (1+o(1))2^{\binom{n}{2}} \cdot \frac{1+\rho}{2},\]
which forces \(\rho \to 1\). The proportion of asymmetric graphs in \(\mathcal{G}_n\) is then \(n! \cdot \rho|H| / 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \to 1\). \(\square\)</div>

### 3.5 Primitivity

When a group acts transitively on a set, it may or may not respect additional structure. This leads to the notion of primitive versus imprimitive actions.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Block of Imprimitivity). Let \(G\) act transitively on \(V\) and \(S \subseteq V\). Then \(S\) is a <em>block of imprimitivity</em> if for all \(g \in G\), either \(S^g = S\) or \(S^g \cap S = \emptyset\).</div>

The trivial blocks are singletons \(\{v\}\) and all of \(V\). A transitive group is **primitive** if it has no non-trivial blocks.

<div class="example"><strong>Example.</strong> The cube \(Q_3\) has antipodal pairs as blocks of imprimitivity (automorphisms preserve distances, so opposite vertices always map to opposite vertices). The cycle \(C_n\) for composite \(n = xy\) has blocks \(\{0, x, 2x, \ldots, (y-1)x\}\).</div>

![Primitive vs imprimitive graph](/pics/co444/primitive-vs-imprimitive.png)

*Left: \(C_5\) is primitive (prime \(n\), no non-trivial block structure). Right: \(C_6\) is imprimitive — the three coloured pairs of antipodal vertices form the blocks of imprimitivity.*

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> Let \(G\) act transitively on \(V\) and fix \(x \in V\). Then \(G\) is primitive if and only if \(G_x\) is a maximal subgroup of \(G\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> We prove both contrapositives.

(\(\Rightarrow\)) Suppose \(B\) is a non-trivial block with \(x \in B\). Let \(G_B = \{g \in G : B^g = B\}\). Then \(G_x < G_B < G\): since \(|B| \geq 2\) there is some \(h\) with \(x^h \neq x\) but \(x^h \in B\), so \(h \in G_B \setminus G_x\). Since \(B \subsetneq V\), transitivity gives \(G_B \neq G\).

(\(\Leftarrow\)) If \(G_x < H < G\), let \(B\) be the orbit of \(x\) under \(H\). Then \(|B| \geq 2\) (since \(H \supsetneq G_x\)) and \(|B| < |V|\) by Orbit-Stabilizer. One checks that \(B\) is a non-trivial block. \(\square\)</div>

## Chapter 4: Transitive and Primitive Graphs

Transitivity conditions on graphs — vertex-transitivity, edge-transitivity, arc-transitivity — measure how "symmetric" a graph is. These properties have deep implications for graph structure, connectivity, and matchings.

### 4.1 Vertex-Transitive Graphs

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Vertex-Transitive). A graph \(X\) is <em>vertex-transitive</em> if \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\) acts transitively on \(V(X)\): for any \(u, v \in V(X)\), there exists \(g \in \mathrm{Aut}(X)\) with \(u^g = v\).</div>

Intuitively, a vertex-transitive graph "looks the same from every vertex." All such graphs are regular.

**The \(k\)-cube \(Q_k\).** The \(k\)-cube has vertex set \(\mathbb{Z}_2^k\) and edges between binary strings differing in exactly one coordinate (Hamming distance 1). The translation maps \(P_v : x \mapsto x + v\) form a subgroup of \(\mathrm{Aut}(Q_k)\) that acts transitively, so \(Q_k\) is vertex-transitive. Moreover, coordinate permutations yield \(|\mathrm{Aut}(Q_k)| \geq 2^k k!\).

### 4.2 Cayley Graphs

Cayley graphs are the canonical examples of vertex-transitive graphs. They arise directly from the multiplication structure of a group.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Cayley Graph). Let \(G\) be a group and \(C \subseteq G \setminus \{1\}\) be inverse-closed. The <em>Cayley graph</em> \(X(G,C)\) has \(V = G\) and \(E = \{gh : g^{-1}h \in C\}\).</div>

The condition \(1 \notin C\) ensures no loops; inverse-closure ensures undirectedness.

![Cayley graph of Z_6 with generators {1,5}](/pics/co444/cayley-graph-z6.png)

*The Cayley graph \(X(\mathbb{Z}_6, \{1,5\}) \cong C_6\): the group \(\mathbb{Z}_6\) with generators \(\pm 1\) yields the 6-cycle. The left-multiplication maps \(\tau_g : x \mapsto gx\) act transitively on vertices.*

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Every Cayley graph \(X(G,C)\) is vertex-transitive.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> For each \(g \in G\), define \(\tau_g : V(X) \to V(X)\) by \(x \mapsto gx\). Then \(\tau_g \in \mathrm{Aut}(X)\) since \(xy \in E \iff y^{-1}x \in C \iff (gy)^{-1}(gx) \in C \iff \tau_g(x)\tau_g(y) \in E\). The set \(\{\tau_g : g \in G\}\) acts transitively since \(\tau_{wv^{-1}}\) maps \(v \mapsto w\) for any \(v, w\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> \(X(G,C)\) is connected if and only if \(C\) generates \(G\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> (\(\Rightarrow\)) Any path from \(e\) to \(g\) gives \(g\) as a product of elements from \(C\). (\(\Leftarrow\)) If \(g = c_1 \cdots c_k\) with \(c_i \in C\), then the sequence \(e, c_1, c_1c_2, \ldots, g\) is a path in \(X(G,C)\). \(\square\)</div>

Not every vertex-transitive graph is a Cayley graph. The **Petersen graph** \(J(5,2,0)\) is vertex-transitive but is not a Cayley graph, since neither \(\mathbb{Z}_{10}\) nor the dihedral group \(D_{10}\) produces a Cayley graph isomorphic to it.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If \(G \leq \mathrm{Aut}(X)\) acts regularly on \(V(X)\), then \(X \cong X(G,C)\) for some inverse-closed \(C \subseteq G \setminus \{1\}\).</div>

This gives a clean characterisation: vertex-transitive graphs are precisely those admitting a regular subgroup in their automorphism group — which is exactly a Cayley graph structure. Furthermore:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Every connected vertex-transitive graph is isomorphic to a retract of a Cayley graph.</div>

### 4.3 Edge-Transitive and Arc-Transitive Graphs

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Edge-Transitive, Arc-Transitive). A graph \(X\) is <em>edge-transitive</em> if \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\) acts transitively on \(E(X)\). It is <em>arc-transitive</em> (or <em>symmetric</em>) if \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\) acts transitively on the arcs \(\{(x,y) : xy \in E(X)\}\).</div>

Arc-transitivity implies both vertex and edge transitivity, but the converses fail. The triangular prism is vertex-transitive but not edge-transitive; \(K_{n,m}\) for \(n \neq m\) is edge-transitive but not vertex-transitive.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If \(X\) is edge-transitive and has no isolated vertices but is not vertex-transitive, then \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\) has exactly two orbits, forming a bipartition of \(X\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If \(X\) is both vertex and edge transitive but not arc-transitive, then every vertex has even degree.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> The arc set splits into two orbits \(\Omega_1 = (x,y)^G\) and \(\Omega_2 = (y,x)^G\). Since \(X\) is not arc-transitive, these are disjoint, and the graph of arcs in \(\Omega_1\) is a regular oriented graph. Each vertex \(u\) satisfies \(\deg_X(u) = d^+_{\Omega_1}(u) + d^-_{\Omega_1}(u) = 2d^+_{\Omega_1}(u) \equiv 0 \pmod 2\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary.</strong> If \(X\) is vertex and edge transitive with odd vertex degrees, then \(X\) is arc-transitive.</div>

### 4.4 Connectivity and Matchings of Vertex-Transitive Graphs

The symmetry of vertex-transitive graphs constrains their connectivity and matching properties in remarkable ways.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Edge Connectivity). The <em>edge connectivity</em> \(\kappa_1(X)\) is the minimum number of edges whose deletion increases the component count. An <em>edge atom</em> is a minimum-size \(S \subseteq V(X)\) with \(|\partial S| = \kappa_1(X)\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> If \(X\) is connected and vertex-transitive, then \(\kappa_1(X)\) equals the degree of each vertex.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Clearly \(\kappa_1(X) \leq k\). Let \(A\) be an edge atom. For any \(g \in \mathrm{Aut}(X)\), \(A^g\) is also an edge atom, so either \(A = A^g\) or \(A \cap A^g = \emptyset\). Thus \(A\) is a block of imprimitivity for \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\), and \(X[A]\) is \(\ell\)-regular for some \(\ell < k\). Computing: \(|\partial A| = |A|(k-\ell) \geq |A|(k+1-|A|)\). Since \(|A| \leq k\), we get \(|\partial A| \geq k\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> Let \(X\) be connected and vertex-transitive. Then \(X\) has a matching missing at most one vertex, and every edge lies in some maximum matching.</div>

This is proved by a careful induction on vertices and edges, using the vertex-transitivity to propagate matching properties from subgraphs back to \(X\).

![Vertex-transitive circulant graph on 12 vertices](/pics/co444/transitive-graph.png)

*The circulant \(C_{12}(1,5)\): a vertex-transitive graph on 12 vertices. Every vertex has degree 4, and the automorphism group acts transitively — every vertex looks identical.*

## Chapter 5: Eigenvalues and the Adjacency Matrix

The second major algebraic tool in graph theory is linear algebra: the adjacency matrix and the spectrum of its eigenvalues encode surprising information about graph structure.

### 5.1 The Adjacency Matrix and Spectrum

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Adjacency Matrix). The <em>adjacency matrix</em> \(A = A(X) \in \{0,1\}^{V \times V}\) has \(A_{u,v} = 1\) if \(u \sim v\) and 0 otherwise. Since \(X\) is simple and undirected, \(A\) is symmetric with zero diagonal.</div>

![Small graph with its adjacency matrix](/pics/co444/adjacency-matrix.png)

*A 5-vertex graph alongside its adjacency matrix. The symmetry \(A = A^T\) reflects the undirectedness of the graph; the zero diagonal reflects the absence of loops.*

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Spectrum). The <em>spectrum</em> of \(X\) is the multiset of eigenvalues of \(A(X)\). The <em>characteristic polynomial</em> is \(\phi(A,x) = \det(xI - A(X))\).</div>

Since \(A(X)\) is real and symmetric, all its eigenvalues are real and it has an orthonormal eigenvector basis. Isomorphic graphs have the same spectrum — but the converse fails (non-isomorphic graphs can be cospectral).

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> \((A^r)_{u,v}\) equals the number of walks from \(u\) to \(v\) of length \(r\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary.</strong> If \(X\) has \(m\) edges and \(t\) triangles and \(A = A(X)\), then \(\mathrm{tr}\, A = 0\), \(\mathrm{tr}\, A^2 = 2m\), and \(\mathrm{tr}\, A^3 = 6t\). In general, \(\mathrm{tr}\, A^r = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i^r\).</div>

**Computing eigenvalues.** If \(f : V(X) \to \mathbb{R}\) satisfies \(\sum_{v \sim u} f(v) = \lambda f(u)\) for all \(u\), then \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A(X)\) with eigenvector \(f\).

<div class="example"><strong>Example</strong> (Regular Graphs). For a \(k\)-regular graph, the constant function \(f \equiv 1\) satisfies \(\sum_{v \sim u} 1 = k = k \cdot f(u)\), so \(k\) is always an eigenvalue. Conversely, \(\mathbf{1}\) is an eigenvector if and only if \(X\) is regular.</div>

<div class="example"><strong>Example</strong> (Cycles). For \(C_n\), let \(\tau = e^{2\pi i j/n}\) be an \(n\)-th root of unity and \(f(u) = \tau^u\). Then \(\sum_{v \sim u} f(v) = \tau^{u-1} + \tau^{u+1} = (\tau^{-1}+\tau)\tau^u\), so \(\lambda_j = 2\cos(2\pi j/n)\) for \(j = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If \(X\) is \(k\)-regular, then \(X\) and its complement \(\bar{X}\) share the same eigenvectors. The eigenvalues of \(\bar{X}\) are \((n-k-1)\) (with eigenvector \(\mathbf{1}\)) and \(-\theta_j - 1\) for each non-principal eigenvalue \(\theta_j\) of \(X\).</div>

### 5.2 Incidence Matrix and the Laplacian

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Incidence Matrix). The <em>incidence matrix</em> \(B = B(X) \in \{0,1\}^{V \times E}\) has \(B_{u,e} = 1\) if \(u \in e\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> If \(X\) has \(n\) vertices and \(c\) bipartite components, then \(\mathrm{rank}\, B = n - c\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> We show \(\mathrm{null}(B^T) = c\). A vector \(z\) with \(B^T z = 0\) satisfies \(z_u + z_v = 0\) for all \(uv \in E\). Hence \(z_u = 0\) on non-bipartite components and takes opposite values on bipartition classes in bipartite components. So the null space has dimension \(c\). \(\square\)</div>

The key matrix identity \(BB^T = D(X) + A(X)\) (where \(D\) is the diagonal degree matrix) and \(B^T B = 2I + A(L(X))\) connect the incidence matrix to both the adjacency matrix and the line graph.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Laplacian). The <em>Laplacian matrix</em> is \(L(X) = D(X) - A(X)\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> \(L(X)\) is positive semidefinite, with \[x^T L(X) x = \sum_{uv \in E} (x_u - x_v)^2 \geq 0.\]</div>

The Laplacian measures the "roughness" of a function on the graph: \(x^T L x\) is large when adjacent vertices have very different \(x\)-values. The smallest eigenvalue of \(L\) is always 0 (with eigenvector \(\mathbf{1}\)), and:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> The second smallest Laplacian eigenvalue \(\mu_2 > 0\) if and only if \(X\) is connected. For \(k\)-regular \(X\), \(\mu_i = k - \lambda_{n+1-i}\) where \(\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n\) are the adjacency eigenvalues.</div>

### 5.3 Symmetric Matrices and the Spectral Theorem

Since \(A(X)\) is real symmetric, the spectral theorem applies in full. The key properties are:

- Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
- All eigenvalues are real.
- \(\mathbb{R}^n\) has an orthonormal eigenvector basis.
- \(A = P D P^T\) for orthogonal \(P\), giving \(A = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i v_i v_i^T\).

### 5.4 The Courant-Fisher Theorem

The Courant-Fisher theorem characterises eigenvalues as solutions to min-max optimisation problems over the Rayleigh quotient.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Rayleigh Quotient). For \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}\) and symmetric \(A\), the <em>Rayleigh quotient</em> is \(R(x,A) = x^T A x / x^T x\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Courant-Fisher). Let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n\). For all \(k \in [n]\): \[\lambda_k = \max_{S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n,\, \dim S = k} \min_{x \in S,\, x \neq 0} \frac{x^T A x}{x^T x}.\]</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Let \(f_1, \ldots, f_n\) be an orthonormal eigenvector basis. For \(S = \mathrm{span}\{f_1,\ldots,f_k\}\), the minimum of the Rayleigh quotient over \(S\) is \(\lambda_k\) (attained at \(f_k\)), giving \(\geq \lambda_k\). For any other \(k\)-dimensional \(S\), intersecting with \(\mathrm{span}\{f_k,\ldots,f_n\}\) (dimension \(n-k+1\)) gives a nonzero vector with Rayleigh quotient \(\leq \lambda_k\), so the minimum is \(\leq \lambda_k\). \(\square\)</div>

### 5.5 The Maximum Eigenvalue

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Let \(\bar{d}\) be the average degree and \(\Delta\) the maximum degree of \(X\). Then \(\bar{d} \leq \lambda_1 \leq \Delta\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> The lower bound follows by evaluating the Rayleigh quotient at \(x = \mathbf{1}\): \(\lambda_1 \geq \mathbf{1}^T A \mathbf{1}/n = 2|E|/n = \bar{d}\). For the upper bound, if \(f\) is the principal eigenvector and \(u\) attains the maximum of \(f\), then \(\lambda_1 f(u) = (Af)(u) = \sum_{v \sim u} f(v) \leq \deg(u) f(u) \leq \Delta f(u)\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Wilf, 1967). The chromatic number satisfies \(\chi(X) \leq \lfloor \lambda_1 \rfloor + 1\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> By induction on \(n\). Since the average degree is \(\leq \lambda_1\), some vertex \(u\) has degree \(\leq \lfloor \lambda_1 \rfloor\). Removing \(u\), the induced subgraph has maximum eigenvalue \(\leq \lambda_1\) (by interlacing), so by induction is \((\lfloor\lambda_1\rfloor+1)\)-colourable. Reintroduce \(u\): it has at most \(\lfloor\lambda_1\rfloor\) neighbours, so one colour remains available. \(\square\)</div>

### 5.6 Eigenvalue Interlacing

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Cauchy Interlacing). Let \(A\) be symmetric \(n \times n\) and \(B\) an \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) principal submatrix. If \(\alpha_1 \geq \cdots \geq \alpha_n\) and \(\beta_1 \geq \cdots \geq \beta_{n-1}\) are their eigenvalues, then \[\alpha_1 \geq \beta_1 \geq \alpha_2 \geq \beta_2 \geq \cdots \geq \alpha_{n-1} \geq \beta_{n-1} \geq \alpha_n.\]</div>

Interlacing shows that removing a vertex from a graph cannot decrease the minimum eigenvalue or increase the maximum eigenvalue. Combined with the positive semidefinite matrix argument, this gives:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If \(Y\) is an induced subgraph of \(X\), then \(\lambda_{\min}(X) \leq \lambda_{\min}(Y) \leq \lambda_{\max}(Y) \leq \lambda_{\max}(X)\).</div>

### 5.7 Perron-Frobenius Theorem

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Perron-Frobenius). Let \(X\) be connected and \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n\) be the eigenvalues of \(A(X)\). Then:
<ol>
<li>\(\lambda_1\) has a strictly positive eigenvector.</li>
<li>\(\lambda_1 > \lambda_2\) (the largest eigenvalue is simple).</li>
<li>\(\lambda_1 \geq -\lambda_n\), with equality if and only if \(X\) is bipartite.</li>
</ol></div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong>
(a) Let \(f\) be a unit eigenvector of \(\lambda_1\) and \(f^+ = |f|\). By the Courant-Fisher theorem, \(\lambda_1 = f^T A f \leq (f^+)^T A f^+ \leq \lambda_1\), forcing \(f^+\) to also be a maximiser — hence an eigenvector of \(\lambda_1\). If \(f^+(u) = 0\) for some \(u\), connectivity yields an edge \(vw\) with \(f^+(v) = 0 < f^+(w)\), contradicting \(\sum_{w \sim v} f^+(w) = \lambda_1 f^+(v) = 0\).

(b) If \(\lambda_2 = \lambda_1\), then any eigenvector \(g\) of \(\lambda_2\) would also have \(|g|\) strictly positive. But \(g \perp f^+\) forces \(g\) to change sign, and connectivity gives an edge where \(g(u)g(v) < 0 \neq |g(u)||g(v)|\), contradicting the equality case.

(c) The inequality \(|\lambda_n| \leq \lambda_1\) follows from the triangle inequality on the Rayleigh quotient. For bipartite \(X\) with bipartition \(S, T\): if \(f\) is an eigenvector of \(\lambda\), define \(\bar{f}(u) = f(u)\) for \(u \in S\) and \(\bar{f}(u) = -f(u)\) for \(u \in T\). Then \(A\bar{f} = -\lambda \bar{f}\), so \(-\lambda\) is an eigenvalue, giving symmetry of the spectrum around 0. The converse is similar. \(\square\)</div>

### 5.8 Strongly Regular Graphs

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Strongly Regular). A graph \(X\) is <em>strongly regular</em> with parameters \((n,k,a,c)\) if: \(|V(X)| = n\), \(X\) is \(k\)-regular, every adjacent pair shares exactly \(a\) common neighbours, and every non-adjacent pair shares exactly \(c\) common neighbours.</div>

<div class="example"><strong>Example.</strong> \(C_5\) is \((5,2,0,1)\)-strongly regular. The Petersen graph is \((10,3,0,1)\)-strongly regular. The line graph of \(K_n\) is \(\bigl(\binom{n}{2}, 2n-4, n-2, 4\bigr)\)-strongly regular.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition</strong> (Parameter Relation). A \((n,k,a,c)\) strongly regular graph satisfies \(c(n-k-1) = k(k-a-1)\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Fix \(u \in V\). Count edges between \(N(u)\) and \(V \setminus (N(u) \cup \{u\})\) in two ways: each non-neighbour contributes \(c\) edges, giving \(c(n-k-1)\); each neighbour has \(k-1-a\) neighbours in \(V \setminus (N(u) \cup \{u\})\), giving \(k(k-1-a)\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Lemma.</strong> For a non-complete strongly regular graph with parameters \((n,k,a,c)\), the adjacency matrix has exactly three distinct eigenvalues: \(k\), and the two roots \(\theta, \tau\) of \(\lambda^2 - (a-c)\lambda - (k-c) = 0\). Their multiplicities are:
\[m_\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[(n-1) - \frac{2k+(n-1)(a-c)}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right], \quad m_\tau = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[(n-1) + \frac{2k+(n-1)(a-c)}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right],\]
where \(\Delta = (a-c)^2 + 4(k-c)\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> The key identity is \(A^2 = kI + aA + c(J - I - A)\), giving \(A^2 - (a-c)A - (k-c)I = cJ\). Any eigenvector \(v\) orthogonal to \(\mathbf{1}\) satisfies \(\lambda^2 - (a-c)\lambda - (k-c) = 0\), so \(\lambda \in \{\theta, \tau\}\). The multiplicities follow from \(m_\theta + m_\tau = n-1\) and \(k + m_\theta \theta + m_\tau \tau = \mathrm{tr}\, A = 0\). \(\square\)</div>

Conversely, a connected regular graph with exactly three distinct eigenvalues is strongly regular. The requirement that \(m_\theta, m_\tau\) be positive integers is a strong integrality constraint on the parameters \((n,k,a,c)\).

### 5.9 Paley Graphs

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Paley Graph). For a prime power \(q \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), the <em>Paley graph</em> \(P(q)\) has vertex set \(\mathrm{GF}(q)\) and edge set \(\{uv : u - v \text{ is a nonzero square in } \mathrm{GF}(q)\}\).</div>

The condition \(q \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) ensures that \(-1\) is a square in \(\mathrm{GF}(q)\), making the edge relation symmetric. Paley graphs are \(\frac{q-1}{2}\)-regular, arc-transitive, self-complementary, and strongly regular with parameters \(\bigl(q, \frac{q-1}{2}, \frac{q-5}{4}, \frac{q-1}{4}\bigr)\).

### 5.10 Expanders and the Second Eigenvalue

The second eigenvalue of the Laplacian, \(\mu_2\), measures how well-connected a graph is beyond mere connectivity. A graph with large \(\mu_2\) is a good **expander**: every small set of vertices has many edges leaving it.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Isoperimetric Ratio). For \(S \subseteq V(X)\), the <em>isoperimetric ratio</em> is \(\theta(S) = e(S, \bar S)/|S|\). The <em>isoperimetric number</em> of \(X\) is \(\theta(X) = \min_{|S| \leq |V|/2} \theta(S)\). \(X\) is an <em>\(\alpha\)-expander</em> if \(\theta(X) \geq \alpha\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> \(\theta(X) \geq \mu_2/2\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> For \(S \subseteq V\) with \(|S| \leq n/2\), set \(\alpha = |S|/n\) and \(x = \chi_S - \alpha \mathbf{1}_n\). Then \(x \perp \mathbf{1}\) and \(\|x\|^2 = \alpha n(1-\alpha)\). By the Courant-Fisher theorem:
\[\mu_2 \leq \frac{x^T L x}{x^T x} = \frac{1}{\alpha n(1-\alpha)} \sum_{uv \in E} (x_u - x_v)^2 = \frac{e(S,\bar{S})}{\alpha n(1-\alpha)} = \frac{\theta(S)}{1-\alpha} \leq 2\theta(S).\]
Taking the minimum over \(S\) gives \(\theta(X) \geq \mu_2/2\). \(\square\)</div>

This inequality — sometimes called the **Cheeger inequality** for graphs — is one of the most important results in spectral graph theory. It says that a graph with a large spectral gap \(\mu_2\) is necessarily a good expander. The study of graph families with simultaneously large degree and large \(\mu_2\) is the theory of **Ramanujan graphs**, an active research area.
