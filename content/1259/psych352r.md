# Chapter 1: What Is Culture, and What Is Cross-Cultural Psychology?

## Section 1.1: Defining Culture

Culture is one of the most contested concepts in the social sciences. Kroeber and Kluckhohn (1952) catalogued over 160 different definitions. For psychological purposes, a working definition:

<div class="definition">
<strong>Culture (psychological definition).</strong> Culture is a shared system of beliefs, values, norms, practices, and artifacts transmitted across generations within a group that shapes the thoughts, emotions, and behaviours of its members.
</div>

Key features of this definition:
- **Shared**: Culture is intersubjective, not merely individual.
- **Transmitted**: Culture is learned, not biologically inherited.
- **Adaptive**: Cultural practices often solve recurring problems of group survival.
- **Dynamic**: Cultures change, merge, and fragment over time.

The culture concept must be distinguished from:
- **Race**: a socially constructed category based on physical appearance, not equivalent to culture.
- **Ethnicity**: a self-identified affiliation with a cultural group, often shared heritage.
- **Nationality**: legal citizenship, which may or may not correspond to cultural identity.

## Section 1.2: Levels of Culture

Culture operates at multiple levels of analysis:

| Level | Description | Examples |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Macro/societal** | National or regional culture | "Canadian culture," "East Asian cultures" |
| **Organizational** | Shared practices within institutions | Corporate culture, academic culture |
| **Micro/subgroup** | Smaller within-society groupings | Generational culture, ethnic subcultures |
| **Individual** | Internalized cultural schemas | Personal cultural identity |

## Section 1.3: Cross-Cultural vs. Cultural Psychology

Two related but distinct fields study culture and psychology:

**Cross-cultural psychology** treats culture as an independent variable and tests whether psychological findings obtained in one culture generalize to others. Its goal is to identify universal vs. culture-specific phenomena. The methodology is comparative.

**Cultural psychology** treats culture as constitutive of mind rather than as context. Shweder's dictum: "culture and psyche make each other up." Cultural psychology is more interpretive and less comparative.

The tension between these perspectives mirrors a broader debate about whether psychology's task is to discover universal laws or to describe the diversity of human experience.

## Section 1.4: The WEIRD Problem

Most psychological research has been conducted on **WEIRD** samples: **W**estern, **E**ducated, **I**ndustrialized, **R**ich, and **D**emocratic populations. Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010) demonstrated that WEIRD populations are statistical outliers on many psychological dimensions rather than universal representatives of humanity.

Examples of phenomena that are not universal:
- The Müller-Lyer illusion (susceptibility varies with ecology)
- Self-serving attribution bias (lower in collectivist cultures)
- Individualistic self-concept (less prominent outside the West)

The WEIRD critique requires psychology to treat its findings as culturally situated unless demonstrated otherwise.

# Chapter 2: Cross-Cultural Research Methods

## Section 2.1: Methodological Challenges

Cross-cultural research faces unique methodological threats that do not arise in single-culture studies.

### Equivalence

**Measurement equivalence** (or invariance) is the extent to which a construct measured by a questionnaire or task has the same meaning and scaling properties across cultural groups.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Levels of equivalence.</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Conceptual equivalence</strong>: The construct itself (e.g., "depression") has the same meaning across cultures.</li>
<li><strong>Linguistic equivalence</strong>: The translated items convey the same meaning.</li>
<li><strong>Metric equivalence</strong>: The scale intervals are equal across groups (equal distances on the scale represent equal differences in the construct).</li>
<li><strong>Scalar equivalence</strong>: The absolute values (intercepts) are equivalent — groups with the same true score get the same mean response.</li>
</ul>
Only when scalar equivalence holds can mean scores be meaningfully compared across cultures.
</div>

Testing for equivalence requires **multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)** or **item response theory (IRT)** models. Most published cross-cultural comparisons do not formally test for measurement equivalence — a significant methodological gap.

### Response Biases

- **Acquiescence bias**: Tendency to agree with statements regardless of content, more prevalent in some cultures.
- **Extreme response bias**: Tendency to use scale endpoints, more prevalent in individualistic cultures.
- **Social desirability bias**: Tendency to report culturally approved responses.

### Sampling

Cultural comparisons are most informative when sampling is theory-driven — selecting cultures that represent theoretically relevant variation (e.g., high vs. low collectivism) rather than convenience samples. National samples may mask substantial within-country variation.

## Section 2.2: Research Designs

| Design | Description | Strengths | Limitations |
|--------|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Comparative** | Compare groups on a measure | Tests generalizability | Confounds culture with many variables |
| **Indigenous** | Develop constructs within one culture | Captures emic reality | May not generalize |
| **Cross-national survey** | Large-N international surveys (e.g., World Values Survey) | Breadth | Instrument equivalence concerns |
| **Experimental** | Manipulate cultural priming | Causal inference | Low ecological validity |
| **Ethnographic** | Immersive field observation | Deep understanding | Limited generalizability, observer effects |

### Emic vs. Etic Perspectives

- **Emic** (from *phonemic*): Concepts and measures that are internally valid within a specific culture; culture-specific.
- **Etic** (from *phonetic*): Concepts that apply universally across cultures; culturally general.

The **derived etic** approach (Berry): develop emic measures within each culture, then test for structural similarity across cultures. This avoids imposing WEIRD constructs on non-Western samples.

# Chapter 3: Dimensions of Cultural Variation

## Section 3.1: Individualism and Collectivism

The most studied dimension of cultural variation is **individualism–collectivism** (I–C):

<div class="definition">
<strong>Individualism.</strong> A cultural syndrome in which the self is construed as autonomous and independent; personal goals take priority over group goals; identity is defined by individual traits and achievements.
</div>

<div class="definition">
<strong>Collectivism.</strong> A cultural syndrome in which the self is construed as interdependent with significant others; group goals take priority over personal goals; identity is defined by group memberships.
</div>

Triandis (1995) identified important subtypes:
- **Horizontal individualism** (Sweden): autonomy without assertion of superiority.
- **Vertical individualism** (USA): autonomy combined with competition and hierarchy.
- **Horizontal collectivism** (Israeli kibbutz): cooperation among equals.
- **Vertical collectivism** (Japan, India): in-group loyalty with deference to hierarchy.

Hofstede's country scores place the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom highest on individualism; Guatemala, Ecuador, and Panama lowest.

### Implications of I–C

| Domain | Individualistic cultures | Collectivistic cultures |
|--------|-------------------------|------------------------|
| Self-concept | Independent, unique attributes | Interdependent, relational |
| Motivation | Personal achievement, intrinsic | Harmony, social approval |
| Conflict style | Direct confrontation | Avoidance, face-saving |
| Attribution | Dispositional (FAE) | Situational, contextual |
| Well-being | Self-esteem, personal growth | Social harmony, fulfilling roles |

## Section 3.2: Hofstede's Six Dimensions

Based on surveys of IBM employees in 50+ countries, Hofstede (1980, updated) proposed a dimensional model of national culture:

1. **Individualism–Collectivism (IDV)**: See above.
2. **Power Distance (PDI)**: Acceptance of unequal distribution of power. High PDI: deference to authority, hierarchical organizations. Low PDI: flat hierarchies, participative management.
3. **Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI)**: Tolerance for ambiguity. High UAI: many rules, low risk tolerance. Low UAI: flexible, comfort with novelty.
4. **Masculinity–Femininity (MAS)**: Masculine: achievement, competitiveness, gender differentiation. Feminine: quality of life, cooperation, gender equality.
5. **Long-Term vs. Short-Term Orientation (LTO)**: Long-term: thrift, perseverance, future orientation. Short-term: respect for tradition, present focus.
6. **Indulgence vs. Restraint (IVR)**: Indulgence: gratification of natural desires. Restraint: suppression of needs via social norms.

<div class="remark">
<strong>Criticism of Hofstede's model.</strong> The model has been criticized for (1) treating nations as equivalent to cultures, ignoring within-country variation; (2) being derived from a single company (IBM), limiting ecological validity; (3) ecological fallacy — applying group-level scores to individual behaviour; and (4) the dimensions not being fully independent. Despite these criticisms, the model remains the most widely used framework in cross-cultural management and psychology.
</div>

## Section 3.3: Tightness–Looseness

Michele Gelfand's **tightness–looseness** dimension captures the strength of social norms and tolerance for deviance:

- **Tight cultures** (Japan, Norway, Singapore): strong norms, low deviance tolerance, punish violators strongly.
- **Loose cultures** (United States, Brazil, Greece): flexible norms, high deviance tolerance.

Ecological threat (resource scarcity, disease prevalence, natural disasters) predicts cultural tightness — tight norms coordinate defence against collective threats.

## Section 3.4: Independent vs. Interdependent Self-Construal

Markus and Kitayama (1991) proposed that cultures differ in the **self-construal** they foster:

- **Independent self-construal**: The self is a bounded, autonomous entity defined by internal attributes (traits, abilities, values). Social relationships are chosen.
- **Interdependent self-construal**: The self is defined by its relationships and social roles. Maintaining harmony and fulfilling relational obligations is central to identity.

These self-construals have downstream effects on cognition (holistic vs. analytic perception), motivation (self-enhancement vs. self-improvement), emotion (pride vs. shame orientation), and social behaviour.

# Chapter 4: Cultural Differences in Communication

## Section 4.1: High-Context vs. Low-Context Communication

Edward Hall (1976) distinguished between **high-context** and **low-context** communication styles:

- **Low-context communication** (characteristic of Northern Europe, North America): messages are explicit, direct, verbal. Meaning is in the words.
- **High-context communication** (characteristic of East Asia, Middle East, Latin America): messages depend on context, relationship history, and nonverbal cues. Much meaning is implicit.

These differences create systematic misunderstandings in cross-cultural interactions. A "yes" in a high-context culture may mean "I have heard you" rather than "I agree."

## Section 4.2: Verbal and Nonverbal Communication

**Verbal communication** differs across cultures in:
- Directness/indirectness
- Turn-taking and interruption norms
- Appropriate topics and levels of disclosure
- Use of politeness markers and honorifics

**Nonverbal communication** includes:
- **Proxemics**: interpersonal distance. Hall identified intimate (0–18 in.), personal (18 in.–4 ft), social (4–12 ft), and public (12+ ft) zones, with considerable cultural variation.
- **Haptics**: touch norms. Mediterranean and Latin American cultures are high-contact; Northern European and East Asian cultures are low-contact.
- **Eye contact**: prolonged eye contact signals respect in some cultures, disrespect in others.
- **Facial expressions**: Ekman's basic emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, surprise) appear to have universal recognition, but display rules — norms about when and how to express emotions — are culturally variable.

## Section 4.3: Politeness Theory

Brown and Levinson's politeness theory proposes that communication acts involve threats to **face** — public self-image. Strategies to mitigate face threats:

- **Positive politeness**: affirm and support the other person's desire to be liked.
- **Negative politeness**: respect the other person's autonomy and right not to be imposed upon.
- **Off-record**: use indirect communication so the face-threatening act is deniable.

High-context cultures tend to prioritize negative face (not imposing) and off-record strategies; low-context cultures tend toward more direct communication.

# Chapter 5: Immigrants, Refugees, and Acculturation

## Section 5.1: Acculturation

**Acculturation** refers to the cultural and psychological changes that occur when individuals from different cultures come into sustained contact. Berry's (1997) two-dimensional model proposes that acculturation strategies depend on two questions:

1. Is maintaining one's heritage culture of value?
2. Is having positive relationships with the host society of value?

<div class="definition">
<strong>Berry's acculturation strategies.</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Integration</strong>: Maintain heritage culture AND engage with host culture. Associated with best psychological outcomes.</li>
<li><strong>Assimilation</strong>: Give up heritage culture to join host culture.</li>
<li><strong>Separation</strong>: Maintain heritage culture while rejecting host culture.</li>
<li><strong>Marginalization</strong>: Neither maintain heritage nor engage with host culture. Associated with worst outcomes.</li>
</ul>
</div>

Integration is associated with better mental health, higher self-esteem, and greater well-being than other strategies, particularly when the host society adopts a multicultural rather than assimilationist ideology.

## Section 5.2: Acculturative Stress

Acculturative stress refers to the stress that arises specifically from the demands of acculturation: language barriers, discrimination, loss of social networks, role changes, and navigating bicultural identity.

Factors that moderate acculturative stress:
- **Pre-migration stress**: trauma, persecution (especially for refugees).
- **Social support**: co-ethnic community presence and quality.
- **Perceived discrimination**: higher discrimination → higher stress.
- **Cultural distance**: greater differences between heritage and host culture → more difficulty.
- **Legal status**: undocumented immigrants face unique stressors.

## Section 5.3: Refugees vs. Voluntary Immigrants

Refugees face unique psychological challenges compared to voluntary immigrants:
- **Pre-migration trauma**: exposure to war, torture, persecution.
- **Forced displacement**: no choice in leaving, often rapid exit.
- **Uncertain legal status**: asylum processes create prolonged uncertainty.
- **Limited economic integration**: restrictions on employment.

Rates of PTSD, depression, and anxiety are elevated in refugee populations compared to voluntary migrants. However, resilience factors — community support, cultural identity, purpose — modulate outcomes substantially.

# Chapter 6: Stereotyping, Prejudice, Discrimination, and Racism

## Section 6.1: Social Cognition Foundations

**Stereotypes** are cognitive schemas that associate social groups with attributes. They arise from:
- **Categorization**: the cognitive necessity of grouping objects and people.
- **Illusory correlation**: over-estimating the co-occurrence of distinctive groups and distinctive behaviours.
- **Cultural transmission**: stereotypes are learned from family, media, and cultural environment.

**Prejudice** is an affective attitude toward a group (typically negative). **Discrimination** is differential treatment based on group membership.

## Section 6.2: Types of Racism

- **Individual racism**: explicitly negative beliefs about racial groups.
- **Institutional/systemic racism**: policies, practices, and structures that produce racially unequal outcomes regardless of individual intent.
- **Aversive racism** (Dovidio & Gaertner): The form most common among well-intentioned people in contemporary Western societies. Explicit egalitarian values coexist with implicit racial biases; discrimination occurs subtly in ambiguous situations.
- **Internalized racism**: acceptance of negative societal messages about one's own group.

## Section 6.3: Implicit Bias

Implicit attitudes can be measured with reaction-time paradigms such as the **Implicit Association Test (IAT)**, which measures the strength of automatic associations between concepts (e.g., race + good/bad).

IAT scores predict discriminatory behaviour over and above explicit measures, though the predictive validity of the IAT is debated. Meta-analyses suggest implicit measures predict discriminatory behaviour, especially in subtle or ambiguous situations.

## Section 6.4: Cross-Cultural Variation in Racism

Racism is not culturally uniform. Forms and targets of prejudice vary across societies:
- Ethnic prejudice in homogeneous societies may target class or caste rather than visible minority groups.
- Colorism (discrimination based on skin tone within racial groups) is documented in many African, Asian, and Latin American contexts.
- **Realistic group conflict theory** (Sherif): prejudice increases when groups compete for scarce resources.
- **Social identity theory** (Tajfel & Turner): in-group favoritism and out-group derogation arise from basic group identification processes.

# Chapter 7: Cultural Identity Development

## Section 7.1: Models of Ethnic Identity Development

**Ethnic identity** refers to the degree to which one identifies with and values membership in an ethnic group. Phinney's (1992) model identifies three stages:

1. **Unexamined ethnic identity**: little reflection on ethnicity; may accept majority group's negative characterizations (foreclosure) or have no engagement (diffusion).
2. **Ethnic identity search**: exposure to discrimination or cross-cultural contact triggers exploration.
3. **Achieved ethnic identity**: secure, positive sense of ethnic identity following exploration.

Higher ethnic identity achievement is associated with higher self-esteem, greater psychological well-being, and better academic outcomes, particularly for minority youth.

## Section 7.2: Bicultural Identity

People who are exposed to two cultures from an early age may develop a **bicultural identity** — the ability to internalize and draw on both cultural frameworks. This includes:

- **Frame switching**: alternating between cultural orientations depending on context.
- **Cultural blending**: integrating elements of both cultures into a unique identity.

**Bicultural identity integration (BII)**: The extent to which a person perceives their two cultural identities as compatible vs. conflicting. High BII is associated with greater creativity, flexibility, and well-being.

## Section 7.3: Intersectionality

Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) holds that identity dimensions (race, gender, class, sexuality, disability) cannot be analyzed in isolation — they interact to produce unique experiences of privilege and oppression. For example, the experiences of Black women cannot be reduced to "Black experiences" + "women's experiences" but involve a distinct intersection.

Applied to cross-cultural psychology, intersectionality challenges research that treats cultural group membership as a single, dominant identity variable.

# Chapter 8: Culture and Physical Health

## Section 8.1: Cultural Influences on Health Behaviours

Health behaviours — diet, exercise, substance use, help-seeking — are culturally embedded. Cultural norms determine:
- What foods are valued or forbidden
- Whether physical activity is embedded in daily life or treated as leisure
- Whether mental and physical illness are distinguished
- Whether professional medical care or traditional/folk medicine is preferred

The **health belief model** and **theory of planned behaviour** have been extended to account for cultural variation in health beliefs (e.g., fatalism, collectivist decision-making in treatment choices).

## Section 8.2: Sociocultural Determinants of Health

Socioeconomic status, discrimination, and acculturation stress are major social determinants of health disparities:

- **Allostatic load**: cumulative physiological wear from chronic stress. Discrimination increases allostatic load.
- **Weathering hypothesis** (Geronimus): early health deterioration in Black Americans from cumulative racial stress.
- **Healthy immigrant effect**: new immigrants often have better health than native-born populations, which erodes over time as they adopt host-country diets and lifestyles.

## Section 8.3: Cultural Explanatory Models of Illness

Kleinman's **explanatory model** framework identifies that patients and providers may hold different models of illness:
- **Patient model**: often includes supernatural causes, imbalance, moral violation.
- **Biomedical model**: mechanistic, disease-focused.

Cultural competence in health care requires eliciting patients' explanatory models to negotiate effective treatment plans.

# Chapter 9: Culture and Mental Health

## Section 9.1: Culture and Psychopathology

The relationship between culture and mental illness is multidimensional:
- **Cultural shaping of symptom expression**: Depression may manifest as somatic complaints (fatigue, pain) in cultures where emotional expression is stigmatized.
- **Cultural variation in diagnostic thresholds**: What counts as a disorder depends on cultural norms.
- **Culture-bound syndromes**: Clusters of symptoms recognized in specific cultural contexts but not fitting standard DSM/ICD categories. Examples: *ataque de nervios* (Latin American emotional episode), *taijin kyofusho* (Japanese fear of offending others), *amok* (Southeast Asian).

The DSM-5 includes a **Cultural Formulation Interview** to assess cultural context of presentation.

## Section 9.2: Help-Seeking and Stigma

Cross-cultural differences in mental health help-seeking are well-documented:
- **Stigma** associated with mental illness is higher in many Asian cultures, reducing willingness to seek professional care.
- **Collectivist cultures** often involve family rather than individual decision-making about treatment.
- **Somatization**: Conversion of psychological distress into physical symptoms is more common in populations where emotional expression is culturally restricted.

## Section 9.3: Cultural Competence in Clinical Practice

**Cultural competence** refers to the ability to interact effectively with people from different cultures. Components include:
- **Cultural awareness**: Recognition of one's own cultural biases.
- **Cultural knowledge**: Familiarity with specific cultural groups' worldviews and health beliefs.
- **Cultural skills**: Effective communication and assessment across cultural contexts.
- **Cultural encounters**: Direct experience with diverse populations.

The concept has evolved toward **cultural humility** — an ongoing, self-reflexive commitment to learning, rather than a fixed set of competencies.

# Chapter 10: Building Cultural Competence

## Section 10.1: Contact Hypothesis

Gordon Allport's **contact hypothesis** (1954) proposes that intergroup contact reduces prejudice under four optimal conditions:

1. **Equal status** between groups in the contact situation.
2. **Common goals** pursued cooperatively.
3. **Intergroup cooperation** without intergroup competition.
4. **Authority sanction**: support from institutional authorities.

Meta-analyses (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006) confirm that contact reduces prejudice, even when conditions are not fully optimal. The effect is partly mediated by reduced anxiety and increased empathy.

## Section 10.2: Intercultural Competence Development

Bennett's **Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity (DMIS)** describes movement from ethnocentrism to ethnorelativism:

| Stage | Orientation | Description |
|-------|-------------|-------------|
| Denial | Ethnocentric | Culture difference not perceived; cultural isolation |
| Defense | Ethnocentric | Own culture superior; cultural difference threatening |
| Minimization | Ethnocentric | Differences minimized; "we're all basically the same" |
| Acceptance | Ethnorelative | Recognizes and respects cultural differences |
| Adaptation | Ethnorelative | Can shift frame of reference; effective cross-cultural communication |
| Integration | Ethnorelative | Identity includes multicultural frames; does not privilege any single culture |

## Section 10.3: Microaggressions

Microaggressions (Sue et al., 2007) are brief, everyday indignities — verbal, behavioural, or environmental — that communicate hostile, derogatory, or negative messages to members of marginalized groups:

- **Microinsults**: communications that convey rudeness or insensitivity.
- **Microinvalidations**: communications that exclude or negate the experiences of people of color.
- **Environmental microaggressions**: institutional and structural messages that demean or marginalize.

Cumulative exposure to microaggressions is associated with psychological distress, even when individual incidents seem minor.

## Section 10.4: Cultural Safety Beyond Competence

**Cultural safety** (Ramsden, 1990, developed in Māori nursing context) moves beyond individual competence to address power dynamics in cross-cultural interactions. An encounter is culturally safe when the recipient of care feels their identity, values, and needs are respected, not imposed upon. Cultural safety requires practitioners to examine their own power and the systemic factors that produce health inequities — not merely to acquire cultural knowledge about the "other."
