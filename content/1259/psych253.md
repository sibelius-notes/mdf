# Chapter 1: Situational Influence and the Foundations of Social Psychology

## Section 1.1: The Core Insight of Social Psychology

The **fundamental insight** of social psychology is the **power of the situation**: human behaviour is far more influenced by the immediate social context than common sense (and everyday psychology) suggest. People tend to attribute behaviour to stable, internal traits — **dispositionism** — while underweighting the power of situational forces.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Fundamental attribution error (FAE).</strong> The tendency to overestimate the role of dispositional factors (traits, character) and underestimate the role of situational factors when explaining others' behaviour (Ross, 1977). Also called the <em>correspondence bias</em>.
</div>

The FAE leads us to conclude that a person who does something cruel is cruel, without considering the situational pressures they were under.

## Section 1.2: The Milgram Obedience Studies

Stanley Milgram's (1963) experiments are the most famous demonstration of situational power. Participants were instructed by an authority figure (an experimenter in a lab coat) to administer increasingly severe electric shocks to a "learner" (a confederate) for each wrong answer.

Despite the learner's protests and screams, **65% of participants delivered the maximum 450-volt shock** — well beyond the levels labelled "Danger: Severe Shock." Milgram had predicted that only 1–3% would comply at the highest level.

Key situational factors that increased obedience:
- **Proximity to authority**: obedience was highest when the experimenter was present in the room.
- **Distance from the victim**: obedience was highest when the learner was in another room.
- **Legitimacy of the institution**: conducted at Yale, obedience was higher than at a less prestigious setting.
- **Graduated commitment**: the shocks increased slowly; participants could not identify a clear stopping point.

Milgram's studies demonstrate that ordinary people, under situational pressure, will perform acts they would normally consider deeply wrong.

## Section 1.3: The Stanford Prison Experiment

Philip Zimbardo's (1971) Stanford Prison Experiment assigned participants randomly to roles as "guards" or "prisoners" in a simulated prison. Within days, guards began behaving with striking cruelty, and prisoners showed signs of emotional breakdown. The study was terminated after 6 days (planned for 2 weeks).

The experiment illustrates **role theory**: the social roles we inhabit shape our behaviour profoundly, even when those roles are arbitrary and short-term.

<div class="remark">
<strong>Critical evaluation.</strong> Both Milgram's and Zimbardo's studies are now recognized as ethically problematic and have been subjected to reanalysis. Reinterpretations of Zimbardo's study suggest more active coaching of the guards than originally reported. Nevertheless, the broad lesson — that situations profoundly shape behaviour — is well supported.
</div>

# Chapter 2: Research Methods in Social Psychology

## Section 2.1: The Experimental Method

Social psychology is primarily experimental. Key concepts:

- **Random assignment**: ensures that pre-existing differences between participants are distributed randomly across conditions, allowing causal inference.
- **Independent variable (IV)**: the variable manipulated by the experimenter.
- **Dependent variable (DV)**: the outcome measured.
- **Demand characteristics**: cues in the experimental setting that communicate the expected response to participants.
- **Cover story**: a false rationale for the study, used to prevent demand characteristics.

## Section 2.2: Mundane vs. Experimental Realism

- **Mundane realism**: the extent to which the study mirrors real-world situations.
- **Experimental realism**: the extent to which participants are genuinely engaged and affected by the experimental situation.

Milgram's obedience studies had low mundane realism (real authority figures don't usually ask people to shock others) but very high experimental realism (participants were genuinely stressed and torn). High experimental realism is more important for drawing valid conclusions about psychological processes.

# Chapter 3: Conformity, Social Norms, and the Psychology of Culture

## Section 3.1: Conformity

**Conformity** is the tendency to change behaviour, beliefs, or attitudes to match a group's real or imagined expectations.

**Asch's (1951) line studies**: participants judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard line. The task was easy — correct answers were obvious. But when five confederates unanimously gave the wrong answer, 75% of real participants conformed at least once, and the overall error rate was 37%.

Two processes drive conformity:
- **Informational influence**: following the group because you believe it has better information about reality.
- **Normative influence**: following the group to be liked or to avoid social disapproval.

## Section 3.2: Pluralistic Ignorance

**Pluralistic ignorance** occurs when everyone in a group privately rejects a norm but believes that everyone else accepts it, so the norm persists:

\[
\text{Private belief} \neq \text{Inferred group belief} \to \text{Silence} \to \text{Norm persists}
\]

Example: binge drinking on campuses. Most students privately find heavy drinking excessive but incorrectly believe their peers drink more than they do. This misperception sustains norms of heavy drinking.

## Section 3.3: Social Norms and Culture

**Descriptive norms**: what most people do. **Injunctive norms**: what most people approve or disapprove of doing. These can diverge (most people litter, but most people disapprove of littering).

**Cultural transmission**: norms are maintained through social learning, modelling, and enforcement of deviance. The study of how cultures create and transmit shared norms is a core concern of cultural psychology (see PSYCH 352R).

# Chapter 4: Social Cognition

## Section 4.1: Schemas and Heuristics

A **schema** is a cognitive structure that organizes knowledge and guides information processing. Schemas determine what we notice, how we interpret ambiguous information, and what we remember.

Mental shortcuts (**heuristics**) allow fast, efficient — but sometimes biased — judgments:

- **Availability heuristic**: estimate the probability of an event by how easily examples come to mind. Overestimate the frequency of dramatic but rare events (plane crashes, shark attacks) relative to mundane but common events (car accidents, heart disease).
- **Representativeness heuristic**: judge the probability that something belongs to a category by how closely it resembles the prototype of that category. Leads to the **conjunction fallacy**: judging "feminist bank teller" as more probable than "bank teller" (Linda problem; Kahneman & Tversky).
- **Anchoring**: initial estimates are insufficiently adjusted from an initial anchor value.

## Section 4.2: Attributions

**Attribution** is the process of explaining the causes of behaviour. Heider's classic framework:

- **Internal (dispositional) attributions**: behaviour attributed to the person's traits, abilities, or character.
- **External (situational) attributions**: behaviour attributed to the environment, circumstances, or luck.

**Covariation model** (Kelley, 1967): we attribute behaviour to whatever factor **covaries** with it. We consider:
- **Consensus**: do others behave this way?
- **Distinctiveness**: does this person behave this way only in this situation?
- **Consistency**: does this person behave this way across time?

High consensus + high distinctiveness + high consistency → external attribution. Low consensus + low distinctiveness + high consistency → internal attribution.

## Section 4.3: Automatic vs. Controlled Processing

Dual-process models (Kahneman's System 1/System 2):

| System 1 (automatic) | System 2 (controlled) |
|---|---|
| Fast, effortless | Slow, effortful |
| Associative, intuitive | Analytic, deliberate |
| Context-dependent | Context-independent |
| Susceptible to biases | Corrects for biases (when motivated) |

Most social perception is System 1: quick impressions formed from minimal information. Controlled processing can correct for biases but requires cognitive resources and motivation.

# Chapter 5: The Self

## Section 5.1: Self-Concept

The **self-concept** is the set of beliefs we hold about who we are — our traits, abilities, values, and social identities.

**Self-schemas**: cognitive generalizations about the self, derived from past experience, that organize and guide the processing of self-relevant information.

**Social comparison theory** (Festinger, 1954): in the absence of objective information, people evaluate their abilities and opinions by comparing themselves to others. Comparisons are typically made to similar others.

## Section 5.2: Self-Esteem and Self-Enhancement

**Self-esteem** is an overall evaluation of the self as positive or negative.

The **self-enhancement motive**: people are motivated to view themselves positively. Strategies include:
- **Self-serving attributions**: attributing successes to internal factors and failures to external factors.
- **Downward social comparison**: comparing to worse-off others.
- **BIRG-ing** (basking in reflected glory): associating with successful others.
- **Self-handicapping**: creating obstacles to success in advance, to have an excuse for failure.

Cross-cultural variation: self-enhancement is stronger in individualistic cultures; collectivistic cultures show more self-criticism and **self-improvement motivation**.

## Section 5.3: Cognitive Dissonance

**Cognitive dissonance** (Festinger, 1957) is the discomfort arising from holding contradictory cognitions. People are motivated to reduce dissonance by:
- Changing one of the cognitions
- Adding new consonant cognitions
- Reducing the importance of the conflict

**Classic demonstration** (Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959): participants who performed a boring task and then told the next participant it was interesting for $1 (small external justification) later rated the task as more enjoyable than those who were paid $20 (large external justification). The $1 participants had to convince themselves they actually enjoyed it to justify their behaviour.

**Post-choice dissonance**: after making a difficult choice, people enhance the attractiveness of the chosen option and diminish the unchosen option — the "spreading of alternatives."

# Chapter 6: Attitudes and Persuasion

## Section 6.1: The Structure of Attitudes

An **attitude** is a relatively enduring evaluation of an object, person, or issue that has **cognitive**, **affective**, and **behavioural** components (the ABC model). Attitudes vary in:
- **Strength**: how strongly held (attitude certainty, resistance to change)
- **Accessibility**: how easily the attitude comes to mind
- **Implicit vs. explicit**: implicit attitudes (measured with IAT) can diverge from explicit, consciously reported attitudes

## Section 6.2: Persuasion — The Elaboration Likelihood Model

The **Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)** (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) proposes two routes to persuasion:

- **Central route**: careful scrutiny of message arguments. Leads to durable attitude change when recipients are motivated and able to process. Changed attitudes predict behaviour and are resistant to counter-persuasion.
- **Peripheral route**: reliance on heuristics and superficial cues (communicator attractiveness, message length, source credibility). Faster but less durable attitude change.

Factors that increase central route processing:
- Personal relevance of the issue
- Need for cognition (individual difference in enjoyment of effortful thinking)
- Absence of distraction
- Low time pressure

## Section 6.3: Resistance to Persuasion

**Reactance** (Brehm, 1966): when people feel their freedom to hold a belief or make a choice is threatened, they move in the opposite direction. High-pressure persuasion often backfires.

**Inoculation theory** (McGuire): pre-exposing people to weakened counterarguments — with refutation — strengthens resistance to later persuasion, as in vaccine inoculation.

# Chapter 7: Attraction and Close Relationships

## Section 7.1: Interpersonal Attraction

Major determinants of liking:

- **Proximity** (propinquity effect): people who are physically closer are more likely to become friends. Increased exposure leads to **mere exposure effect**: repeated exposure to a novel stimulus increases liking for it.
- **Similarity**: we are attracted to others who share our attitudes, values, and backgrounds ("similarity breeds liking").
- **Physical attractiveness**: across cultures, physical attractiveness strongly predicts initial attraction. The **halo effect**: attractive people are assumed to have other positive qualities.
- **Reciprocal liking**: knowing someone likes us increases our attraction to them.

## Section 7.2: Love and Close Relationships

**Sternberg's triangular theory of love** proposes three components:
1. **Intimacy**: emotional closeness, feelings of connection.
2. **Passion**: romantic arousal and attraction.
3. **Commitment**: decision to remain in the relationship.

Different combinations of these components produce different love types: companionate love (intimacy + commitment), romantic love (intimacy + passion), consummate love (all three).

**Attachment theory** (Bowlby; Hazan & Shaver): early attachment styles — secure, anxious-preoccupied, dismissive-avoidant, fearful-avoidant — shape adult romantic relationships.

# Chapter 8: Stereotyping, Prejudice, and Stigma

## Section 8.1: Stereotypes as Cognitive Structures

**Stereotypes** are cognitive schemas that associate attributes with social groups. They arise from:
- Normal categorization processes
- Illusory correlation (Hamilton & Gifford, 1976): minority group members and infrequent behaviours are both distinctive, so they are over-associated in memory.
- Cultural transmission through media, family, and socialization.

Stereotypes are resistant to change because they direct attention toward confirming evidence and away from disconfirming evidence.

## Section 8.2: Prejudice and Discrimination

**Prejudice** is an attitudinal response (usually negative) to a group based on group membership. **Discrimination** is differential treatment based on group membership.

**Aversive racism** (Dovidio & Gaertner): Modern racism in the West is characterized by conflicting non-prejudiced self-image and negative implicit attitudes. Discrimination occurs in ambiguous situations where it can be attributed to factors other than race.

## Section 8.3: The Social Stigma

**Stigma** (Goffman, 1963): a deeply discrediting attribute that reduces a person from a whole to a tainted, discounted one. Three types:
- **Abominations of the body**: physical disabilities.
- **Blemishes of character**: mental illness, addiction, criminality.
- **Tribal stigma**: race, ethnicity, religion.

**Stereotype threat** (Steele & Aronson, 1995): awareness that one could confirm a negative stereotype about one's group impairs performance in stereotype-relevant domains. Black students underperformed on an academic test when it was described as a measure of intelligence (threat condition) but not when the same test was described as a problem-solving task.

# Chapter 9: Groups, Social Identities, and Intergroup Conflict

## Section 9.1: Social Identity Theory

**Social identity theory** (Tajfel & Turner, 1979): a significant part of self-concept derives from group memberships. People are motivated to maintain positive **social identity** — positive evaluation of their group relative to relevant comparison groups. This leads to:

- **In-group favoritism**: more positive evaluation of own group.
- **Out-group derogation**: more negative evaluation of other groups.
- **Minimal group paradigm**: even arbitrary categorizations (e.g., "Klee group" vs. "Kandinsky group") produce in-group favoritism and out-group discrimination.

## Section 9.2: Groupthink

**Groupthink** (Janis, 1972): a deterioration of mental efficiency and moral judgment in a cohesive group, driven by the desire for unanimity. Antecedents include high cohesion, insulation from outside information, and directive leadership.

Symptoms: illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalization, suppression of dissent, illusion of unanimity.

Historical cases: Bay of Pigs invasion, Challenger disaster.

Prevention: encourage critical evaluation, seek outside opinions, assign a "devil's advocate."

## Section 9.3: Deindividuation and Crowd Psychology

**Deindividuation**: loss of self-awareness and individual identity in group settings leads to reduced self-regulation and normative constraint. Anonymity increases deindividuation.

**Social loafing**: individuals exert less effort in a group task than when working alone (Ringelmann effect), particularly when individual contributions cannot be identified.

# Chapter 10: Altruism

## Section 10.1: The Bystander Effect

**Kitty Genovese case** (1964): a woman was attacked outside her apartment building while, according to early reports, 38 witnesses failed to call police (the actual number and their ability to see the attack was more limited than reported, but the psychological phenomenon is real).

Latané and Darley (1970) identified processes that inhibit bystander intervention:

1. **Notice**: bystanders must perceive that something is happening.
2. **Interpret as emergency**: ambiguous events are often interpreted as non-emergencies, especially via **pluralistic ignorance**.
3. **Feel responsible**: **diffusion of responsibility** — the more bystanders present, the less any individual feels responsible. Probability of helping decreases as group size increases.
4. **Know how to help**: lack of competence inhibits action.
5. **Act**: **evaluation apprehension** — fear of appearing foolish if the situation is not an emergency.

## Section 10.2: Empathy-Altruism Hypothesis

**Empathy-altruism hypothesis** (Batson, 1991): empathic concern for another person produces genuinely altruistic motivation — motivation to help the other person regardless of benefits to oneself.

Batson's research showed that when empathy was induced, participants helped even when escape from the situation was easy (ruling out distress-reduction as a motive).

**The scope of empathy**: **compassion fade** — people are more moved by a single identified victim ("Rokia, a 7-year-old girl in Mali...") than by statistics of suffering. Identifiable victim effect + arithmetic of compassion.

# Chapter 11: Aggression

## Section 11.1: Theories of Aggression

**Frustration-aggression hypothesis** (Dollard et al., 1939): aggression is always a consequence of frustration, and frustration always leads to aggression. Revised by Berkowitz (1989): frustration leads to aggression only when the frustration is aversive and when aggression-related cues are present.

**Social learning theory** (Bandura, 1973): aggression is learned through observation and modelling. Bobo doll studies: children exposed to aggressive adult models showed more aggression than those who observed non-aggressive models.

**General Aggression Model (GAM)** (Anderson & Bushman, 2002): a comprehensive model incorporating personal variables (aggressive traits, hostile attribution bias) and situational variables (provocation, weapons cues) that converge on hostile arousal and cognition, leading to appraisal and action.

## Section 11.2: Media and Aggression

The relationship between violent media (video games, films) and real-world aggression is extensively studied. Meta-analyses (Anderson et al.) find small positive effects of violent media on aggressive thoughts, feelings, and behaviour. Critics (Ferguson) argue the effect sizes are trivial and the research is methodologically flawed.

## Section 11.3: Reducing Aggression

Effective strategies for reducing aggression:

- **Incompatible responses**: empathy and laughter are incompatible with aggression; inducing these reduces aggressive responses.
- **Attributional retraining**: reducing hostile attribution bias by training people to consider benign explanations for ambiguous provocations.
- **Catharsis critique**: the idea that venting anger "drains" aggressive impulses is not supported; venting typically escalates aggression.
- **Structural interventions**: reduce heat stress, crowding, economic inequality — structural conditions that reliably increase aggression at the population level.
