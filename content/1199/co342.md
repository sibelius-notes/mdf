## Chapter 1: Review

This chapter revisits foundational material from prerequisites (Math 249 / CO 250 level), establishing notation and the key theorems we will use throughout. Even familiar results are stated precisely, because graph theory rewards careful definitions.

### Trees and Forests

A **tree** is a connected acyclic graph. A **forest** is an acyclic graph (equivalently, a disjoint union of trees). These are among the most fundamental objects in combinatorics: they appear in algorithm design, network routing, phylogenetics, and data structures.

![A tree with 7 vertices](/pics/co342/tree-def.png)

*A balanced binary tree with 7 vertices — a prototypical tree structure.*

One elementary but important fact about forests is that they always have "leaf" vertices — degree-1 vertices that can be removed without disconnecting anything. The proof is elegant: just follow the longest path.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 1.1.1.</strong> Every forest \(G = (V, E)\) with at least one edge has a leaf vertex — that is, a vertex of degree 1.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Let \(v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\) be the vertices of a longest path in \(G\). Since \(|E| \geq 1\), we have \(k \geq 2\). Consider the terminal vertex \(v_k\). If it had a neighbour \(w \notin \{v_{k-1}\}\), then either \(w\) is on the path (creating a circuit, impossible in a forest) or \(w\) is off the path (extending it, contradicting maximality). So \(v_k\) has degree exactly 1. \(\square\)</div>

A useful equivalent characterisation: a graph on \(n\) vertices is a tree if and only if it is connected and has exactly \(n-1\) edges. Trees have exactly one face when drawn as plane graphs, reflecting the formula \(v - e + f = 2\) applied to forests.

![A forest: two disjoint trees](/pics/co342/forest-def.png)

*A forest consisting of two disconnected trees — the union of trees with no extra edges.*

### Graph Colorings

A **proper \(k\)-coloring** of a graph \(G\) assigns one of \(k\) colors to each vertex such that adjacent vertices receive different colors. The **chromatic number** \(\chi(G)\) is the minimum \(k\) for which a proper \(k\)-coloring exists.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.2.1</strong> (Chromatic Number). The chromatic number \(\chi(G)\) of a graph \(G\) is the minimum \(k \geq 0\) such that the vertices of \(G\) can be assigned colors from \(\{1, 2, \ldots, k\}\) with adjacent vertices always receiving different colors.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 1.2.1</strong> (Greedy Bound). If every vertex of \(G\) has degree at most \(\Delta\), then \(\chi(G) \leq \Delta + 1\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> By induction on \(|V(G)|\). For the inductive step, take any vertex \(v\). By hypothesis, \(G - v\) is \((\Delta+1)\)-colorable. Since \(v\) has at most \(\Delta\) neighbors, at least one color from \(\{1,\ldots,\Delta+1\}\) is not used by any neighbor of \(v\). Assign that free color to \(v\). \(\square\)</div>

The bound \(\Delta + 1\) is tight for complete graphs \(K_n\) (where \(\Delta = n-1\) and \(\chi = n\)) and for odd cycles \(C_{2k+1}\) (where \(\Delta = 2\) and \(\chi = 3\)). Brooks' theorem (beyond the scope of this course) strengthens the bound: if \(G\) is connected, not a complete graph, and not an odd cycle, then \(\chi(G) \leq \Delta\).

![A proper 3-coloring](/pics/co342/coloring-3.png)

*A proper 3-coloring: no two adjacent vertices share a color.*

![K4 as a 4-chromatic graph](/pics/co342/coloring-chromatic.png)

*The complete graph \(K_4\) requires exactly 4 colors — it is 4-chromatic.*

The celebrated Four Color Theorem (Appel-Haken, 1976) states that every planar graph is 4-colorable. This is one of the deepest results in combinatorics and was the first major theorem proved with computer assistance.

### Matchings

**Matchings** are a central topic in combinatorial optimization. A matching captures the idea of pairing up elements of a set subject to compatibility constraints — scheduling workers to shifts, assigning residents to hospitals, or routing packets in a network.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.3.1</strong> (Matching). A <strong>matching</strong> \(M\) in a simple graph \(G\) is a subset of edges \(M \subseteq E(G)\) such that no two edges in \(M\) share a vertex. Formally, \(\{x,y\} \in M\) implies \(\{x,z\}, \{y,z\} \notin M\) for all \(z \neq x,y\). The size \(\nu(G)\) denotes the number of edges in a maximum (largest) matching.</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.3.2</strong> (Saturation). A matching \(M\) <strong>saturates</strong> (or <strong>satisfies</strong>) a vertex \(v\) if some edge of \(M\) is incident to \(v\). A vertex not saturated by \(M\) is <strong>\(M\)-exposed</strong>.</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.3.3</strong> (Perfect Matching). A matching is <strong>perfect</strong> if it saturates every vertex. Perfect matchings can only exist when \(|V(G)|\) is even.</div>

![A graph with a highlighted matching](/pics/co342/matching-def.png)

*Red edges form a matching: each vertex is incident to at most one red edge.*

The distinction between **maximal** and **maximum** matchings deserves emphasis. A matching is maximal if no edge can be added to it; it is maximum if no matching has more edges. Every maximum matching is maximal, but the converse fails.

![Maximum vs maximal matching comparison](/pics/co342/matching-max-vs-maximal.png)

*Left: a maximal matching of size 2 (cannot extend, but not maximum). Right: a maximum matching of size 3 on the same path graph \(P_6\).*

#### 1.3.1 Hall's Theorem

The most important theorem in matching theory is Hall's Marriage Theorem (1935), which gives a complete combinatorial characterization of when a bipartite graph has a matching saturating one side. Its name comes from the following metaphor: we have a set \(X\) of men and a set \(Y\) of women, with edges indicating compatibility. When can every man be matched to a compatible woman?

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.3.4</strong> (Neighbourhood). For \(S \subseteq V(G)\), the <strong>neighbourhood</strong> is \(\Gamma_G(S) := \{w \in V(G) : \exists v \in S, \{v,w\} \in E(G)\}\) — the set of all vertices adjacent to some vertex in \(S\).</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.3.5</strong> (Hall's Condition). Let \(G\) be bipartite with parts \(X, Y\). <strong>Hall's Condition</strong> holds if \(|\Gamma(S)| \geq |S|\) for every \(S \subseteq X\).</div>

Hall's Condition is a necessary condition for a matching saturating \(X\): if any \(k\) vertices on the left have fewer than \(k\) neighbors on the right, they cannot all be matched. Remarkably, it is also sufficient.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 1.3.1</strong> (Hall's Marriage Theorem, 1935). Let \(G\) be bipartite with parts \(X, Y\). There exists a matching saturating \(X\) if and only if Hall's Condition holds: \(|\Gamma(S)| \geq |S|\) for all \(S \subseteq X\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong>
<em>(\(\Rightarrow\))</em> If a matching saturates \(X\), then for any \(S \subseteq X\), each vertex in \(S\) is matched to a distinct neighbor, so \(|\Gamma(S)| \geq |S|\).

<em>(\(\Leftarrow\))</em> By induction on \(|X|\). For \(|X|=1\), Hall's Condition gives at least one neighbor to match to.

<strong>Case I (Tight set exists):</strong> Pick any \(x \in X\) and any neighbor \(y \in Y\). If Hall's Condition holds in the subgraph \(G - x - y\) (with parts \(X-x\) and \(Y-y\)), the inductive hypothesis gives a matching saturating \(X-x\), which combined with the edge \(xy\) saturates all of \(X\).

<strong>Case II (No tight set):</strong> If Hall's Condition fails for \(G - x - y\), there is \(S_0 \subseteq X - x\) with \(|\Gamma_{G-x-y}(S_0)| < |S_0|\). Since Hall's Condition holds for \(G\) and removing \(y\) reduces the neighborhood of \(S_0\) by at most 1, we get \(|\Gamma_G(S_0)| = |S_0|\). We find disjoint matchings: one saturating \(S_0\) into \(\Gamma_G(S_0)\) (by Hall's Condition applied to \(S_0\)), and one saturating \(X \setminus S_0\) in the subgraph avoiding \(S_0\) and \(\Gamma_G(S_0)\) (Hall's Condition verified via inclusion-exclusion). Their union saturates \(X\). \(\square\)</div>

![Bipartite graph satisfying Hall's condition](/pics/co342/halls-condition-ok.png)

*Hall's Condition satisfied: every subset \(S \subseteq X\) has \(|\Gamma(S)| \geq |S|\). A perfect matching exists.*

![Bipartite graph violating Hall's condition](/pics/co342/halls-condition-fail.png)

*Hall's Condition violated: \(S = \{x_1,x_2,x_3\}\) but \(|N(S)| = 1 < 3\). No matching saturating \(X\) exists.*

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary 1.3.1.1.</strong> Every regular bipartite graph with degree \(\geq 1\) has a perfect matching.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Let \(G\) be \(k\)-regular bipartite with parts \(X, Y\). For any \(S \subseteq X\), count edges from \(S\) to \(\Gamma(S)\): there are exactly \(k|S|\) such edges, each incident to a vertex in \(\Gamma(S)\) with degree \(k\), so \(k|S| \leq k|\Gamma(S)|\), giving \(|\Gamma(S)| \geq |S|\). Hall's Condition holds. By symmetry \(|X| = |Y|\), so the matching is perfect. \(\square\)</div>

#### 1.3.2 Berge's Theorem

Berge's theorem characterizes maximum matchings via **augmenting paths** — alternating paths that start and end at exposed vertices, and can be used to increase the matching size.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.3.6</strong> (Alternating Path). An <strong>\(M\)-alternating path</strong> is a path in \(G\) whose edges alternate between being in \(M\) and not in \(M\).</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.3.7</strong> (Augmenting Path). An <strong>\(M\)-augmenting path</strong> is an \(M\)-alternating path whose both endpoints are \(M\)-exposed. Augmenting the matching along this path (swapping matched/unmatched edges) yields a matching of size \(|M|+1\).</div>

![An augmenting path in a bipartite graph](/pics/co342/augmenting-path.png)

*Current matching in red; augmenting path in blue connecting two exposed vertices. Swapping along the blue path increases the matching by one.*

![Animation of augmenting path algorithm](/pics/co342/augmenting-path.gif)

*Animated: the augmenting path algorithm on a bipartite graph, step by step.*

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 1.3.2</strong> (Berge, 1957). A matching \(M\) in a graph \(G\) is maximum if and only if \(G\) contains no \(M\)-augmenting path.</div>

This theorem provides the conceptual basis for all matching algorithms: to find a maximum matching, repeatedly search for augmenting paths and augment until none remain.

### Vertex Covers

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 1.4.1</strong> (Vertex Cover). A <strong>vertex cover</strong> is a set \(C \subseteq V(G)\) such that every edge has at least one endpoint in \(C\): \(\forall \{x,y\} \in E(G),\ x \in C \vee y \in C\). The minimum cover size is \(\tau(G)\).</div>

![A vertex cover highlighted in gold](/pics/co342/vertex-cover-def.png)

*Gold vertices form a vertex cover: every edge touches at least one gold vertex.*

Matchings and vertex covers are dual to each other. The following inequality is immediate:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 1.4.1.</strong> \(\nu(G) \leq \tau(G) \leq 2\nu(G)\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> <em>Lower bound:</em> Every cover must include at least one endpoint of each matching edge, and matching edges are disjoint, so \(\tau(G) \geq \nu(G)\). <em>Upper bound:</em> Taking all endpoints of a maximum matching gives a valid cover of size \(2\nu(G)\) (else some edge would extend the matching, a contradiction). \(\square\)</div>

#### König's Theorem

For bipartite graphs, the inequality \(\nu \leq \tau\) becomes an equality — a remarkable fact with no analogue for general graphs.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 1.4.2</strong> (König's Theorem, 1931). For any bipartite graph \(G\), \(\nu(G) = \tau(G)\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> It suffices to produce a vertex cover of size \(\nu(G)\). Let \(A, B\) be the bipartition. Let \(S \subseteq A, T \subseteq B\) be a minimum vertex cover. Since \(S + T\) covers all edges, there are no edges between \(A \setminus S\) and \(B \setminus T\).

We apply Hall's Theorem to find matchings: for any \(Q \subseteq S\), if \(Q\) had fewer than \(|Q|\) neighbors in \(B \setminus T\), we could replace \(Q\) in the cover by \(\Gamma_G(Q) \setminus T\) (a smaller set covering the same edges), contradicting minimality. So Hall's Condition holds, and we find a matching saturating \(S\) into \(B \setminus T\). Symmetrically, we find a matching saturating \(T\) into \(A \setminus S\). These two matchings are disjoint and together have \(|S| + |T| = \tau(G)\) edges, so \(\nu(G) \geq \tau(G)\). Combined with \(\nu(G) \leq \tau(G)\), we conclude. \(\square\)</div>

![König's theorem: matching and cover of the same size](/pics/co342/konig-theorem.png)

*Gold nodes form a minimum vertex cover; red edges form a maximum matching. König's theorem guarantees their sizes are equal in bipartite graphs.*

König's theorem fails for non-bipartite graphs: the triangle \(K_3\) has \(\nu = 1\) but \(\tau = 2\). The obstruction is odd cycles, which cannot be perfectly matched.

## Chapter 2: Introduction

### Formal Definitions

Graph theory requires a careful general definition of "graph" that handles loops and parallel edges. Felix Zhou's notes follow the Diestel convention:

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 2.1.1</strong> (Graph). A <strong>graph</strong> \(G = (V, E, i)\) is a triple where \(V\) is a finite set of <strong>vertices</strong>, \(E\) is a finite set of <strong>edges</strong> with \(V \cap E = \emptyset\), and \(i : V \times E \to \{0, 1, 2\}\) is the <strong>incidence function</strong> satisfying: \(i(v,e)\) counts the number of times edge \(e\) is incident to vertex \(v\), and \(\sum_{v \in V} i(v,e) = 2\) for every \(e \in E\). When \(i(v,e) = 2\) for some \(v\), the edge \(e\) is a <strong>loop</strong>. A <strong>simple graph</strong> has no loops and no parallel edges.</div>

This formal triple-based definition is more general than the familiar "set of vertices and set of edges" description from undergraduate courses. The advantage appears in dual graph constructions: if \(G = (V, E, i)\) is a plane graph with faces \(F\), its dual \(G^* = (F, E, j)\) uses the same edge set \(E\) with a new incidence function \(j\), naturally handled by this framework.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 2.1.2</strong> (Degree). The <strong>degree</strong> of vertex \(v\) is \(\deg(v) = \sum_{e \in E} i(v, e)\). Loops contribute 2 to the degree.</div>

The **Handshaking Lemma** states that \(\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2|E|\), since each edge contributes exactly 2 to the total degree sum.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 2.1.3</strong> (Subgraph). A <strong>subgraph</strong> of \(G = (V, E, i)\) is a triple \(H = (V', E', i')\) with \(V' \subseteq V\), \(E' \subseteq E\), and \(i' = i|_{V' \times E'}\). The <strong>induced subgraph</strong> \(G[X]\) on \(X \subseteq V\) includes all edges of \(G\) with both endpoints in \(X\).</div>

![A graph with a highlighted induced subgraph](/pics/co342/subgraph-def.png)

*Red vertices and edges form an induced subgraph: all edges between red vertices are included.*

### Graph Families and Basic Notions

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition 2.1.4</strong> (Graph Families). Key named families:
<ul>
<li><strong>Complete graph</strong> \(K_n\): all \(\binom{n}{2}\) possible edges on \(n\) vertices; \(\chi(K_n) = n\).</li>
<li><strong>Cycle</strong> \(C_n\): a single circuit on \(n\) vertices.</li>
<li><strong>Path</strong> \(P_n\): a path on \(n\) vertices.</li>
<li><strong>Bipartite graph</strong>: vertex set partitioned into \(X \cup Y\) with all edges between \(X\) and \(Y\); equivalent to being 2-colorable.</li>
<li><strong>Complete bipartite graph</strong> \(K_{m,n}\): all \(mn\) edges between parts of size \(m\) and \(n\).</li>
</ul></div>

![A basic undirected graph](/pics/co342/graph-basic.png)

*A simple undirected graph on 6 labeled vertices: the fundamental combinatorial object.*

![A bipartite graph K_{3,2}](/pics/co342/bipartite-def.png)

*The complete bipartite graph \(K_{3,2}\): every vertex on the left is adjacent to every vertex on the right.*

![A directed graph](/pics/co342/digraph-basic.png)

*A directed graph (digraph): edges have orientation, represented by arrows.*

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 2.1.5</strong> (Connectivity). Vertices \(u, v \in V(G)\) are <strong>connected</strong> if there is a walk from \(u\) to \(v\). The graph \(G\) is connected if every pair of vertices is connected. Connectedness is an equivalence relation on \(V\); its equivalence classes induce the <strong>components</strong> of \(G\).</div>

A key fact: a bipartite graph is characterized by having no odd cycles. The BFS 2-coloring check assigns alternating colors during a breadth-first traversal; a contradiction (adjacent vertices of the same color) occurs precisely when an odd cycle is found.

![BFS-based 2-colorability check](/pics/co342/bfs-coloring.gif)

*BFS coloring animation: vertices are colored alternately as the BFS frontier expands. If a conflict arises, the graph is not bipartite.*

## Chapter 3: Connectedness

Connectivity measures how "robustly" a graph is connected: how many vertices or edges must be removed to disconnect it. This is crucial in network reliability, VLSI design, and algorithm complexity.

### Connectivity and Cut Vertices

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 3.1.1</strong> (\(k\)-Connectivity). A graph \(G\) is <strong>\(k\)-connected</strong> (for \(k \geq 1\)) if \(|V(G)| > k\) and there is no set \(X \subseteq V(G)\) with \(|X| < k\) such that \(G - X\) is disconnected. The <strong>connectivity</strong> \(\kappa(G)\) is the largest \(k\) for which \(G\) is \(k\)-connected.</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 3.1.2</strong> (Cut Vertex). A vertex \(v\) is a <strong>cut vertex</strong> of \(G\) if \(G - v\) is disconnected (or has more components than \(G\)). Equivalently, there exist vertices \(a, b\) such that \(v\) lies on every \(a\)–\(b\) path.</div>

Key equivalences: being 1-connected is the same as being connected (and not a singleton). Being 2-connected means connected with no cut vertex (with the exception of the single-edge graph \(K_2\)).

![2-connected graph and graph with cut vertex](/pics/co342/connectivity-def.png)

*Left: a 2-connected graph (no cut vertex). Right: vertex \(v_3\) in red is a cut vertex — removing it disconnects the graph into two triangles.*

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 3.2.1</strong> (Ear Decomposition). A loopless graph \(G\) is 2-connected if and only if it can be built starting from a circuit by successively <strong>adding paths</strong> (called <strong>ears</strong>): at each step, a path \(P\) with both endpoints on the current graph (but no internal vertices) is attached.</div>

This constructive characterization is very useful: it shows that 2-connected graphs have a "layered" structure that can be built incrementally.

### The Block Graph

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 3.3.1</strong> (Block). A <strong>block</strong> of \(G\) is a maximal connected subgraph with no cut vertex. Each block is either 2-connected, a single edge (bridge), or an isolated vertex.</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 3.3.2</strong> (Block Graph). The <strong>block-cut tree</strong> (or block graph) of a connected graph \(G\) is a bipartite graph with parts \(B\) (blocks) and \(K\) (cut vertices), where a block is adjacent to a cut vertex iff the cut vertex is in that block. This is always a tree.</div>

The block-cut tree is a beautiful structural decomposition: it reveals exactly how the 2-connected pieces are joined together at cut vertices, like beads on a string.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 3.3.1.</strong> Every graph is the union of its blocks. Any two distinct blocks share at most one vertex (which is necessarily a cut vertex).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof (intersection size).</strong> If blocks \(B_1, B_2\) share two distinct vertices \(x, y\), then \(B_1 \cup B_2\) is a connected subgraph: since neither \(B_1\) nor \(B_2\) has a cut vertex, any vertex \(w\) in \(B_1 \cup B_2\) minus any single vertex remains connected, yielding a larger 2-connected subgraph and contradicting maximality of \(B_1\) and \(B_2\). \(\square\)</div>

### Contractions and Tutte's Theorem

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 3.4.1</strong> (Edge Contraction). For an edge \(e = \{u,v\} \in E(G)\), the graph \(G/e\) is obtained by <strong>contracting</strong> \(e\): delete \(u\) and \(v\), add a new vertex \(x_{uv}\), and connect \(x_{uv}\) to every former neighbor of \(u\) or \(v\). Any edges parallel to \(e\) become loops.</div>

Contraction is the dual of subdivision. Together with deletion, it generates the minor relation, which underlies some of the deepest theorems in graph theory (Robertson-Seymour).

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 3.4.3.</strong> If \(G\) is 2-connected, \(e \in E(G)\), and \(|V(G)| > 3\), then at least one of \(G/e\) or \(G - e\) is 2-connected.</div>

The analogous statement for 3-connectivity is Tutte's Wheel Theorem:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 3.4.9</strong> (Tutte's Wheel Theorem). Let \(G\) be a 3-connected graph with \(|V(G)| > 4\). Then \(G\) has an edge \(e\) such that \(G/e\) is also 3-connected.</div>

This theorem implies that every 3-connected graph can be built from a wheel \(W_k\) by successively splitting vertices or adding edges — a precise analogue of the ear decomposition for 2-connectivity.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 3.4.5.</strong> Every \(k\)-connected graph has minimum vertex degree at least \(k\).</div>

### Menger's Theorem

Menger's theorem (1927) is the cornerstone of connectivity theory. It establishes a min-max duality between paths and separators, deeply related to the max-flow min-cut theorem in network flows.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 3.5.1</strong> (Internally Disjoint Paths). Two \(a\)–\(b\) paths are <strong>internally disjoint</strong> if they share no internal vertices (and if \(a,b\) are adjacent, no edges either).</div>

![Three internally vertex-disjoint paths between s and t](/pics/co342/menger-paths.png)

*Three internally vertex-disjoint \(s\)–\(t\) paths (colored differently). Menger's theorem guarantees this is possible whenever no set of fewer than 3 vertices separates \(s\) from \(t\).*

![A separating set of 3 vertices](/pics/co342/menger-cut.png)

*A vertex cut \(\{x_1, x_2, x_3\}\) (red) that blocks all \(s\)–\(t\) paths. Menger's theorem says the minimum such cut size equals the maximum number of internally disjoint paths.*

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem 3.5.1</strong> (Menger I). For \(|V(G)| > k\), the following are equivalent:
<ol>
<li>\(G\) is \(k\)-connected.</li>
<li>For every pair \(a \neq b \in V(G)\), there are \(k\) internally disjoint \(a\)–\(b\) paths.</li>
</ol></div>

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem 3.5.2</strong> (Menger II). Let \(a, b\) be distinct vertices of \(G\) and \(k \geq 1\). Exactly one of the following holds:
<ol>
<li>There exist \(k\) internally disjoint \(a\)–\(b\) paths.</li>
<li>There is a set \(X \subseteq V(G) \setminus \{a,b\}\) with \(|X| < k\) such that \(G - X\) contains no \(a\)–\(b\) path.</li>
</ol></div>

The most general form, Menger III (also called the global version), deals with sets \(A, B \subseteq V(G)\):

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem 3.5.4</strong> (Menger III). Given \(A, B \subseteq V(G)\) and integer \(k \geq 1\), exactly one of the following holds:
<ol>
<li>There are \(k\) vertex-disjoint \(A\)–\(B\) paths in \(G\).</li>
<li>There is some \(X \subseteq V(G)\) with \(|X| < k\) such that \(G - X\) has no \(A\)–\(B\) path.</li>
</ol>
Moreover, the maximum number of vertex-disjoint \(A\)–\(B\) paths equals the minimum size of an \(A\)–\(B\) separator.
</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Induct on \(|E(G)|\). If \(G\) has only loops, every \(A\)–\(B\) separator contains \(A \cap B\), which itself is one. For a non-loop edge \(e = \{u,v\}\), apply the inductive hypothesis to \(G/e\). Carefully track how the contracted vertex \(x_e\) interacts with the sets \(A, B\) (via sets \(A', B'\) in the contracted graph) to lift the result back to \(G\). The key technical step shows \(x_e\) must lie in any minimum separator of \(G/e\), then "uncontracting" gives a separator of size exactly \(k\) in \(G\), from which \(k\) disjoint \(AX\)-paths and \(XB\)-paths are assembled into \(k\) disjoint \(AB\)-paths. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma 3.5.5</strong> (Fan Lemma). If \(a \in V(G)\) and \(B \subseteq V(G) \setminus \{a\}\), then either there are \(k\) paths from \(a\) to \(B\) that are pairwise disjoint except at \(a\) (a "\(k\)-fan"), or there is a set \(X \subseteq V(G) \setminus \{a\}\) with \(|X| < k\) separating \(a\) from \(B\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 3.5.6.</strong> If \(G\) is \(k\)-connected (\(k \geq 2\)) and \(A \subseteq V(G)\) with \(|A| \leq k\), then \(G\) contains a circuit passing through every vertex of \(A\).</div>

## Chapter 4: Planar Graphs

Planar graphs are those that can be drawn in the plane without edge crossings. They arise naturally in geography (maps), VLSI chip design, and network layout. Euler's formula and Kuratowski's theorem together give a complete picture of planarity.

### Plane Graphs and Embeddings

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 4.1.1</strong> (Plane Graph). A <strong>plane graph</strong> is a pair \(G = (V, E)\) where \(V \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) is a finite set and each edge is an arc (a curve homeomorphic to \([0,1]\)) whose endpoints are in \(V\), with arc interiors pairwise disjoint and disjoint from \(V\).</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 4.1.2</strong> (Planar Graph). A graph is <strong>planar</strong> if it has a plane drawing. A plane drawing is also called a <strong>planar embedding</strong>.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 4.1.2</strong> (Thomassen). The class of graphs admitting plane drawings with curved edges equals the class admitting drawings with polygonal (straight-segment) edges.</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 4.2.1</strong> (Faces). The <strong>faces</strong> of a plane graph are the connected components of \(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus G\). Every plane graph has exactly one unbounded face, called the <strong>outer face</strong>.</div>

![A planar drawing with labeled faces](/pics/co342/planar-drawing.png)

*A planar graph drawn without crossings. The faces \(f_1, f_2, f_3\) are the bounded regions; \(f_\infty\) is the unbounded outer face. Here \(v=4, e=5, f=3\) and \(4-5+3=2\).*

The Jordan Curve Theorem for polygons states that a circuit drawn in the plane divides it into exactly two faces. This is the topological backbone of planarity theory.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 4.2.6.</strong> In a plane graph \(G\): a cut edge (bridge) lies on the boundary of exactly one face; every non-bridge edge lies on the boundary of exactly two distinct faces.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 4.2.8.</strong> In a 2-connected loopless plane graph, every face boundary is a circuit.</div>

### Euler's Formula

Euler's formula is the fundamental identity relating the combinatorial structure of a planar graph to its topology. Discovered by Euler in 1752, it was one of the first theorems in topology.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 4.3.1</strong> (Euler's Formula). If \(G\) is a connected plane graph, then \[|V(G)| - |E(G)| + |F(G)| = 2\] where \(|F(G)|\) is the number of faces of \(G\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Induct on \(|E(G)|\). If \(G\) is a tree (no circuits), then \(|E| = |V| - 1\) and \(|F| = 1\) (only the outer face), giving \(|V| - (|V|-1) + 1 = 2\). Otherwise, \(G\) has an edge \(e\) in a circuit. Then \(G - e\) is connected, and removing \(e\) merges two faces into one, so \(|F(G-e)| = |F(G)| - 1\). By induction applied to \(G - e\): \(|V| - (|E|-1) + (|F|-1) = 2\), hence \(|V| - |E| + |F| = 2\). \(\square\)</div>

![Euler's formula illustrated on a planar graph](/pics/co342/euler-formula.png)

*\(v=5\), \(e=7\), \(f=4\) (including the outer face): \(5-7+4 = 2\). Euler's formula holds.*

A powerful consequence is an edge count bound:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 4.3.3.</strong> If \(G\) is a simple planar graph with \(|V(G)| \geq 3\), then \[|E(G)| \leq 3|V(G)| - 6.\] If \(G\) is also triangle-free, then \(|E(G)| \leq 2|V(G)| - 4\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> If \(G\) is a forest the bound is obvious. Otherwise, let \(X = \{(f,e) : e \text{ is on the boundary of face } f\}\). Each face has boundary length \(\geq 3\) (since \(G\) is simple), so \(|X| \geq 3|F|\). Each edge borders at most 2 faces, so \(|X| \leq 2|E|\). Thus \(|F| \leq \frac{2}{3}|E|\). Substituting into Euler's formula \(2 = |V| - |E| + |F| \leq |V| - |E| + \frac{2}{3}|E|\) gives \(|E| \leq 3|V| - 6\). For triangle-free graphs, use the sharper bound that each face has boundary length \(\geq 4\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary 4.3.3.1.</strong> \(K_5\) and \(K_{3,3}\) are non-planar.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> \(K_5\): \(|V|=5, |E|=10\), but \(3|V|-6 = 9 < 10\). \(K_{3,3}\): \(|V|=6, |E|=9\), triangle-free, but \(2|V|-4 = 8 < 9\). \(\square\)</div>

![K5 drawn attempting planarity](/pics/co342/k5-nonplanar.png)

*\(K_5\) has 10 edges, exceeding the bound \(3 \times 5 - 6 = 9\). It cannot be planar.*

![K33 drawn with labeled bipartition](/pics/co342/k33-nonplanar.png)

*\(K_{3,3}\) is triangle-free with 9 edges, exceeding the bound \(2 \times 6 - 4 = 8\). It cannot be planar.*

### Subdivisions and Graph Minors

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 4.4.1</strong> (Subdivision). A <strong>subdivision</strong> of a graph \(H\) is obtained by replacing each edge with a path (inserting new degree-2 vertices along each edge). If \(H\) is non-planar, so is any subdivision of \(H\), and so is any graph containing a subdivision of \(H\) as a subgraph.</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 4.6.1</strong> (Minor). A graph \(G\) has \(H\) as a <strong>minor</strong> if \(H\) can be obtained from \(G\) by deleting vertices, deleting edges, and contracting edges. Equivalently, \(G\) has an \(H\)-minor iff the vertices of \(H\) can be mapped to connected vertex-disjoint subgraphs of \(G\), with adjacencies respected.</div>

The distinction between topological minors (subdivisions) and minors (contractions) is subtle but important. Topological minors are the more restrictive notion:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 4.6.3.</strong> If \(G\) has \(H\) as a topological minor, then \(G\) has \(H\) as a minor.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 4.6.4.</strong> \(G\) has \(K_5\) or \(K_{3,3}\) as a topological minor if and only if \(G\) has \(K_5\) or \(K_{3,3}\) as a minor.</div>

The proof uses the fact that both \(K_5\) and \(K_{3,3}\) have maximum degree \(\leq 4\) and \(3\) respectively, so their minors can be "expanded" back into topological copies.

![A K3,3 subdivision inside the Petersen graph](/pics/co342/kuratowski-subdivision.png)

*The Petersen graph contains a subdivision of \(K_{3,3}\) (red terminals). This proves the Petersen graph is non-planar via Kuratowski's theorem.*

### Kuratowski's Theorem

Kuratowski's theorem (1930) gives a complete characterization of planar graphs — arguably the most celebrated theorem in graph theory.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem 4.7.1</strong> (Kuratowski's Theorem). The following are equivalent for any graph \(G\):
<ol>
<li>\(G\) is planar.</li>
<li>\(G\) contains no topological minor in \(\{K_5, K_{3,3}\}\).</li>
<li>\(G\) contains no minor in \(\{K_5, K_{3,3}\}\).</li>
</ol></div>

The key step is the 3-connected case:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma 4.7.4.</strong> If \(G\) is 3-connected and has no minor in \(\{K_5, K_{3,3}\}\), then \(G\) is planar.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Take a minimal counterexample. By Tutte's Wheel Theorem, there is an edge \(e = \{u,v\}\) such that \(G/e\) is 3-connected. Since \(G/e\) has no \(K_5\) or \(K_{3,3}\) minor (or else \(G\) would), the minimality assumption gives a planar embedding of \(G/e\). Let \(C\) be the circuit bounding the face containing \(x_e\) (the contracted vertex) in this embedding. Apply the Circle Lemma to \(A = N_G(u) \cap V(C)\) and \(B = N_G(v) \cap V(C)\). The four outcomes of the Circle Lemma yield: a separator of size \(\leq 2\) (contradicting 3-connectedness), a \(K_5\)-topological-minor, a \(K_{3,3}\)-topological-minor, or a planar drawing of \(G\). The first three contradict our assumptions, so \(G\) must be planar, a contradiction. \(\square\)</div>

For the general (not necessarily 3-connected) case, one reduces to the 3-connected case by showing that non-3-connected planar graphs can be built from planar pieces:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 4.7.5.</strong> If \(G\) is planar: (1) \(G\) has an embedding with any specified vertex on the outer face; (2) for any adjacent pair \(u, v\), there is an embedding where the \(uv\)-edge lies on the outer face.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 4.7.6</strong> (Wagner's Theorem). If \(G\) has no \(K_{3,3}\)-minor and no \(K_5\)-minor, then \(G\) is planar.</div>

#### Facial Circuit Characterization for 3-Connected Graphs

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem 4.5.2.</strong> If \(G\) is a simple 3-connected plane graph, a circuit \(C\) of \(G\) is the boundary of a face if and only if \(C\) is:
<ul>
<li><strong>induced</strong> (chordless), and</li>
<li><strong>non-separating</strong>: \(G - C\) is connected.</li>
</ul></div>

This is a purely combinatorial characterization — it does not depend on the choice of planar embedding! For 3-connected planar graphs, the embedding is essentially unique (by Whitney's theorem), so the facial circuits are combinatorially determined.

#### Algorithmic Planarity Testing

Planarity can be tested in \(O(n)\) time (Hopcroft-Tarjan, 1974). The approach in these notes is:

<div class="key-box">
<strong>Planarity Testing Algorithm (sketch):</strong>

```
1. Check if G is 3-connected (O(n²) via pairwise vertex deletion + BFS).
2. If G is 3-connected:
   a. Find edge e such that G/e is 3-connected (Tutte's theorem guarantees existence).
   b. Recursively test G/e.
   c. If G/e is planar, use Circle Lemma to either find a K5/K33 minor or embed G.
3. If not 3-connected:
   a. Handle cut vertices and 2-separators by splitting into simpler pieces.
   b. Embed pieces separately and combine using Proposition 4.7.5.
```
</div>

#### Straight-Line Embeddings

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 4.9.1</strong> (Fáry's Theorem, 1948). Every simple planar graph has a planar embedding in which every edge is a straight line segment.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 4.9.2</strong> (Tutte's Spring Theorem). Every 3-connected simple planar graph has an embedding where all edges are straight line segments and all faces are convex polygons (a "convex embedding").</div>

The spring embedding is constructed as a linear system: fix the outer face as a convex polygon, then set each interior vertex to the average (centroid) of its neighbors' positions. This system has a unique solution, and Tutte's theorem proves the resulting drawing is planar with convex faces.

## Chapter 5: Matchings (Advanced)

### General Matchings and the Tutte-Berge Formula

In Chapter 1 we handled bipartite matchings via Hall's theorem. For general (non-bipartite) graphs, the situation is more complex because odd cycles prevent perfect matchings in a subtle way.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 5.3.1.</strong> If \(S \subseteq V(G)\) and \(G - S\) has more odd components than \(|S|\), then \(G\) has no perfect matching.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Each odd component must send at least one matching edge to \(S\). Since at most \(|S|\) matching edges touch \(S\), at most \(|S|\) odd components can be accommodated. If there are more than \(|S|\) odd components, at least one odd component has a vertex not matched to \(S\), and since the component is odd, some vertex is unmatched. \(\square\)</div>

Tutte's theorem (1947) shows this condition is also sufficient:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary 5.3.4.1</strong> (Tutte's Theorem). \(G\) has a perfect matching if and only if \(\text{odd}(G - S) \leq |S|\) for every \(S \subseteq V(G)\).</div>

The Tutte-Berge formula generalizes this to compute the size of a maximum matching:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem 5.3.3</strong> (Tutte-Berge Formula). For any graph \(G\): \[\nu(G) = \frac{1}{2} \min_{S \subseteq V(G)} \bigl(|V(G)| + |S| - \text{odd}(G - S)\bigr).\]</div>

The minimizing set \(S\) is called a **Berge witness** or **Tutte-Berge witness**. It certifies that no larger matching can exist: the formula says we have exactly \(\frac{1}{2}(\text{odd}(G-S) - |S|)\) unmatched vertices in any maximum matching.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary 5.3.4.2</strong> (Petersen's Theorem, 1891). Every 3-regular (cubic) graph with no cut edge has a perfect matching.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Apply Tutte's theorem. For any \(S \subseteq V(G)\), each odd component of \(G - S\) must have at least 3 edges to \(S\) (since it has odd order, the number of outgoing edges is odd; since the graph has no cut edge, \(|F| \geq 3\)). So \(3 \cdot \text{odd}(G-S) \leq |\text{edges between } S \text{ and odd components}| \leq 3|S|\), giving \(\text{odd}(G-S) \leq |S|\). \(\square\)</div>

### Matching Structure: Gallai-Edmonds

The Gallai-Edmonds decomposition (independently by Gallai and Edmonds, 1960s) gives the complete structural picture of maximum matchings.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 5.4.2</strong> (Avoidable Vertex). A vertex \(v \in V(G)\) is <strong>avoidable</strong> if \(\nu(G-v) = \nu(G)\) — some maximum matching does not saturate \(v\).</div>

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition 5.4.1</strong> (Gallai-Edmonds Partition). Define:
<ul>
<li>\(D(G)\) = the set of avoidable vertices</li>
<li>\(A(G)\) = the set of vertices not in \(D(G)\) with at least one neighbor in \(D(G)\)</li>
<li>\(C(G)\) = \(V(G) \setminus (A(G) \cup D(G))\)</li>
</ul></div>

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem 5.4.1</strong> (Gallai-Edmonds Theorem). With the partition \(D, A, C\) defined above:
<ol>
<li>\(A(G)\) is a Berge witness: \(\nu(G) = \frac{1}{2}(|V(G)| + |A(G)| - \text{odd}(G - A(G)))\).</li>
<li>Every odd component of \(G - A(G)\) is hypomatchable (has a matching leaving any one vertex unmatched) and is contained in \(D(G)\).</li>
<li>Every even component of \(G - A(G)\) has a perfect matching and is contained in \(C(G)\).</li>
</ol></div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition 5.3.4</strong> (Hypomatchable). A graph \(G\) is <strong>hypomatchable</strong> if \(G - u\) has a perfect matching for every \(u \in V(G)\). Equivalently, \(\nu(G - u) = \nu(G)\) for all \(u\). Hypomatchable graphs necessarily have odd order.</div>

The proof uses a clever "twin relation" \(u \sim v\) defined by \(\nu(G - \{u,v\}) < \nu(G)\), showing it is an equivalence relation (the non-trivial part is transitivity), then analyzing the equivalence classes.

### The Blossom Algorithm

The Blossom Algorithm (Edmonds, 1965) finds a maximum matching in a general graph in polynomial time — a landmark in combinatorial optimization.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition 5.6.1</strong> (Alternating Tree). An <strong>\(M\)-alternating tree</strong> rooted at an exposed vertex \(u\) is a tree \(T \subseteq G\) such that:
<ul>
<li>The \(u\)–\(v\) path in \(T\) is \(M\)-alternating for every \(v \in V(T)\).</li>
<li>Every leaf of \(T\) is at even distance from \(u\).</li>
</ul>
Vertices at even distance from \(u\) are <strong>outer vertices</strong>; those at odd distance are <strong>inner vertices</strong>.</div>

<div class="key-box">
<strong>Blossom Algorithm (high-level):</strong>

```
Input: Graph G, initial matching M (possibly empty).
Output: A maximum matching M*.

1. Build an M-alternating forest F rooted at all exposed vertices.
2. For each outer vertex u and edge uv:
   a. If v is exposed: we found an augmenting path → augment M, restart.
   b. If v is an outer vertex in a different tree: augmenting path found → augment M, restart.
   c. If v is an outer vertex in the SAME tree:
      → Found a blossom B (odd cycle). Contract B to a single vertex.
      → Recursively find an augmenting path in G/B.
      → Lift the path back to G (expand the blossom).
   d. If v is an inner vertex: ignore (already handled).
3. If no augmenting path found: M is maximum (by Berge's theorem).
```
</div>

The subtle case (2c) is the "blossom" contraction — the key insight that makes the algorithm work for non-bipartite graphs. Without it, odd cycles would confuse the alternating path search.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Proposition 5.6.1.</strong> Given any matching \(M\) in a graph \(G\), one can efficiently find either:
<ol>
<li>An \(M\)-augmenting path, or</li>
<li>An \(M\)-alternating forest \(F\) such that every \(M\)-exposed vertex is a root of \(F\), every outer vertex of \(F\) has all its neighbors in \(F\), and every neighbor of an outer vertex is inner in the same tree (no augmenting path).</li>
</ol></div>

#### Slither: A Game-Theoretic Application

The Gallai-Edmonds structure has a beautiful application to combinatorial game theory via the game of **Slither**: two players alternately extend a path by choosing an adjacent edge; the player who cannot move loses.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 5.5.1.</strong> If \(G\) has a perfect matching \(M\), the first player (Alice) can force a win by always following the matching edge of the newly added vertex.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 5.5.2.</strong> If \(G\) is hypomatchable, the second player (Bob) can force a win by the symmetric strategy.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition 5.5.3.</strong> If \(C(G) \neq \emptyset\), Alice wins (the first player to move an edge into \(D(G)\) loses). If \(C(G) = \emptyset\), the analysis reduces recursively to the Gallai-Edmonds partition of \(A(G)\).</div>

This elegant application shows how matching theory reaches into other areas of combinatorics — a hallmark of the subject's depth.
