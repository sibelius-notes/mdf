## Chapter 1: Graphs and Digraphs

Network flow is the study of how to route goods, information, or any measurable quantity through a network in an efficient way. Before we can state any problems precisely, we need a solid foundation in the two central combinatorial objects: undirected graphs and directed graphs. This chapter lays that groundwork and establishes the vocabulary that permeates the entire course.

### 1.1 Undirected Graphs

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Graph). An <strong>undirected graph</strong> \(G = (V, E)\) consists of a finite set \(V\) of <strong>vertices</strong> and a set \(E\) of unordered pairs of distinct elements of \(V\) called <strong>edges</strong>.</div>

The **degree** of a vertex \(v\), written \(\deg(v)\), is the number of edges incident to \(v\). A **walk** is a sequence of vertices \(v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_k\) such that \(\{v_i, v_{i+1}\} \in E\) for each \(i\). A **path** is a walk with no repeated vertices. A **cycle** is a closed walk \(v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_k = v_0\) with no repeated internal vertices. The graph \(G\) is **connected** if there is a path between every pair of vertices.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Cut in an undirected graph). Let \(G = (V, E)\) be a graph and \(S \subseteq V\). The <strong>cut</strong> induced by \(S\) is the set
\[\delta(S) := \{e \in E : e \text{ has exactly one endpoint in } S\}.\]
If \(s \in S\) and \(t \notin S\), we call \(\delta(S)\) an <strong>s,t-cut</strong>.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> There is an s,t-path if and only if every s,t-cut is non-empty.</div>

A **tree** is a connected acyclic graph. A **forest** is an acyclic graph (possibly disconnected). A **spanning tree** of \(G\) is a subgraph that is a tree and includes all vertices of \(V\).

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> A tree with \(n\) vertices has exactly \(n-1\) edges. If \(T\) is a tree and \(u,v \in V(T)\) are non-adjacent, then \(T + uv\) contains exactly one cycle \(C\). Moreover, for any \(xy \in C\), the graph \(T + uv - xy\) is again a spanning tree.</div>

This "tree exchange" property is fundamental: it underlies the simplex pivot in the network simplex method we study later.

### 1.2 Directed Graphs (Digraphs)

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Digraph). A <strong>directed graph</strong> (or <strong>digraph</strong>) \(D = (N, A)\) consists of a finite set \(N\) of <strong>nodes</strong> and a set \(A\) of ordered pairs of distinct nodes called <strong>arcs</strong>. For an arc \(uv \in A\), \(u\) is the <strong>tail</strong> and \(v\) is the <strong>head</strong>.</div>

![Directed graph with labeled arcs and vertices](/pics/co351/digraph-def.png)
*A digraph \(D = (N, A)\) with five nodes and six directed arcs. Each arc points from tail to head.*

The **out-degree** of node \(v\) is the number of arcs leaving \(v\); the **in-degree** is the number arriving. A **di-walk** is a sequence of nodes \(v_0, \ldots, v_k\) where each \(v_i v_{i+1} \in A\). A **di-path** has no repeated nodes; a **di-cycle** is a closed di-walk with no repeated internal nodes.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Cut in a digraph). Let \(D = (N, A)\) and \(S \subseteq N\). The <strong>forward cut</strong> is
\[\delta(S) := \{uv \in A : u \in S,\, v \notin S\}\]
and the <strong>backward cut</strong> is \(\delta(\bar{S}) := \{uv \in A : u \notin S,\, v \in S\}\). If \(s \in S\) and \(t \notin S\), then \(\delta(S)\) is an <strong>s,t-cut</strong>.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Let \(D = (N, A)\) and \(s, t \in N\). There is an s,t-dipath if and only if every s,t-cut is non-empty.</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> (\(\Rightarrow\)) Any s,t-dipath must contain at least one arc from every s,t-cut by definition.

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Let \(S\) be the set of all nodes reachable from \(s\) by a di-path. If \(t \in S\), we are done. Otherwise \(\delta(S)\) is an s,t-cut which, by assumption, is non-empty. Any arc \(uv \in \delta(S)\) with \(u \in S\) leads to \(v \notin S\) reachable from \(s\) — contradicting the definition of \(S\). \(\square\)</div>

### 1.3 The Underlying Graph and Cycles in Digraphs

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Underlying graph). The <strong>underlying graph</strong> of \(D = (N, A)\) is the undirected graph \(G = (N, E)\) obtained by ignoring arc directions.</div>

![A digraph alongside its underlying undirected graph](/pics/co351/underlying-graph.png)
*Left: digraph \(D\) with directed arcs. Right: its underlying graph \(G\) with directions removed.*

A **cycle in a digraph** is a cycle in its underlying graph. Given an orientation of a cycle \(C\), arcs pointing in the direction of traversal are **forward arcs** and those opposing it are **backward arcs**. A digraph is **connected** if its underlying graph is connected, and **strongly connected** if every pair of nodes is mutually reachable via di-paths.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Spanning tree of a digraph). A <strong>spanning tree</strong> of \(D\) is a spanning tree of its underlying graph.</div>

Spanning trees of digraphs will be essential as bases for the network simplex method: a spanning tree on \(n\) nodes has exactly \(n-1\) arcs and, crucially, admits a unique path between every pair of nodes in the underlying graph.

### 1.4 Node-Arc Incidence Matrix

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Incidence Matrix). For \(D = (N, A)\), the <strong>node-arc incidence matrix</strong> \(M\) is the \(|N| \times |A|\) matrix with entries
\[M_{v, uv} = \begin{cases} -1 & \text{if } v = u \text{ (arc leaves } v\text{)} \\ +1 & \text{if } v \text{ is the head of arc } uv \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]</div>

Every column of \(M\) has exactly one \(+1\) and one \(-1\). Summing all rows gives the zero vector, so the rows are linearly dependent and \(\text{rank}(M) \leq |N| - 1\). When \(D\) is connected, the rank is exactly \(|N| - 1\).

**Key propositions on linear dependence:**

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> The columns of \(M\) corresponding to a cycle are linearly dependent.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Let \(C\) be a cycle and \(M_C\) the submatrix of columns corresponding to arcs of \(C\). Form \(M_C'\) by multiplying backward-arc columns by \(-1\), so all arcs appear as forward arcs in a directed cycle. In \(M_C'\), each node in \(C\) has exactly one \(+1\) and one \(-1\) in its row. Summing all columns of \(M_C'\) gives the zero vector, so the columns of \(M_C\) are linearly dependent. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> A set of linearly dependent columns of \(M\) must include a cycle.</div>

Together these propositions give the fundamental theorem:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> A set of \(|N|-1\) columns of \(M\) forms a basis if and only if the corresponding arcs form a spanning tree of \(D\).</div>

## Chapter 2: The Transshipment Problem

The **transshipment problem** (TP) is the simplest and most fundamental network flow model. Imagine a set of depots (some with surplus goods, some with deficit) connected by a road network. We want to route goods at minimum cost to satisfy all demands. This chapter develops the LP formulation, its dual, and the network simplex algorithm — a geometrically beautiful specialization of the simplex method exploiting the tree structure of bases.

### 2.1 Problem Formulation

Let \(D = (N, A)\) be a connected digraph, \(b \in \mathbb{R}^N\) be **node demands** (where \(b_v > 0\) means node \(v\) supplies \(b_v\) units and \(b_v < 0\) means it demands \(|b_v|\) units), and \(w \in \mathbb{R}^A\) be **arc costs**. We assume \(\sum_{v \in N} b_v = 0\) (total supply equals total demand).

A **flow** is a vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^A\) satisfying **flow conservation** at every node: inflow minus outflow equals the demand.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Transshipment Problem). The transshipment problem is
\[\min\; w^T x \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x(\delta(\bar{v})) - x(\delta(v)) = b_v\;\;\forall v \in N,\quad x \geq 0.\]
Equivalently, in matrix form: \(\min\; w^Tx\) s.t. \(Mx = b,\; x \geq 0\).</div>

![Transshipment network with supply/demand labels](/pics/co351/transshipment.png)
*A transshipment network. Nodes with \(b > 0\) (yellow) are suppliers; nodes with \(b < 0\) (red) are consumers; \(b = 0\) nodes (grey) are pure transit points.*

### 2.2 Dual LP and Complementary Slackness

The dual of the TP has one variable \(y_v\) for each node \(v\):

\[\max\; y^T b \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_v - y_u \leq w_{uv}\;\;\forall uv \in A,\quad y \text{ free}.\]

Since the primal constraints are equalities, the dual variables are free (unconstrained). The dual is interpreted as a **node potential**: imagine \(y_v\) as the "price" of goods at node \(v\).

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Reduced Cost). Given a node potential \(y \in \mathbb{R}^N\), the <strong>reduced cost</strong> of arc \(uv\) is \(\bar{w}_{uv} := w_{uv} + y_u - y_v\).</div>

A potential is **feasible** (dual feasible) if \(\bar{w}_{uv} \geq 0\) for all arcs \(uv \in A\).

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Complementary Slackness for TP). Primal \(x\) and dual \(y\) are simultaneously optimal if and only if for each arc \(uv \in A\):
\[x_{uv} = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \bar{w}_{uv} = 0.\]</div>

**Economic interpretation:** The reduced cost \(\bar{w}_{uv} = w_{uv} + y_u - y_v\) represents the net cost of buying one unit at \(u\) for price \(y_u\), shipping it at cost \(w_{uv}\), and selling at \(v\) for price \(y_v\). If \(\bar{w}_{uv} > 0\), shipping through \(uv\) is unprofitable, so we never use it (\(x_{uv} = 0\)). If \(\bar{w}_{uv} < 0\), it is profitable, so we want as much flow as possible through it.

### 2.3 Bases are Spanning Trees

A fundamental structural theorem links LP bases for the TP to combinatorial objects:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> A set of \(|N| - 1\) columns of the incidence matrix \(M\) forms a basis if and only if the corresponding arcs form a spanning tree of \(D\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Columns corresponding to a cycle are linearly dependent (the sum of forward arc columns minus backward arc columns is zero), so any basis must be cycle-free. Conversely, any \(|N|-1\) acyclic connected arc set spans all of \(\mathbb{R}^{|N|-1}\) (the column space of \(M\)), hence forms a basis. \(\square\)</div>

A **tree flow** (basic feasible solution) is determined by a spanning tree \(T\): we set non-tree arcs to zero and solve for the tree arc flows iteratively from the leaves inward. At each leaf \(v\) of \(T\), exactly one arc is incident, so its flow is forced to be \(b_v\).

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Tree Flow). A <strong>basic feasible solution</strong> of the TP is a spanning tree \(T\) together with a feasible flow \(x\) where \(x_e = 0\) for all \(e \notin T\). This is called the <strong>tree flow</strong>.</div>

![Tree flow solution showing tree and non-tree arcs](/pics/co351/spanning-tree-flow.png)
*A tree flow: bold blue arcs form the spanning tree \(T\), and dashed grey arcs are non-tree arcs with flow 0. Labels show flow/capacity.*

**Note:** Not every spanning tree yields a feasible flow. For a tree to give a basic feasible solution, the iterative leaf-deletion computation must produce non-negative flows on all tree arcs.

### 2.4 Network Simplex Algorithm for the TP

The **network simplex method** applies the simplex algorithm to the TP, exploiting the tree-basis structure at every step.

**Maintaining the dual:** For a given spanning tree \(T\), we compute potentials \(y\) by setting \(y_{\text{root}} = 0\) for some fixed root node and propagating via \(\bar{w}_{uv} = 0\) for all tree arcs \(uv\): i.e., \(y_v = y_u + w_{uv}\) if \(uv \in T\) (or \(y_v = y_u - w_{vu}\) if \(vu \in T\)).

**Finding an entering arc:** Compute \(\bar{w}_{uv}\) for all non-tree arcs. If any \(\bar{w}_{uv} < 0\), the solution is not optimal; that arc **enters the basis**.

**Finding a leaving arc:** Adding \(uv\) to \(T\) creates exactly one cycle \(C\). Orient \(C\) so that \(uv\) is a forward arc. Increasing flow by \(t\) on \(uv\) requires adding \(t\) to all forward arcs of \(C\) and subtracting \(t\) from all backward arcs. The maximum feasible increase is \(t = \min\{x_{pq} : pq \text{ backward in } C\}\). The backward arc attaining this minimum **leaves the basis**: \(T \leftarrow T + uv - pq\).

<div class="key-box">

```
Network Simplex Algorithm for TP
─────────────────────────────────
Input:  Connected digraph D=(N,A), arc costs w, node demands b,
        initial spanning tree T with feasible tree flow x.
1. Compute node potentials y: set y_root = 0, then for each
   arc uv in T set y_v = y_u + w_uv (or y_v = y_u - w_vu).
2. For each non-tree arc uv, compute w̄_uv = w_uv + y_u - y_v.
3. While ∃ non-tree arc uv with w̄_uv < 0:
   a. T + uv forms a unique cycle C;
      orient C so uv is a forward arc.
   b. Let t = min{x_pq : pq backward arc of C}.
      Let pq attain this minimum.
   c. x_e += t for forward arcs of C;
      x_e -= t for backward arcs of C.
   d. T ← T + uv - pq.
   e. Recompute potentials and reduced costs. Go to 3.
4. Return x (optimal tree flow).
```

</div>

### 2.5 Economic Interpretation of Dual Potentials

Node potentials have a natural economic interpretation. Think of \(y_v\) as the market price of a commodity at node \(v\). The reduced cost \(\bar{w}_{uv} = w_{uv} + y_u - y_v\) is the profit/loss from buying one unit at \(u\), transporting it at cost \(w_{uv}\), and selling at \(v\):
- \(\bar{w}_{uv} > 0\): lose money — don't ship (set \(x_{uv} = 0\)).
- \(\bar{w}_{uv} < 0\): make money — ship as much as possible.
- \(\bar{w}_{uv} = 0\): break even — any flow level is equally optimal.

### 2.6 Unboundedness

The algorithm may fail to find a leaving arc when all backward arcs of the cycle \(C\) have zero flow — equivalently, the entering arc's flow can increase without bound. This happens precisely when \(D\) contains a **negative di-cycle**.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Negative Di-Cycle). A <strong>negative di-cycle</strong> is a directed cycle \(C\) with \(\sum_{e \in C} w_e < 0\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> A feasible TP is unbounded if and only if \(D\) contains a negative di-cycle.</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> (\(\Leftarrow\)) Let \(C\) be a negative di-cycle with \(w(C) < 0\) and let \(x^*\) be a feasible flow. Define \(x_C\) to be \(t\) on arcs of \(C\) and 0 elsewhere. Then \(x^* + x_C\) is feasible (flow conservation holds since \(C\) is a cycle) and has cost \(w^T x^* + t \cdot w(C) \to -\infty\) as \(t \to \infty\). So the TP is unbounded.

(\(\Rightarrow\)) If no leaving arc is found, the entering arc \(uv\) creates a cycle \(C\) with all forward arcs. The potentials along \(C\) sum to give \(\bar{w}_{uv} = \sum_{e \in C} w_e < 0\), so \(C\) is a negative di-cycle. \(\square\)</div>

### 2.7 Finding an Initial BFS

To start the simplex algorithm we need a feasible tree flow. The standard technique introduces an **auxiliary digraph**.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Auxiliary TP). Given \(D = (N, A)\) and \(b \in \mathbb{R}^N\), the auxiliary digraph \(D' = (N', A')\) is:
\begin{align*}
N' &= N \cup \{z\}, \quad b'(z) = 0,\quad b'(v) = b(v)\;\forall v \in N \\
A' &= A \cup \{vz : v \in N,\, b(v) < 0\} \cup \{zv : v \in N,\, b(v) > 0\} \\
w'(e) &= \begin{cases} 0 & e \in A \\ 1 & e \in A' \setminus A \end{cases}
\end{align*}</div>

The auxiliary TP has a natural feasible flow: route all supply through \(z\) and out to demand nodes. This flow is the initial BFS.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> A TP is feasible if and only if its auxiliary TP has optimal value 0.</div>

### 2.8 Feasibility Characterization

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> A TP with digraph \(D = (N, A)\) and node demands \(b \in \mathbb{R}^N\) is infeasible if and only if there exists \(S \subseteq N\) such that \(\delta(S) = \emptyset\) and \(b(S) < 0\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> (\(\Rightarrow\)) If the TP is infeasible, the auxiliary TP has an optimal solution with positive value. In the optimal tree flow for the auxiliary TP, set \(y_z = 0\). Nodes connected to \(z\) via arcs of cost 1 get potentials \(\pm 1\); the rest propagate via cost-0 arcs. Partition \(N\) into \(S^+ = \{v : y_v = 1\}\) and \(S^- = \{v : y_v = -1\}\). A reduced-cost argument shows \(\delta(S^-) = \emptyset\) in \(D\) and \(b(S^-) < 0\).

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Suppose such \(S\) exists with \(\delta(S) = \emptyset\) and \(b(S) < 0\). For any feasible flow \(x\):
\[0 > b(S) = \sum_{v \in S}[x(\delta(\bar{v})) - x(\delta(v))] = x(\delta(\bar{S})) - x(\delta(S)) = x(\delta(\bar{S}))\]
since \(\delta(S) = \emptyset\). But \(x \geq 0\) implies \(x(\delta(\bar{S})) \geq 0\) — contradiction. \(\square\)</div>

## Chapter 3: Minimum-Cost Flow

The **minimum-cost flow problem** (MCFP) generalizes the transshipment problem by adding **capacity constraints**: each arc \(e\) can carry at most \(c_e\) units of flow. This single addition dramatically widens the modeling power — airline scheduling, catering logistics, bipartite matching, and matrix rounding all reduce to MCFP instances.

### 3.1 LP Formulation and Its Dual

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Minimum-Cost Flow Problem). Given \(D = (N, A)\), arc costs \(w \in \mathbb{R}^A\), arc capacities \(c \in \mathbb{R}^A_+\), and node demands \(b \in \mathbb{R}^N\) with \(\sum b_v = 0\):
\[\min\; w^T x \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x(\delta(\bar{v})) - x(\delta(v)) = b_v\;\forall v,\quad 0 \leq x_e \leq c_e\;\forall e \in A.\]</div>

![Min-cost flow problem with cost and capacity labels](/pics/co351/mcfp-example.png)
*An MCFP instance: each arc is labeled with its cost \(w\) and capacity \(c\). We seek a flow satisfying demands at minimum total cost.*

We introduce slack variables \(s_e = c_e - x_e \geq 0\) for each arc, converting the upper-bound constraints to equalities. The augmented LP has the dual:

\[\max\; b^T y - c^T z \quad \text{s.t.} \quad -y_u + y_v - z_{uv} \leq w_{uv},\quad z \geq 0.\]

The reduced cost takes the same form as before: \(\bar{w}_{uv} = w_{uv} + y_u - y_v\). The dual constraint rewrites as \(z_{uv} \geq -\bar{w}_{uv}\), and since we minimize \(c^T z\) (with \(c > 0\)), the optimal \(z\) satisfies \(z_{uv} = \max\{0, -\bar{w}_{uv}\}\). Complementary slackness then yields:

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (CS Conditions for MCFP). A primal-dual pair \((x, y)\) is optimal if and only if for all arcs \(uv \in A\):
<ol>
<li>\(\bar{w}_{uv} < 0 \Rightarrow x_{uv} = c_{uv}\)</li>
<li>\(\bar{w}_{uv} > 0 \Rightarrow x_{uv} = 0\)</li>
<li>\(0 < x_{uv} < c_{uv} \Rightarrow \bar{w}_{uv} = 0\)</li>
</ol></div>

**Economic interpretation:** If the reduced cost is negative, shipping through that arc is profitable, so saturate it; if positive, unprofitable, so use it not at all; if zero, any intermediate flow level is equally optimal.

Note that MCFP cannot be unbounded since capacities \(c_e\) provide an upper bound on total flow.

### 3.2 Basis Structure for MCFP

The augmented matrix of the MCFP LP has rank \(|N| - 1 + |A|\), so a basis has exactly \(|N| + |A| - 1\) elements. Each arc \(e\) contributes either \(x_e\) alone, \(s_e\) alone, or both to the basis:

- **Case 1** (\(s_e\) in basis, \(x_e\) not): \(x_e = 0\).
- **Case 2** (\(x_e\) in basis, \(s_e\) not): \(x_e = c_e\) (arc saturated).
- **Case 3** (both in basis): arc carries an intermediate flow level.

By counting — let \(k\) be the number of Case 3 arcs — there are \(2k + |A| - k = k + |A|\) basic variables total, which must equal \(|N| + |A| - 1\), giving \(k = |N| - 1\). These \(|N|-1\) arcs form a spanning tree (by the same cycle argument as for TP). The remaining arcs in Cases 1 and 2 are fixed at 0 or \(c_e\).

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Tree Flow in MCFP). A <strong>basic feasible solution</strong> for MCFP consists of a spanning tree \(T\) together with a feasible flow \(x\) where every non-tree arc has \(x_e \in \{0, c_e\}\).</div>

### 3.3 Network Simplex for MCFP

The network simplex for MCFP extends the TP version by handling both "free" and "saturated" non-tree arcs. We search for a **violating arc**:
- A non-tree arc \(e\) with \(\bar{w}_e < 0\) and \(x_e = 0\) (should be increased to \(c_e\)), or
- A non-tree arc \(e\) with \(\bar{w}_e > 0\) and \(x_e = c_e\) (should be decreased to 0).

In either case, adding \(e\) to \(T\) creates a cycle. We push flow around the cycle, respecting both \(x \geq 0\) and \(x \leq c\). The amount pushed is \(t = \min\bigl(\{c_f - x_f : f \text{ forward arc of } C\},\; \{x_f : f \text{ backward arc of } C\}\bigr)\). The leaving arc is the one that first reaches 0 or \(c_e\).

<div class="key-box">

```
Network Simplex Algorithm for MCFP
────────────────────────────────────
Input:  D=(N,A), costs w, capacities c, demands b,
        initial spanning tree T with tree flow x (non-tree arcs at 0 or c_e).
1. Compute potentials y from T (same as TP).
2. Compute w̄_e for all non-tree arcs e.
3. While ∃ violating arc e = uv:
   Case A: w̄_e < 0 and x_e = 0 → orient C so e is a forward arc.
   Case B: w̄_e > 0 and x_e = c_e → orient C so e is a backward arc.
   a. t = min({c_f - x_f : f forward}, {x_f : f backward})
   b. Let f be the arc attaining t.
   c. Update flows along C (add t to forward, subtract from backward).
   d. T ← T + e - f.  Recompute potentials and reduced costs.
4. Return x (optimal flow).
```

</div>

### 3.4 Integrality of MCFP

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Integrality). If an MCFP has an optimal solution and all capacities and node demands are integers, then there exists an integral optimal solution.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Since all \(b_v\) are integers, we can find an initial integral basic feasible solution. In each iteration of the network simplex, the update \(t = \min\{x_{pq}, \ldots\}\) involves integer flows and integer capacities, so \(t\) remains an integer. Therefore, at every iteration, all flows remain integers, and the final optimal solution is integral. \(\square\)</div>

### 3.5 Feasibility of MCFP

Use the same auxiliary digraph as for the TP, but the new arcs have infinite capacity (or a large finite bound). The feasibility characterization is:

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> An MCFP is infeasible if and only if there exists \(S \subseteq N\) such that \(b(S) > c(\delta(\bar{S}))\) (more demand in \(S\) than in-capacity) or \(b(S) < -c(\delta(S))\) (more supply in \(S\) than out-capacity).</div>

### 3.6 Applications

**Minimum Bipartite Perfect Matching.** Given a bipartite graph \(G = (A \cup B, E)\) with \(|A| = |B|\) and edge costs, we want a minimum-cost perfect matching. Model as MCFP: direct each edge from \(A\) to \(B\); set \(b_v = -1\) for \(v \in A\), \(b_v = +1\) for \(v \in B\); set all capacities to 1. By integrality, the optimal solution has \(x_e \in \{0,1\}\), giving exactly a perfect matching.

**Airline Scheduling.** A plane visits cities \(1, \ldots, n\). There are \(b_{ij}\) potential passengers from city \(i\) to \(j\) (\(i < j\)), ticket price \(f_{ij}\), and plane capacity \(P\). Model:
- Nodes \(1, \ldots, n\) with arcs along the route for the plane (capacity \(P\), cost 0).
- For each passenger group \((i,j)\), a node with demand \(-b_{ij}\) connected to the plane route at city \(i\) and to a sink at city \(j\) with arc cost \(-f_{ij}\) (negative = revenue).
The minimum-cost flow assigns passengers to the plane or leaves them unserved.

**Catering Problem.** A caterer needs \(b_i\) clean napkins each day \(i = 1, \ldots, n\). New napkins cost \(\alpha\); used napkins can be returned via 1-day service (cost \(\beta\)) or 2-day service (cost \(\gamma\)); storage is free. Model each day as a node, dirty napkins as supply, clean napkins as demand, and the three treatment options as arcs connecting "dirty" nodes to "clean" nodes on the appropriate day. The minimum-cost flow gives the optimal napkin procurement strategy.

**Matrix with Consecutive 1's.** The LP \(\min w^T x\) s.t. \(Ax \geq b\), \(x \geq 0\), where each column of \(A\) has a contiguous block of 1's, can be transformed via successive row operations (subtracting adjacent rows) into an incidence matrix LP — hence it reduces to an MCFP and can be solved by network simplex.

## Chapter 4: Shortest Paths

Although the network simplex can solve shortest path problems as a special case of the TP, it is not polynomial-time. Dedicated shortest-path algorithms exploit the special structure to achieve much better efficiency. This chapter develops three algorithms — Ford's (general), Bellman-Ford (handles negative costs, detects negative cycles), and Dijkstra's (fast for non-negative costs) — together with a rigorous LP-duality perspective.

### 4.1 Problem and LP Formulation

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Shortest s,t-Dipath Problem). Given \(D = (N, A)\), arc costs \(w \in \mathbb{R}^A\), and distinct nodes \(s, t \in N\), find a minimum-cost s,t-dipath (or determine that none exists or the problem is unbounded due to a negative di-cycle).</div>

The **characteristic vector** \(x^P \in \mathbb{R}^A\) of a path \(P\) has \(x^P_e = 1\) for \(e \in A(P)\) and 0 otherwise. The LP formulation treats the shortest path as a special TP with \(b_s = -1\), \(b_t = +1\), \(b_v = 0\) otherwise:

\[\min\; w^T x \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x(\delta(\bar{v})) - x(\delta(v)) = b_v\;\forall v,\quad x \geq 0.\]

The dual is \(\max\; y_t - y_s\) subject to \(y_v - y_u \leq w_{uv}\) for all \(uv \in A\), where \(\bar{w}_{uv} = w_{uv} + y_u - y_v \geq 0\) is the dual feasibility condition (an **equality arc** has \(\bar{w}_{uv} = 0\)).

**Key theorem on LP structure:**

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> If there are no negative di-cycles, then any integral feasible solution \(x\) to the LP decomposes as \(x = x^P + x^{C_1} + \cdots + x^{C_k}\), where \(P\) is an s,t-dipath and \(C_1, \ldots, C_k\) are di-cycles.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Let \(F = \{e : x_e > 0\}\). Since the net flow of \(s\) is \(-1\), every s,t-cut intersects \(F\), so there is an s,t-dipath \(P\) in \(F\). Let \(x' = x - x^P\). Then \(x'\) is a circulation (\(b_v = 0\) for all \(v\)), and by an inductive longest-dipath argument, the support of \(x'\) decomposes into di-cycles. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> If there are no negative di-cycles, then the LP has an optimal solution that is the characteristic vector of a shortest s,t-dipath.</div>

### 4.2 Shortest Path Trees and Subpath Optimality

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Rooted Spanning Tree). A spanning tree \(T\) is <strong>rooted at \(s\)</strong> if for every \(v \in N\), the unique path from \(s\) to \(v\) in \(T\) is an s,v-dipath in \(D\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> A spanning tree \(T\) is rooted at \(s\) if and only if the in-degree of \(s\) is 0 and the in-degree of every other node is exactly 1 in \(T\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Subpath Optimality). Suppose no negative di-cycles exist. If \(v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\) is a shortest \(v_1, v_k\)-dipath, then \(v_1, \ldots, v_i\) is a shortest \(v_1, v_i\)-dipath for every \(i \leq k\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Since there are no negative di-cycles, the LP has an optimal solution that is the characteristic vector of the \(v_1, v_k\)-dipath \(P\). The corresponding optimal dual \(y\) has all arcs of \(P\) as equality arcs (by CS conditions). For the subpath \(P' = v_1, \ldots, v_i\), the same \(y\) is dual feasible for the \(v_1, v_i\)-shortest path LP, and all arcs of \(P'\) are equality arcs. So the CS conditions hold for \(P'\), making it optimal. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Predecessor and Predecessor Digraph). In a spanning tree \(T\) rooted at \(s\), the <strong>predecessor</strong> of node \(v \neq s\) is the unique node \(u\) with \(uv \in T\), written \(p_v = u\). The <strong>predecessor digraph</strong> \(D_p\) has node set \(N\) and arc set \(\{p_v v : v \neq s\}\).</div>

### 4.3 Ford's Algorithm (General Framework)

Ford's algorithm is a general iterative scheme applicable even with negative arc costs.

<div class="key-box">

```
Ford's Algorithm
──────────────────
Input:  D=(N,A), arc costs w, source s.
        (Assume all nodes reachable from s.)
1. Set y_s = 0, y_v = ∞ for all v ≠ s.
   Set predecessor p_v = ∅ for all v.
2. While ∃ arc uv with w̄_uv = w_uv + y_u - y_v < 0:
   a. y_v ← y_u + w_uv.   ("correct" the arc uv)
   b. p_v ← u.
3. Return y (shortest distances) and D_p (shortest-path tree).
```

</div>

**Key properties:**

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> Throughout Ford's algorithm, \(\bar{w}_e \leq 0\) for all arcs \(e \in A(D_p)\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> When \(v\) is corrected via arc \(uv\), we have \(\bar{w}_{uv} = 0\). Subsequently, \(y_v\) does not increase (corrections only decrease potentials), and if \(y_u\) decreases later, \(\bar{w}_{uv} = w_{uv} + y_u - y_v\) only decreases further. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> If \(D_p\) contains a di-cycle at any point, then \(D\) contains a negative di-cycle and the algorithm does not terminate. Similarly, if \(s\) acquires a predecessor, then \(D\) contains a negative di-cycle.</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> If Ford's algorithm terminates, then \(D_p\) is a spanning tree of shortest dipaths rooted at \(s\), and \(y_v = d(s,v)\) for all \(v\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> At termination, \(D_p\) has no cycle and \(s\) has no predecessor, so \(D_p\) is a rooted spanning tree. All arcs in \(D_p\) are equality arcs (since \(\bar{w}_e \leq 0\) throughout and \(y\) is feasible at termination). For any \(v\), the s,v-dipath \(P\) in \(D_p\) satisfies: \(x^P\) is primal feasible and \(y\) is dual feasible with CS conditions holding on all arcs of \(P\). By LP duality, \(y_v - y_s = y_v = d(s,v)\). \(\square\)</div>

### 4.4 Bellman-Ford Algorithm

Bellman-Ford makes Ford's algorithm efficient by scanning all arcs in passes.

<div class="key-box">

```
Bellman-Ford Algorithm
──────────────────────
Input:  D=(N,A), arc costs w, source s.
1. y_s = 0, y_v = ∞ for v ≠ s; p_v = ∅; i = 0.
2. While i < |N| - 1:
   a. For each arc uv ∈ A:
      if w̄_uv < 0 then y_v ← y_u + w_uv; p_v ← u.
   b. i ← i + 1.
3. If any w̄_uv < 0 remains: D has a negative di-cycle.
   Else: y_v = d(s,v) for all v reachable from s.
```

</div>

**Running time:** \(O(|N| \cdot |A|)\).

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Bellman-Ford Correctness). Suppose no negative di-cycles exist. After the \(i\)-th pass, if the shortest s,v-dipath uses at most \(i\) arcs, then \(y_v = d(s,v)\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> By induction on \(i\). Let \(d_v = d(s,v)\).

First note that at any point, \(y_v \geq d_v\): at initialization \(\infty \geq d_v\); when \(y_v \neq \infty\), trace \(v\) back to \(s\) in \(D_p\). Since \(\bar{w}_e \leq 0\) for all \(e \in D_p\), summing gives \(w(P) - y_v \leq 0\) so \(y_v \geq w(P) \geq d_v\).

Base case \(i = 0\): trivial. Inductive step: pick \(v\) with a shortest path using at most \(i+1\) arcs \(s = v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_{i+1} = v\). By subpath optimality and induction, \(y_{v_i} = d_{v_i}\) after pass \(i\). In pass \(i+1\):
- If \(\bar{w}_{v_i v} = 0\): already \(y_v = y_{v_i} + w_{v_i v} = d_v\) and doesn't change.
- If \(\bar{w}_{v_i v} > 0\): then \(y_v < d_v\) — contradicts the lemma.
- If \(\bar{w}_{v_i v} < 0\): the algorithm corrects and sets \(y_v = y_{v_i} + w_{v_i v} = d_v\). \(\square\)
</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary.</strong> At the end of Bellman-Ford: if \(y\) is feasible, then \(y_v = d(s,v)\) for all \(v\). If \(y\) is not feasible, a negative di-cycle exists.</div>

**Application — Currency Exchange:** Given currencies with exchange rates \(r_{uv}\), a profitable cycle exists iff \(\prod_{e \in C} r_e > 1\), i.e., \(\sum_{e \in C} (-\log r_e) < 0\). Set arc costs to \(-\log r_e\) and run Bellman-Ford to detect a negative di-cycle.

### 4.5 Dijkstra's Algorithm

When all arc costs are non-negative, we can exploit a greedy strategy: raise the potentials of unsettled nodes by \(t\) at each step while maintaining feasibility.

**Motivation:** With \(w \geq 0\), setting \(y = 0\) is a feasible dual potential. We maintain feasibility by carefully choosing how much to raise potentials. For an arc \(uv\) with \(u\) settled and \(v\) unsettled, the reduced cost \(\bar{w}_{uv}\) decreases as \(y_v\) increases. We pick the arc \(uv \in \delta(N(T))\) with minimum \(\bar{w}_{uv}\) and raise all unsettled potentials by \(\bar{w}_{uv}\), making \(uv\) an equality arc. Adding \(v\) to the tree settles it permanently.

<div class="key-box">

```
Dijkstra's Algorithm
──────────────────────
Input:  D=(N,A), non-negative costs w ≥ 0, source s.
1. Initialize y_v = 0 for all v; T = {s} (settled set).
2. While T is not a spanning tree of N:
   a. Find arc uv ∈ δ(N(T)) minimizing w̄_uv = w_uv + y_u - y_v.
   b. Set y_z ← y_z + w̄_uv for all z ∉ N(T).
   c. Add arc uv and node v to T (settle v).
3. Output y_v = d(s,v) for all v.
```

</div>

![A weighted digraph with shortest path tree highlighted](/pics/co351/shortest-path-tree.png)
*A shortest path tree (blue arcs) found by Dijkstra's algorithm. Grey arcs were not selected. Node labels show the optimal distance from s.*

![Dijkstra's algorithm animated step by step](/pics/co351/dijkstra.gif)
*Animation: Dijkstra's algorithm. Green nodes are settled (permanently optimized), blue nodes are unsettled. The frontier expands one node at a time.*

**Correctness:** The four cases for how \(\bar{w}_{uv}\) changes when we increment \(y_z\) for \(z \notin N(T)\):
1. Both \(u, v \notin N(T)\): both increase by \(t\), so \(\bar{w}_{uv}\) unchanged.
2. Both \(u, v \in N(T)\): neither changes, \(\bar{w}_{uv}\) unchanged.
3. \(u \notin N(T)\), \(v \in N(T)\): \(y_u\) increases, \(\bar{w}_{uv}\) increases — still feasible.
4. \(u \in N(T)\), \(v \notin N(T)\): \(y_v\) increases, \(\bar{w}_{uv}\) decreases — the minimum such arc becomes an equality arc and is added to \(T\).

All arcs in \(T\) are equality arcs at all times, and \(y\) is always feasible. The LP argument then gives \(y_v = d(s,v)\) upon termination.

**Running time:** \(O(|N| \log |N| + |A|)\) with a Fibonacci heap.

**Applications:**
- **Network Reliability:** Each arc has reliability \(r_e \in (0,1]\). Maximize \(\prod_{e \in P} r_e\) by minimizing \(\sum_{e \in P} (-\log r_e)\). Since \(-\log r_e \geq 0\), Dijkstra applies.
- **Currency Exchange:** To detect a profitable exchange cycle, use Bellman-Ford (negative di-cycle detection) with costs \(-\log r_e\).

## Chapter 5: Maximum Flow and Min-Cut

Maximum flow asks: given a source \(s\) and sink \(t\) in a capacitated network, what is the greatest amount of material we can push from \(s\) to \(t\) without violating any capacity? The celebrated **Max-Flow Min-Cut theorem** provides a deep and beautiful answer.

### 5.1 LP Formulation

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Maximum Flow). Given \(D = (N, A)\) with capacities \(c \geq 0\) and nodes \(s, t \in N\), the <strong>maximum flow problem</strong> is:
\[\max\; x(\delta(s)) - x(\delta(\bar{s})) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x(\delta(\bar{v})) - x(\delta(v)) = 0\;\forall v \neq s,t,\quad 0 \leq x_e \leq c_e.\]</div>

This is a special case of MCFP (set \(b_s = -f\), \(b_t = f\), all other \(b_v = 0\) and maximize \(f\)).

![s-t flow network with capacity labels](/pics/co351/flow-network.png)
*An s-t flow network with capacity labels on arcs. The source \(s\) (left) and sink \(t\) (right) are highlighted.*

### 5.2 Residual Graph and Augmenting Paths

Given a flow \(x\), the **residual graph** \(D'\) captures where we can push additional flow:

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Residual Graph). For each arc \(uv \in A\):
<ul>
<li>If \(x_{uv} < c_{uv}\): add forward arc \(uv\) to \(D'\) with residual capacity \(c_{uv} - x_{uv}\).</li>
<li>If \(x_{uv} > 0\): add backward arc \(vu\) to \(D'\) with residual capacity \(x_{uv}\).</li>
</ul></div>

![A flow network and its residual graph side by side](/pics/co351/residual-graph.png)
*Left: a flow network with arc labels flow/capacity. Right: the residual graph — blue arcs are forward residuals, red dashed arcs are backward residuals.*

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Augmenting Path). An <strong>augmenting path</strong> is an s,t-dipath in the residual graph \(D'\).</div>

![Residual graph with an augmenting path highlighted in red](/pics/co351/augmenting-path.png)
*An augmenting path (red) in the residual graph. Pushing flow along this path increases the total flow.*

### 5.3 Ford-Fulkerson Algorithm

<div class="key-box">

```
Ford-Fulkerson Algorithm
──────────────────────────
Input:  Digraph D=(N,A), capacities c, source s, sink t.
1. Initialize x_e = 0 for all e ∈ A. Form residual graph D'.
2. While ∃ s,t-dipath (augmenting path) P in D':
   a. Let γ = min{residual capacity on P}.
   b. Augment x along P by γ:
      • Forward arcs: x_e += γ.
      • Backward arcs: x_e -= γ.
   c. Update D'.
3. Return x (maximum flow).
```

</div>

![Ford-Fulkerson algorithm steps animated](/pics/co351/ford-fulkerson.gif)
*Animation: Ford-Fulkerson on a 6-node network. Red arcs show the augmenting path at each iteration. Watch the flow labels update after each augmentation.*

**Termination:**
- If \(c\) is integer-valued, each augmentation increases flow by at least 1, so the algorithm terminates.
- If \(c\) is rational, scale to integer capacities.
- If \(c\) is irrational, Ford-Fulkerson may cycle forever.

Even for integer capacities, the algorithm may require \(|f^*|\) iterations (pseudopolynomial). The Edmonds-Karp variant fixes this.

### 5.4 Edmonds-Karp: Shortest Augmenting Paths

If we always augment along the path with the **fewest arcs** (found by BFS), we obtain Edmonds-Karp.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Edmonds-Karp Complexity). Edmonds-Karp terminates in \(O(|N| \cdot |A|^2)\) time.</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Let \(d_f(s,v)\) denote the BFS distance from \(s\) to \(v\) in the residual graph \(D_f\) for flow \(f\).

**Lemma:** \(d_f(s,v) \leq d_{f'}(s,v)\) for successive flows \(f, f'\) — BFS distances are non-decreasing. (Proof by contradiction: if \(d_{f'}(s,v) < d_f(s,v)\), trace back the shortest path in \(D_{f'}\); any new arc used must have been a backward arc in \(D_f\), meaning flow was pushed along it in the previous iteration, but this leads to a distance contradiction.)

**Definition:** Arc \(uv\) on an augmenting path \(P\) is **critical** if its residual equals the bottleneck of \(P\). After a critical arc \(uv\) is used, it disappears from \(D'\). Before it can reappear, flow must be pushed along \(vu\), requiring \(d_{f'}(s,u) \geq d_f(s,u) + 2\). Since BFS distances are bounded by \(|N|\), each arc can be critical at most \(|N|/2\) times. There are at most \(2|A|\) arcs, so the total iterations are \(O(|N| \cdot |A|)\), and each iteration costs \(O(|A|)\) for BFS. \(\square\)</div>

### 5.5 Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (s,t-Cut). For \(S \subseteq N\) with \(s \in S, t \notin S\), the <strong>capacity</strong> of the cut is \(c(\delta(S)) = \sum_{uv \in \delta(S)} c_{uv}\).</div>

![An s-t cut illustrated with dashed boundary](/pics/co351/st-cut.png)
*An s,t-cut with \(S\) (yellow) and \(T = N \setminus S\) (blue). Red arcs cross the cut.*

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> For any s,t-flow \(x\) and any s,t-cut \(\delta(S)\), the value of \(x\) is at most \(c(\delta(S))\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> The net flow out of \(s\) equals the net flow out of \(S\) (since all intermediate nodes have zero net flow). Then:
\[\text{value} = x(\delta(s)) - x(\delta(\bar{s})) = x(\delta(S)) - x(\delta(\bar{S})) \leq x(\delta(S)) \leq c(\delta(S)). \quad \square\]</div>

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Max-Flow Min-Cut). The maximum value of an s,t-flow equals the minimum capacity of an s,t-cut:
\[\max\text{-flow} = \min\text{-cut}.\]</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> Let \(x\) be a maximum s,t-flow (Ford-Fulkerson terminates since capacities are finite and the LP is feasible). Since there is no augmenting path, let \(S = \{v : \exists \text{ s,v-dipath in } D'\}\). Then \(s \in S\), \(t \notin S\), and \(\delta_{D'}(S) = \emptyset\).

- For \(uv \in \delta_D(S)\): \(uv\) is not in \(D'\) so \(x_{uv} = c_{uv}\).
- For \(uv \in \delta_D(\bar{S})\): \(vu\) is not in \(D'\) so \(x_{uv} = 0\).

Therefore flow value \(= x(\delta(S)) - x(\delta(\bar{S})) = c(\delta(S)) - 0 = c(\delta(S))\). By the lemma, this cut is minimum and the flow is maximum. \(\square\)</div>

![Minimum cut illustration with cut capacity labeled](/pics/co351/mincut-illustration.png)
*The minimum cut for this network: the red arcs cross from S (yellow) to T (blue), and their capacities sum to the minimum cut capacity.*

### 5.6 Flow Decomposition

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Flow Decomposition). Given integer arc capacities, if \(x\) is an integral s,t-flow of value \(k\), then \(x\) is the characteristic vector of \(k\) s,t-dipaths and a collection of di-cycles.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> If \(k = 0\), \(x\) is a circulation. For \(k > 0\): since the net flow out of \(s\) is \(k\), every s,t-cut has positive flow, so there is an s,t-dipath \(P\) with \(x^P_e \leq x_e\). Then \(x - x^P\) is an integral s,t-flow of value \(k-1\); by induction we are done. \(\square\)</div>

### 5.7 Combinatorial Applications of Max-Flow Min-Cut

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Menger's Theorem, Arc-Disjoint). The maximum number of arc-disjoint s,t-dipaths equals the minimum number of arcs whose removal disconnects \(s\) from \(t\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> If there are \(k\) arc-disjoint dipaths, we need at least one arc from each to disconnect \(s\) from \(t\), so max \(\geq\) min. For equality: apply max-flow min-cut with capacities \(c = 1\). The integer max-flow value \(k\) equals the min-cut size. By flow decomposition, the flow corresponds to \(k\) arc-disjoint s,t-dipaths (since each arc has capacity 1, no arc appears in two paths). Removing all arcs in the min-cut \(\delta(S)\) disconnects \(s\) from \(t\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Menger's Theorem, Node-Disjoint). If \(st \notin A\), the maximum number of node-disjoint s,t-dipaths equals the minimum size of an s,t-separator (a set \(X \subseteq N \setminus \{s,t\}\) whose removal disconnects \(s\) from \(t\)).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Split each node \(v \neq s,t\) into two nodes \(v^-\) and \(v^+\) connected by arc \(v^- v^+\) of capacity 1; map each original arc \(uv\) to arc \(u^+ v^-\) with capacity \(\infty\). Apply the arc-disjoint Menger's theorem. A min-cut can only use finite-capacity arcs, i.e., the node-splitting arcs, which correspond exactly to an s,t-separator. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (König's Theorem). In a bipartite graph \(G = (A \cup B, E)\), the size of a maximum matching equals the size of a minimum vertex cover: \(\nu(G) = \tau(G)\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Direct edges from \(A\) to \(B\), add source \(s\) with arcs to each \(a \in A\) (capacity 1), and sink \(t\) with arcs from each \(b \in B\) (capacity 1). A max-flow corresponds to a max matching. For the min s,t-cut \(\delta(S)\): let \(K = (A \setminus S) \cup (B \cap S)\). This is a vertex cover of size equal to the cut capacity, so \(\tau(G) \leq \nu(G)\). The trivial inequality \(\nu(G) \leq \tau(G)\) gives equality. \(\square\)</div>

### 5.8 Flows with Lower Bounds

We can enrich the model with **lower bounds** \(\ell_e \geq 0\): a feasible flow must satisfy \(\ell_e \leq x_e \leq c_e\). The residual graph for a flow \(x\) has: forward arcs with capacity \(c_e - x_e\), backward arcs with capacity \(x_e - \ell_e\) (preventing flow from dropping below \(\ell_e\)).

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> For any s,t-flow \(x\) with lower bounds and any s,t-cut \(\delta(S)\), the value of \(x\) is at most \(c(\delta(S)) - \ell(\delta(\bar{S}))\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Generalized Max-Flow Min-Cut). Given lower bounds, the maximum value of a feasible s,t-flow equals \(\min_S \{c(\delta(S)) - \ell(\delta(\bar{S}))\}\) (provided a feasible solution exists).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Feasibility with Lower Bounds). The network is infeasible if and only if there exists an s,t-cut \(\delta(S)\) such that \(c(\delta(S)) < \ell(\delta(\bar{S}))\).</div>

### 5.9 Preflow-Push Algorithm

The preflow-push (push-relabel) algorithm achieves better worst-case complexity by working with an infeasible **preflow** that satisfies the optimality condition throughout, until it becomes a feasible flow.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (Preflow and Excess). An s,t-<strong>preflow</strong> is a function \(0 \leq x \leq c\) with excess \(\mathrm{ex}(v) = x(\delta(\bar{v})) - x(\delta(v)) \geq 0\) for all \(v \neq s, t\). A height function \(h: N \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\) is <strong>compatible</strong> with a preflow if:
<ul>
<li>\(h(s) = |N|\), \(h(t) = 0\)</li>
<li>\(h(v) \leq h(u) + 1\) for every arc \(uv\) in the residual graph (no arc points steeply downward)</li>
</ul></div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If a preflow \(x\) and heights \(h\) are compatible, then the residual graph \(D'\) has no s,t-dipath.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Suppose \(P: s = v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_k = t\) is an s,t-dipath in \(D'\). By compatibility, \(h(v_{i+1}) \geq h(v_i) - 1\) for each \(i\). Summing: \(h(t) \geq h(s) - k\), i.e., \(0 \geq |N| - k\), so \(k \geq |N|\). But \(P\) has at most \(|N|-1\) arcs — contradiction. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="key-box">

```
Preflow-Push (Push-Relabel) Algorithm
──────────────────────────────────────
Input:  D=(N,A), capacities c, source s, sink t.
Initialization:
  • h(s) = |N|, h(v) = 0 for all v ≠ s.
  • x_e = c_e for all e ∈ δ_D(s); x_e = 0 otherwise.
Loop:
  While ∃ u ∈ N \ {s,t} with ex(u) > 0:
    If ∃ arc uv ∈ D' with h(v) = h(u) - 1:
      Push: push min{r_uv, ex(u)} along uv.
    Else:
      Relabel: h(u) ← h(u) + 1.
Return x (maximum flow).
```

</div>

**Correctness:** The algorithm maintains compatible preflow and heights throughout. At termination, no non-source, non-sink node has excess, so the preflow is a feasible flow. By the lemma, the residual graph has no s,t-dipath, so the flow is maximum.

**Termination and Complexity:**

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If \(\mathrm{ex}(u) > 0\), there is a u,s-dipath in \(D'\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Let \(T\) be the set of nodes with no u,s-dipath in \(D'\). Then \(\delta_{D'}(T) = \emptyset\), so for each arc \(uv \in \delta_{D'}(T)\): if \(vu \in A(D)\), then \(x_{vu} = 0\); if \(uv \in A(D)\), then \(x_{uv} = c_{uv}\). Computing: \(\sum_{u \in T} \mathrm{ex}(u) = x(\delta(\bar{T})) - x(\delta(T)) = 0 - c(\delta(T)) \leq 0\). Since excess is non-negative, \(\mathrm{ex}(u) = 0\) for all \(u \in T\). \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition.</strong> \(h(u) \leq 2|N| - 1\) throughout the algorithm.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Suppose \(h(u)\) is raised to \(2|N|\). Then \(\mathrm{ex}(u) > 0\), so by the lemma there is a u,s-dipath of length at most \(|N|-1\) in \(D'\). By compatibility, \(h(s) \geq h(u) - (|N|-1) \geq 2|N| - (|N|-1) = |N| + 1 > |N|\) — contradiction. \(\square\)</div>

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Preflow-Push Complexity). Preflow-push terminates in \(O(|N|^2 |A|)\) operations:
<ul>
<li>At most \(2|N|^2\) relabel operations (each node relabeled at most \(2|N|-1\) times).</li>
<li>At most \(2|N||A|\) saturating pushes (between two saturating pushes on the same arc, at least 2 relabels occur; each node relabeled at most \(2|N|\) times).</li>
<li>At most \(4|N|^2|A|\) non-saturating pushes (use the potential function \(\Phi = \sum_{v: \mathrm{ex}(v)>0} h(v)\)).</li>
</ul></div>

The total complexity is \(O(|N|^2 |A|)\), which is better than Edmonds-Karp's \(O(|N||A|^2)\) for dense graphs.

### 5.10 Applications of Maximum Flow

**Matrix Rounding.** Given a matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), round each entry to \(\lfloor M_{ij} \rfloor\) or \(\lceil M_{ij} \rceil\) such that every row sum and column sum is also rounded. Model as a flow problem: add source \(s\) and sink \(t\), nodes \(r_i\) (rows) and \(c_j\) (columns). Arc \(s r_i\) has bounds \((\lfloor \text{row}_i \rfloor, \lceil \text{row}_i \rceil)\); arc \(c_j t\) has bounds \((\lfloor \text{col}_j \rfloor, \lceil \text{col}_j \rceil)\); arc \(r_i c_j\) has bounds \((\lfloor M_{ij} \rfloor, \lceil M_{ij} \rceil)\). By integrality, a valid rounding always exists.

**Maximum Closure.** A **closure** is a set \(S \subseteq N\) in a digraph with \(\delta(S) = \emptyset\) (closed under outgoing arcs). Given weights \(w: N \to \mathbb{R}\), find a maximum-weight closure. Add source \(s\) and sink \(t\); for \(w_v > 0\), arc \(sv\) with capacity \(w_v\); for \(w_v < 0\), arc \(vt\) with capacity \(-w_v\); original arcs with capacity \(\infty\). Max closure weight \(= \sum_{w_v > 0} w_v - \min\text{-cut}\).

## Chapter 6: Global Minimum Cuts

### 6.1 Global Minimum Cut in Directed Graphs (Hao-Orlin)

A **global minimum cut** of a digraph \(D\) is a non-trivial set \(S \subsetneq N\) minimizing \(c(\delta(S))\).

**Naive approach:** Run max-flow for all \(O(|N|^2)\) source-sink pairs. A simple improvement: fix any \(s \in N\); \(s\) is on one side of the global min cut. Find the minimum s,t-cut for all \(t \in N\) (and reverse-direction cuts). This requires \(2(|N|-1)\) max-flow computations.

**Hao-Orlin** does better by solving all minimum s-cuts simultaneously using a modified preflow-push.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (s-cut, X,t-cut). An <strong>s-cut</strong> has the form \(\delta(S)\) with \(s \in S\) and \(S \neq N\). Given \(X \subseteq N\) and \(t \notin X\), an <strong>X,t-cut</strong> has the form \(\delta(S)\) with \(X \subseteq S\) and \(t \notin S\).</div>

<div class="key-box">

```
Generic Algorithm for Minimum s-Cut
──────────────────────────────────────
1. Set X = {s}.
2. While X ≠ N:
   a. Pick t ∉ X; find a minimum X,t-cut δ(S*).
   b. Store δ(S*). Add t to X.
3. Output the minimum over all stored cuts.
```

</div>

**Correctness:** Let \(\delta(S^*)\) be a global minimum s-cut. Consider the first time \(t \notin S^*\) is chosen. At that moment, \(X \subseteq S^*\), so \(\delta(S^*)\) is an X,t-cut. The algorithm finds a minimum X,t-cut \(\delta(\hat{S})\) with \(c(\delta(\hat{S})) \leq c(\delta(S^*))\). But \(\delta(\hat{S})\) is also an s-cut, so \(c(\delta(\hat{S})) \geq c(\delta(S^*))\). Hence both cuts have the same capacity.

**Hao-Orlin** implements the Generic Algorithm by modifying the preflow-push to maintain an X-preflow and track cut levels.

<div class="definition">
<strong>Definition</strong> (X-Preflow, Compatible Height). For \(X \subseteq N\), an <strong>X-preflow</strong> satisfies \(\mathrm{ex}(v) \geq 0\) for all \(v \notin X\). Heights \(h\) are <strong>compatible</strong> with an X-preflow if:
<ol>
<li>\(h(v) = |N|\) for all \(v \in X\)</li>
<li>\(h(t) \leq |X| - 1\)</li>
<li>\(h(v) \leq h(u) + 1\) for all \(uv \in A(D')\)</li>
</ol></div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Level, Cut-Level). A <strong>level</strong> \(k\) is the set \(H(k)\) of nodes with height \(k\). A <strong>cut-level</strong> is a level \(k\) where no arc in \(D'\) goes from \(H(k)\) to \(H(k-1)\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If \(\ell\) is a cut-level and \(\mathrm{ex}(v) = 0\) for all \(v\) with \(h(v) < \ell\) (except \(t\)), then \(\{v : h(v) \geq \ell\}\) is a minimum X,t-cut.</div>

<div class="key-box">

```
Hao-Orlin Algorithm
──────────────────────
Initialization:
  X = {s}; pick t ∉ X; h(s) = |N|, h(v) = 0 otherwise;
  ℓ = |N| - 1; push as much flow out of X as possible.
While X ≠ N:
  Run push-relabel with exceptions:
    (a) Only select v with ex(v) > 0 and h(v) < ℓ.
    (b) If we would relabel v and v is the only node at height h(v):
        instead set ℓ = h(v).   (maintain non-empty levels consecutive)
    (c) If we would relabel v to ℓ: relabel but reset ℓ = |N| - 1.
  When no node has ex(v) > 0 and h(v) < ℓ:
    Store cut {v : h(v) ≥ ℓ} (this is a minimum X,t-cut by the Lemma).
    Add t to X; set h(t) = |N|; push all flow out of t.
    Pick new t = node with lowest height; reset ℓ = |N| - 1.
Output minimum over all stored cuts.
```

</div>

**Key invariants maintained by Hao-Orlin:**
- Non-empty levels below \(|N|\) remain consecutive.
- \(h(t) \leq |X| - 1\) throughout (proved by tracking how \(t\) changes when \(X\) grows).
- \(\ell\) is always a cut-level.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Hao-Orlin). The global minimum s-cut can be found in \(O(|N|^2|A|)\) time — the same asymptotic complexity as a single preflow-push computation.</div>

### 6.2 Global Minimum Cut in Undirected Graphs (Karger's Algorithm)

For undirected graphs \(G = (V, E)\) with edge capacities \(c \in \mathbb{R}^E_+\), we want the minimum capacity non-trivial cut.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Edge Contraction). A <strong>contraction</strong> of edge \(e = uv\) merges \(u\) and \(v\) into a new supernode, keeping all other edges (including parallel edges, but removing self-loops).</div>

**Karger's Algorithm** repeatedly contracts a random edge (with probability proportional to its capacity) until only 2 supernodes remain. The remaining edges form a cut.

<div class="key-box">

```
Karger's Algorithm
──────────────────────
Input:  Undirected graph G=(V,E) with capacities c.
1. While |V| > 2:
   a. Select edge uv with probability c_{uv} / Σ_{e ∈ E} c_e.
   b. Contract uv: merge u,v; keep parallel edges; remove self-loops.
2. Output the cut defined by the two remaining supernodes.
```

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> For a global minimum cut \(\delta(S^*)\), the probability that Karger's algorithm outputs \(\delta(S^*)\) is at least \(\binom{|V|}{2}^{-1}\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> Use the remark that \(\sum_{e \in E} c_e = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{v \in V} c(\delta(\{v\})) \geq \frac{|V|}{2} c(\delta(S^*))\) (since \(\delta(S^*)\) is a global minimum cut, so every vertex cut has capacity at least \(c(\delta(S^*))\)).

Consider the \((k+1)\)-th iteration when \(|V|-k\) vertices remain. Assume \(\delta(S^*)\) has survived so far. The probability of contracting an edge in \(\delta(S^*)\) is:
\[\frac{c(\delta(S^*))}{\sum_{e \in E'} c_e} \leq \frac{c(\delta(S^*))}{(|V|-k)/2 \cdot c(\delta(S^*))} = \frac{2}{|V|-k}.\]

So \(\delta(S^*)\) survives iteration \(k+1\) with probability at least \(1 - \frac{2}{|V|-k}\). The algorithm runs until 2 vertices remain (\(k\) up to \(|V|-3\)). The probability \(\delta(S^*)\) survives all contractions is at least:
\[\prod_{k=0}^{|V|-3} \left(1 - \frac{2}{|V|-k}\right) = \frac{|V|-2}{|V|} \cdot \frac{|V|-3}{|V|-1} \cdots \frac{2}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{|V|(|V|-1)} = \binom{|V|}{2}^{-1}. \quad \square\]</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary.</strong> Running Karger's algorithm \(\alpha |V|^2\) times and outputting the minimum cut found gives a minimum cut with probability at least \(1 - e^{-2\alpha}\).</div>

<div class="proof">
<strong>Proof.</strong> The probability of not finding \(\delta(S^*)\) in \(\alpha|V|^2\) runs is at most:
\[\left(1 - \frac{1}{\binom{|V|}{2}}\right)^{\alpha|V|^2} \leq \left(1 - \frac{2}{|V|^2}\right)^{\alpha|V|^2} \leq e^{-2\alpha}. \quad \square\]</div>

The total cost of running \(\alpha|V|^2\) repetitions is \(O(|V|^4 \log |V|)\) — elegant and easily parallelized.

## Summary of Key Theorems and Running Times

| Algorithm | Problem | Running Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network Simplex | TP / MCFP | Not polynomial in general | Exponential worst-case; excellent in practice |
| Ford's Algorithm | Shortest paths | Pseudopolynomial / may not terminate | General negative costs; does not terminate on neg. di-cycles |
| Bellman-Ford | Shortest paths | \(O(\|N\| \cdot \|A\|)\) | Detects negative di-cycles |
| Dijkstra | Shortest paths (non-neg.) | \(O(\|N\| \log \|N\| + \|A\|)\) | Requires \(w \geq 0\); Fibonacci heap |
| Ford-Fulkerson | Max flow | Pseudopolynomial | Integer caps only; may not terminate for irrationals |
| Edmonds-Karp | Max flow | \(O(\|N\| \cdot \|A\|^2)\) | Polynomial; BFS shortest augmenting path |
| Preflow-Push | Max flow | \(O(\|N\|^2 \|A\|)\) | Better for dense graphs |
| Hao-Orlin | Global min cut (directed) | \(O(\|N\|^2 \|A\|)\) | Modification of preflow-push; finds all s-cuts |
| Karger | Global min cut (undirected) | \(O(\|V\|^4 \log \|V\|)\) (randomized) | Probability \(\geq 1 - e^{-2\alpha}\) after \(\alpha|V|^2\) runs |

The duality perspective unifies everything: in every problem, the optimality conditions are complementary slackness conditions on a primal-dual LP pair. Understanding duality is understanding why these algorithms work and when they stop.
