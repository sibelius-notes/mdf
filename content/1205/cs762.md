## Introduction

The central theme of CS 762 is **structural graph theory** in service of algorithms. Many classical problems — graph coloring, independent set, dominating set — are NP-hard in general. But for graphs belonging to special classes, efficient algorithms frequently exist. Prof. Biedl's course asks three organizing questions:

1. What graph problems become easier when the input belongs to a class \(\mathcal{C}\)?
2. How easy is it to *recognize* membership in \(\mathcal{C}\)?
3. What are the structural properties and equivalent characterizations of \(\mathcal{C}\)?

**Graph conventions.** All graphs are finite, simple, and undirected unless stated otherwise. We write \(n = |V|\) and \(m = |E|\). Graphs are stored as adjacency (incidence) lists: vertex or edge insertion costs \(O(1)\), edge deletion \(O(1)\) given a pointer, vertex deletion \(\Theta(\deg v)\).

**Warm-up: Weighted Dominating Set on Paths.** Even before studying special graph classes, dynamic programming can exploit path structure. Define:

\[
\delta(v_i, 1) = \text{weight of best dominating set in } \{v_1,\ldots,v_i\} \text{ containing } v_i
\]
\[
\delta(v_i, 0) = \text{weight of best dominating set in } \{v_1,\ldots,v_i\} \text{ NOT containing } v_i
\]

The key insight: if \(v_i \notin D\) then \(v_{i-1}\) must be in \(D\) to dominate \(v_i\). If \(v_i \in D\) then \(v_{i-1}\) is already dominated and we may freely include or exclude it. This yields a linear-time solution — the pattern of "conditioning on the separator between solved and unsolved" recurs throughout the course.

![Weighted DP on a path with table](/pics/cs762/weighted-path-dp.png)
*The DP table \(\delta(\cdot,1)\) and \(\delta(\cdot,0)\) for a weighted path graph. Optimal solution is obtained bottom-up.*

## Chapter 1: Planar Graphs

Planar graphs form the gateway into structural graph theory. They are among the oldest and most thoroughly understood graph classes, and their study motivates virtually every technique encountered later in the course: embeddings, dual constructions, separator theorems, and forbidden minor characterizations all make their first appearance here. The goal of this chapter is not merely to catalog properties of planar graphs, but to understand *why* those properties hold and how they can be exploited algorithmically.

### 1.1 Definitions and Embeddings

A **planar graph** is one that can be drawn in the plane without edge crossings. Making this precise requires separating the drawing (an analytic object) from the combinatorial information it encodes.

To work rigorously with planarity, we need to make the notion of a "drawing" mathematically precise. The following definition captures the essential requirement: edges are continuous curves, and distinct edge interiors never meet.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Planar Drawing). A <em>planar drawing</em> \(\Gamma\) assigns each vertex to a distinct point in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and each edge to a curve beginning at its two endpoints, such that the interiors of distinct edge-curves are pairwise disjoint.</div>

A planar drawing carves the plane into regions. These regions — the <em>faces</em> — are the fundamental objects of planar graph theory, and counting them correctly is what gives Euler's formula its power.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Face). A <em>face</em> of a planar drawing \(\Gamma\) is a maximal connected region of \(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \Gamma(G)\). Exactly one face is unbounded — the <em>outer face</em> \(f_\infty\).</div>

The existence of the unique unbounded outer face is a topological fact: removing a compact graph from the plane leaves exactly one unbounded connected component.

![Planar graph with labeled faces](/pics/cs762/planar-graph-def.png)
<em>A planar graph drawn without crossings. Interior faces \(f_1, f_2, f_3\) and the outer face \(f_\infty\) are labeled.</em>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Proposition</strong> (Closure under subgraph/minor). (1) Every subgraph of a planar graph is planar. (2) Adding an edge preserves planarity if both endpoints lie on the same face. (3) Edge contraction always preserves planarity. (4) Therefore any minor of a planar graph is planar.</div>

This closure under minors will become central in Chapter 6: it means that planarity is a <em>minor-closed</em> property, and by the Robertson–Seymour theorem this forces a finite forbidden minor characterization — which turns out to be exactly \(\{K_5, K_{3,3}\}\).

The combinatorial essence of a drawing is captured by the cyclic order of edges at each vertex. Two very different drawings can carry the same combinatorial information, so we want a discrete object that captures exactly this information.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Rotation System). The <em>rotation system</em> of a drawing \(\Gamma\) is the collection, for each vertex \(v\), of the clockwise cyclic order of edges incident to \(v\).</div>

A rotation system is a purely combinatorial object — a finite collection of cyclic orderings — with no reference to coordinates. Together with a choice of outer face, it completely encodes the planar embedding.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Combinatorial Embedding). A <em>combinatorial (planar) embedding</em> is a rotation system together with a designated outer face. Facial circuits can be read directly: starting at edge \(e = uv\), take the next edge after \(e\) in the clockwise order at \(v\), and continue.</div>

The combinatorial embedding is remarkably powerful: once we know the cyclic edge orders at every vertex, we can recover all faces of the drawing by following the "next edge at the far endpoint" rule, without ever consulting a geometric picture.

![All faces colored and labeled](/pics/cs762/planar-graph-faces.png)
<em>Every face — interior and exterior — can be identified from the rotation system alone.</em>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma</strong> (Re-rooting). Let \(G\) be a connected plane graph and \(F\) any face. There is a planar drawing of \(G\) with the same rotation system in which \(F\) is the outer face.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Pick a point \(p\) in \(F\) and a ray \(r\) from \(p\). If \(r\) crosses an edge \(e\) on the current outer face, reroute \(e\) around \(p\) preserving the rotation system, reducing the crossing count. Induct on crossings. \(\square\)</div>

The re-rooting lemma tells us that the designation of an "outer face" is a matter of convention rather than a deep structural distinction: every face of a connected plane graph can serve as the outer face. This flexibility is frequently exploited — for instance, in the dual graph construction and in the max-flow algorithm of Section 1.4.

### 1.2 Euler's Formula

With the basic definitions in hand, we can now derive the most celebrated and useful theorem about planar graphs. Euler's formula relates the three fundamental counts of a plane graph — vertices, edges, and faces — in a beautifully simple linear relation. That this relation is constant (equal to 2) for <em>every</em> connected plane graph is striking; it says that the topology of the drawing imposes an iron constraint on the combinatorics.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Euler's Formula). Let \(G\) be a connected plane graph with \(n\) vertices, \(m\) edges, and \(f\) faces. Then
\[ n - m + f = 2. \]
</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> By induction on \(f\). If \(f = 1\), then \(G\) contains no cycle, so \(G\) is a tree: \(m = n-1\) and \(n - (n-1) + 1 = 2\). ✓ For \(f > 1\), remove an edge \(e\) on a non-outer face. This merges two faces into one, so \(n' = n\), \(m' = m-1\), \(f' = f-1\). Apply induction. \(\square\)</div>

The proof strategy is characteristic of planar graph arguments: induct by reducing faces one at a time, maintaining the formula at each step. The base case — a tree, with exactly one face — is the anchor.

![Euler's formula annotated on a graph](/pics/cs762/euler-formula-example.png)
<em>Example: \(n=6\), \(m=9\), \(f=5\) gives \(6 - 9 + 5 = 2\). ✓</em>

Euler's formula immediately implies a tight upper bound on the number of edges in a planar graph. This edge bound is the workhorse of almost every planarity argument.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary</strong> (Edge bound). Any simple connected planar graph with \(n \geq 3\) satisfies \(m \leq 3n - 6\). Any triangle-free simple connected planar graph satisfies \(m \leq 2n - 4\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Each face is bounded by at least 3 edges (or 4 if triangle-free), each edge borders at most 2 faces: \(2m \geq 3f\). Euler: \(f = 2 - n + m\). Substituting and solving gives the bound. \(\square\)</div>

The proof uses a double-counting argument on the "face-edge incidence" — a technique that recurs throughout the course. An immediate consequence of Euler's formula is that the number of faces is a topological invariant of the graph — any two planar drawings have the same face count.

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 2.4.1.1</strong>. Let \(G\) be planar. Any planar drawing of \(G\) has the same number of faces.

</div>

The two bounds (\(m \leq 3n-6\) and \(m \leq 2n-4\)) immediately settle the non-planarity of the two forbidden minors.

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 2.4.1.3</strong>. Every simple planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 5.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 2.4.2</strong>. Every simple planar triangle-free graph with at least 3 vertices has at most \(2n - 4\) edges.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary.</strong> \(K_5\) and \(K_{3,3}\) are not planar.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> \(K_5\) has \(n=5\), \(m=10 > 3(5)-6=9\). \(K_{3,3}\) is bipartite (triangle-free) with \(n=6\), \(m=9 > 2(6)-4=8\). \(\square\)</div>

The non-planarity of \(K_5\) and \(K_{3,3}\) is more than a curiosity: Kuratowski's theorem asserts that <em>every</em> non-planar graph contains one of them as a subdivision. In this sense \(K_5\) and \(K_{3,3}\) are the complete obstruction theory for planarity.

![K5 with inevitable crossing](/pics/cs762/k5-drawing.png)
<em>\(K_5\): any planar drawing must have at least one crossing (red dashed edges). The red star marks the crossing point.</em>

![K3,3 with inevitable crossing](/pics/cs762/k33-drawing.png)
<em>\(K_{3,3}\): the complete bipartite graph on \(3+3\) vertices cannot be embedded in the plane without a crossing.</em>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Kuratowski / Wagner). A graph is planar if and only if it contains neither \(K_5\) nor \(K_{3,3}\) as a topological minor (i.e., as a subdivision).</div>

Kuratowski's theorem is one of the great gems of combinatorics: it reduces the potentially uncountable family of non-planar graphs to just two obstructions. Wagner's formulation replaces topological minors with graph minors, and both versions play roles in the course.

### 1.3 Algorithmic Consequences

The edge bound \(m \leq 3n-6\) is not only a non-planarity certificate; it is the source of several linear or near-linear-time algorithms. Every planar subgraph must satisfy the same bound, so <em>every subgraph of a planar graph has minimum degree at most 5</em>. This minimum-degree guarantee is the key to efficient greedy algorithms.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 2.4.1</strong> (Minimum-Degree Order). A vertex order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) such that \(v_i\) has minimum degree in \(G[v_1, \ldots, v_n]\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 2.4.4</strong>. Every planar graph has a minimum-degree order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) such that each \(v_i\) has at most 5 predecessors. It can be found in linear time.

</div>

**Minimum-degree order.** Since every planar subgraph also has minimum degree \(\leq 5\) (from the edge bound), we can build a vertex order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) where each \(v_i\) has at most 5 predecessors in \(O(n)\) time.

**Six-coloring.** Greedy coloring along the minimum-degree order uses at most 6 colors. A more careful argument gives a linear-time 5-coloring algorithm.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Four Color Theorem — Appel & Haken 1976). Every planar graph is 4-colorable.</div>

The Four Color Theorem required a computer-assisted proof and remains one of the most computationally demanding results in mathematics. In contrast, the 5-colorability of planar graphs admits a clean inductive proof using the minimum-degree order — an instructive instance of how structural bounds yield algorithmic dividends.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 2.4.6</strong>. Any planar graph can be stored using \(O(n)\) space so that adjacency testing is \(O(1)\).

</div>

**Adjacency testing.** Storing predecessors in the minimum-degree order allows \(O(1)\) adjacency testing using \(O(n)\) space (since each vertex has \(\leq 5\) predecessors).

**Maximum clique.** Using \(O(1)\) adjacency testing, we can find maximum cliques in planar graphs by checking each vertex's constant-size predecessor set.

**Dijkstra's algorithm.** Since \(m \in O(n)\) for planar graphs, binary heaps suffice and Fibonacci heaps are unnecessary: shortest paths run in \(O(n \log n)\).

### 1.4 Dual Graphs

One of the most powerful ideas in planar graph theory is the construction of the *dual graph*, which interchanges the roles of vertices and faces. Historically this duality underpins Kirchhoff's laws for electrical networks and, in modern form, gives the clean connection between minimum cuts and shortest paths in planar graphs.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Dual Graph). For a connected plane graph \(G\) with a fixed embedding, the <em>dual graph</em> \(G^<em>\) has one vertex per face of \(G\), and an edge \(f^</em>g^*\) for every edge of \(G\) shared by faces \(f\) and \(g\).</div>

The dual is itself a plane graph (embed each dual vertex inside its corresponding face, and route dual edges across primal edges). Crucially, taking the dual twice recovers the original graph.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 2.3.1</strong>. Let \(G\) be a connected plane graph. Then \((G^<em>)^</em> \cong G\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 2.3.2</strong>. The dual graph of any connected plane graph \(G\) can be computed in \(O(m + n)\) time.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 2.3.2</strong> (Polygon Mesh). A way to approximate surfaces by small polygons. For genus-0 surfaces the polygon mesh is a planar graph. A triangular mesh is one where every face is a triangle; converting it to a quadrangular mesh is equivalent to finding a perfect matching in the dual.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> \((G^<em>)^</em> \cong G\) for any connected plane graph \(G\). The dual can be computed and stored in \(O(m + n)\) time using cross-linked incidence lists.</div>

This involution — dualizing twice yields the original — is what makes planar duality such a clean and useful tool. It converts cut problems in the primal to cycle problems in the dual, and vice versa.

<strong>Application — polygon mesh conversion.</strong> Given a triangular mesh (a triangulated planar graph), converting it to a quadrangular mesh means finding a perfect matching in the dual graph — a classical task solvable in linear time.

<strong>Application — maximum flow via shortest paths.</strong> For an undirected flow network that is <strong>\(st\)-planar</strong> (both \(s\) and \(t\) lie on the outer face), the minimum \(st\)-cut corresponds to a shortest path in the dual. We first set up the formal definitions of flow networks.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 3.2.1</strong> (Flow Network). A simple graph \(G\) with two distinct vertices \(s, t\) and a capacity function \(c : E \to \mathbb{R}^+\). The network is undirected if \(c(i,j) = c(j,i)\) for all \(ij \in E\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 3.2.2</strong> (Network Flow). A function \(x : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}^+\) such that: (i) \(x(ij) > 0 \Rightarrow ij \in E\); (ii) \(x(ij) - x(ji) \leq c(ij)\) (capacity constraint); (iii) \(x(\delta(v)) = x(\delta(\bar v))\) for all \(v \neq s, t\) (balance constraint).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 3.2.3</strong> (Flow Value). The value of a flow \(x\) is \(x(\delta(s)) - x(\delta(\bar s))\), the net flow out of \(s\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 3.2.4</strong> (st-Cut). A partition \((S, \bar S)\) of the vertices with \(s \in S\) and \(t \notin S\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 3.2.5</strong> (Cut Value). The value of a cut \((S, \bar S)\) is \(x(\delta(S))\), the total capacity of edges crossing from \(S\) to \(\bar S\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 3.2.1</strong>. If \(x\) is a flow and \((S, \bar S)\) is an \(st\)-cut, then \(x(\delta(s)) - x(\delta(\bar s)) \leq x(\delta(S))\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 3.2.2</strong> (Maximum-Flow Minimum-Cut). For any network, the value of the maximum \(st\)-flow equals the value of the minimum \(st\)-cut.

</div>

For planar graphs, a minimum cut corresponds to a cycle in the dual. More precisely:

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 3.2.3</strong>. A minimum cut in an undirected planar network \((G, s, t)\) corresponds to a shortest-weight cycle in the dual graph \(G^*\) that separates \(s\) and \(t\).

</div>

For st-planar graphs, this is made algorithmic via the dual network:

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 3.2.6</strong> (Dual Network for st-Planar Graph). Add an edge \(st\) in the primal to obtain \(G'\). Take the dual \((G')^<em>\) and delete the edge between the two faces \(s^</em>, t^<em>\) incident to \(st\). For any other dual edge \(e^</em>\), set \(w(e^*) = c(e)\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 2.2.2</strong> (st-Planar). A graph is st-planar if it has a planar embedding in which both \(s\) and \(t\) lie on the outer face.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 3.2.4</strong>. The length of the shortest \(s^<em>t^</em>\)-path in the dual network equals the value of maximum flow in the primal network.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 3.2.5</strong>. Maximum flow in an undirected st-planar graph can be computed in \(O(n)\) time.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Maximum flow in an undirected \(st\)-planar graph can be computed in \(O(n)\) time by running Dijkstra's on the dual network.</div>

The key construction: add a temporary edge \(st\) on the outer face, form \(G'\), take its dual \((G')^<em>\), remove the dual edge between the two faces incident to \(st\), and set dual edge weights equal to primal capacities. A shortest \(s^</em>t^*\)-path in this network yields both the maximum flow value and a minimum cut. This result is a striking demonstration of planar duality at work: a flow problem in the primal becomes a shortest-path problem in the dual, and linear-time algorithms that would be impossible in general graphs become accessible.

### 1.5 NP-Hard Problems in Planar Graphs

Not everything becomes easy on planar graphs. Several fundamental problems remain NP-hard even when the input is planar, and understanding <em>why</em> — through carefully constructed reductions — reveals the limits of structural restrictions. The two main proof techniques exploit either the ability to simulate crossings with planar gadgets, or the fact that a simpler NP-hard problem (Planar 3-SAT) is already stated on a planar object.

Several problems remain NP-hard even restricted to planar graphs. The proof techniques are:
1. <strong>Crossing gadget</strong>: reduce from the general (non-planar) version using a gadget that simulates a crossing while preserving planarity.
2. <strong>Planarity-preserving reduction</strong>: start from an NP-hard problem already stated on planar graphs (e.g., Planar 3-SAT) and show the reduction preserves planarity.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (NP-hardness in planar graphs). 3-Coloring, Independent Set, Vertex Cover, Dominating Set, and Hamiltonian Cycle are all NP-hard in planar graphs. Independent Set is NP-hard even in planar graphs of maximum degree 3.</div>

These hardness results are important calibration points: they tell us that planarity alone is not a sufficient structural restriction for tractability. To get polynomial-time algorithms for hard problems, we need additional structural parameters — which is precisely the motivation for treewidth and the separator hierarchy developed in Chapters 3 and 4.

The crossing gadget argument works as follows. For 3-coloring, a crossing gadget is a planar subgraph on four boundary vertices \(a, a', b, b'\) such that any 3-coloring forces \(c(a) = c(a')\) and \(c(b) = c(b')\), while both same-color and different-color assignments are achievable.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 3.1.1</strong>. In any 3-coloring of the crossing gadget, \(c(a) = c(a')\) and \(c(b) = c(b')\). Moreover, there is a 3-coloring where \(c(a) = c(b)\) and one where \(c(a) \neq c(b)\).

</div>

Using this gadget, any graph \(G\) can be made planar while preserving 3-colorability.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 3.1.2</strong>. \(G\) is 3-colorable if and only if the planarized graph \(G_1\) (with crossings replaced by gadgets) is 3-colorable.

</div>

**Planar 3-SAT** is central: given a 3-SAT formula, build a graph with one vertex per variable, one per clause, edges for (literal, clause) containment, and a cycle over variables. If this SAT graph is planar, the instance is Planar 3-SAT.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 3.1.1</strong> (3-SAT Graph). Given an instance of 3-SAT, the corresponding SAT-graph has: one vertex for every variable \(x_i\); one vertex for every clause \(c_j\); an edge \(x_i c_j\) iff \(c_j\) contains \(x_i\) or \(\bar x_i\); and a variable cycle \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n, x_1\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 3.1.3</strong>. 3-SAT reduces to Planar 3-SAT, so Planar 3-SAT is NP-hard.

</div>

The reduction makes the SAT graph planar by rerouting the variable cycle and using crossing gadgets. The key reductions for other problems follow:

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 3.1.4</strong>. 3-SAT reduces polynomially to Independent Set: given a 3-SAT instance with \(N\) variables and \(M\) clauses, construct a graph \(G'\) with a clause-triangle per clause and a variable-cycle per variable. Then the 3-SAT instance is satisfiable iff \(G'\) has an independent set of size \(M + \sum_i \deg(x_i)\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 3.1.5</strong>. Applying the same reduction to Planar 3-SAT yields an instance of Independent Set whose graph is planar. Thus Independent Set is NP-hard in planar graphs.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 3.1.5.1</strong>. Independent Set is NP-hard even in planar graphs with maximum degree 3.

</div>

## Chapter 2: Graph Drawing and Embeddings

Chapter 1 established the basic theory of planarity. This chapter turns to finer questions about *how* graphs are embedded: how to search a planar graph in a way that respects the embedding, how to test planarity algorithmically, and how to produce aesthetically and mathematically canonical drawings. These questions have both theoretical and practical significance — canonical orderings and straight-line drawings underlie modern graph visualization tools.

### 2.1 Right-First Search

Ordinary DFS ignores the geometry of a planar embedding and treats all outgoing edges symmetrically. In a plane graph, there is a canonical way to break ties: use the cyclic edge order at each vertex. **Right-First Search** is a DFS that breaks ties using the embedding: at each vertex, edges are explored in counter-clockwise order with the incoming edge last.

<div class="key-box">

```
RightFirstSearch(G, v₀, e₀):
    discovered[v] = None for all v
    def recurse(v, e):
        discovered[v] = e  # arrived via e
        for eᵢ in edges at v in CCW order, with e last:
            v, w = eᵢ
            if discovered[w] is None:
                recurse(w, eᵢ)
    recurse(v₀, e₀)
```

</div>

The key insight is that always taking the "rightmost" option at each step produces paths with a global optimality property. To make this precise, we need to define what "rightmost" means globally.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Right-most path). Let \(\pi\) be a path from \(s\) to \(x\), both on the outer face. The <em>right region</em> of \(\pi\) is bounded by \(\pi\) and the counter-clockwise outer face path from \(s\) to \(x\). We call \(\pi\) <em>right-most</em> if every other \(sx\)-path lies in the left region.</div>

The right-most path is the one that "hugs" the rightmost part of the plane as tightly as possible. The following lemma says that greedy right-first choices globally produce this extremal path.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> Running right-first search at \(s\) (starting with the clockwise outer face edge of \(s\)) produces a right-most path to any \(x\) reached.</div>

This lemma is the engine behind the Menger algorithm for st-planar graphs: right-most paths in an st-planar graph are "as far apart as possible," so finding them greedily and removing used vertices produces a maximum collection of vertex-disjoint paths.

<strong>Menger's problem in \(st\)-planar graphs.</strong> Given an \(st\)-planar directed graph, find the maximum number of vertex-disjoint \(st\)-paths. Algorithm:
1. Temporarily insert edge \(ts\) on the outer face.
2. Run right-first search from \(s\) relative to \(ts\).
3. If \(t\) is reached, output the path, remove used vertices (excluding \(s, t\)), repeat.

Since each call only visits previously unvisited edges, total time is \(O(m + n)\). The right-most lemma guarantees optimality.

### 2.2 Planarity Testing

A graph is planar if and only if a planar embedding exists. Recognizing planarity and constructing the embedding can both be done in linear time. This is a remarkable algorithmic achievement: planarity is a global topological property, yet it can be certified by a local, incremental DFS procedure.

The key idea is to build the embedding one DFS subtree at a time. At each step, we maintain the invariant that the partial embedding is a valid "bush form."

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Bush Form). The <em>bush form</em> \(B(V')\) of a subset \(V' \subseteq V\) consists of \(G[V']\) together with one leaf for every cut edge \((v, w)\) with \(v \in V', w \notin V'\). A bush form is <em>valid</em> if all leaves lie on the outer face.</div>

The validity condition ensures that the "pending" edges (those connecting the current partial subgraph to the rest of the graph) can all be routed out through a single face, leaving room for the rest of the embedding to be appended.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 4.1.1</strong>. Let \(G\) be planar and \((V', \bar{V}')\) be a cut for which \(G[V \setminus V']\) is connected. Then \(G[V']\) has a planar embedding where all endpoints of cut edges lie on the outer face.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Lemma.</strong> If \(G\) is planar and \(G[V \setminus V']\) is connected, then \(B(V')\) has a valid embedding.</div>

The <strong>Haeupler–Tarjan algorithm</strong> runs DFS and, at each retreat from a vertex \(w\) to parent \(v\), checks whether the bush form of \(D(w)\) (descendants of \(w\)) can accommodate the edges from \(v\) into \(D(w)\) consecutively on the outer face. Two auxiliary lemmas drive the correctness argument.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 4.2.1</strong>. If \(G\) is planar, then for any tree edge \(vw\) with \(v\) the parent, the bush form \(B(D(w))\) has a valid embedding where the leaves of cut-edges are consecutive on the outer face.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 4.2.2</strong>. PQ-trees can be implemented such that a reduction for constraint \(I\) takes \(O(|I|)\) amortized time.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 4.2.3</strong>. Let \(X\) be a finite set and \(\mathcal{I}\) a finite set of consecutive constraints. We can test whether there is a permutation of \(X\) such that for every \(I \in \mathcal{I}\) the elements are consecutive in \(O(\sum_{I \in \mathcal{I}} |I|)\) time.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 4.2.4</strong>. At the time we retreat from \(w\) to its parent \(v\), if \(G\) is planar then a reduction of the PQ-tree \(PQ(w,t)\) with respect to constraint \(I := S(v)\) is successful.

</div>

This is tracked using **PQ-trees** — a data structure specifically designed for the problem of maintaining consecutive orderings under constraints.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (PQ-Tree). A rooted tree with elements of \(X\) at the leaves, encoding permissible permutations of \(X\). A <strong>P-node</strong> allows its children in any order; a <strong>Q-node</strong> allows its subtree to be reversed. Given a constraint \(I \subseteq X\), a <em>reduction</em> restricts the tree to permutations where \(I\) is consecutive. A reduction for constraint \(I\) costs \(O(|I|)\) amortized.</div>

PQ-trees encode a potentially exponential family of permutations in a polynomial-sized structure, and reductions are cheap precisely because the planarity constraints imposed at each DFS step are local. This amortized efficiency is what enables the linear overall runtime.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Haeupler–Tarjan). Planarity testing and embedding construction can be done in \(O(m)\) time.</div>

This is optimal: any algorithm must at least read the entire input graph, which takes \(\Omega(m)\) time.

![SPQR tree node types](/pics/cs762/spqr-tree-node.png)
<em>The four SPQR-tree node types encode biconnected components: S (series), P (parallel), Q (single edge), R (rigid/triconnected). The SPQR tree gives a compact representation of all planar embeddings of a biconnected graph.</em>

### 2.3 Triangulated Graphs and Canonical Orderings

To produce straight-line and grid drawings of planar graphs, we need a special vertex ordering that allows an inductive construction of the drawing. Triangulated graphs — those where every face is a triangle — are the natural starting point, since every planar graph can be triangulated by adding edges. The canonical ordering is the key algorithmic tool.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Triangulated Graph). A simple planar graph with \(n \geq 3\) vertices and a planar embedding in which every face is a triangle. Equivalently: a maximal planar graph, or a planar graph with exactly \(3n - 6\) edges.</div>

Triangulated planar graphs are the "densest" planar graphs possible — adding any edge would create a crossing. Three useful characterizations hold simultaneously:

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 5.1.1</strong>. The following are equivalent for a simple planar graph with \(n \geq 3\): (1) \(G\) is triangulated; (2) \(G\) is maximal planar; (3) \(G\) has exactly \(3n - 6\) edges.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 5.1.1</strong> (Maximal Planar Graph). A simple planar graph to which no edge can be added while preserving planarity.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 5.1.2</strong>. Any triangulated graph with \(n \geq 3\) has exactly \(2n - 4\) faces.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 5.1.3</strong>. Any simple triangulated graph is 3-connected.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 5.1.4</strong>. Any simple planar graph can be made triangulated by adding edges.

</div>

The triangulated disk is the building block for inductive constructions:

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 5.2.1</strong> (Inner Triangulated Graph). A set of points and straight-line edges such that all interior faces are triangles.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 5.2.2</strong> (Triangulated Disk). A planar graph with a planar drawing that is inner triangulated and whose outer face is a simple cycle.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 5.2.1</strong>. Any triangulated disk \(G\) is 2-connected. Furthermore, any separating pair \(\{v, w\}\) has both \(v, w\) on the outer face connected by a chord.

</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Canonical Ordering). A vertex order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) of a triangulated planar graph where \(v_1, v_2, v_n\) are the outer face and for each \(3 \leq k \leq n-1\), vertex \(v_k\) has at least two predecessors on the outer face of \(G[v_1, \ldots, v_k]\) and at least one successor.</div>

The canonical ordering is designed so that each new vertex \(v_k\) "sits on top of" a consecutive arc of the current outer face, making an inductive placement of vertices geometrically consistent.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 5.3.1</strong>. Let \(G\) be a triangulated graph with canonical order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\). For each \(3 \leq k \leq n-1\), the predecessors of \(v_k\) on the outer face of \(G_{k-1}\) form a consecutive path.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 5.3.2</strong>. Let \(G\) be a triangulated disk with outer-face edge \(ab\). Then \(G\) has a vertex order \(v_1 = a, v_2 = b, v_3, \ldots, v_n\) such that for \(i \geq 3\), vertex \(v_i\) has at least two predecessors on the outer face of \(G[v_1, \ldots, v_i]\) and at least one successor.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 5.3.3</strong>. Let \(G\) be a triangulated planar graph with outer face \(\{a, b, t\}\). There is a canonical order of \(G\) starting \(v_1 = a\), \(v_2 = b\), \(v_n = t\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 5.3.4</strong>. A canonical order of a triangulated planar graph can be found in linear time.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Every triangulated planar graph has a canonical ordering, computable in \(O(n)\) time.</div>

The existence of a canonical ordering is not obvious: it requires showing that there always exists a vertex whose removal leaves the graph in the right inductive state. The proof uses the structure of 2-connected triangulated disks.

<strong>Key property (maintained inductively):</strong> \(G_k = G[v_1, \ldots, v_k]\) is a <em>triangulated disk</em> — 2-connected, all inner faces are triangles, the outer face is a simple cycle. The predecessors of \(v_{k+1}\) form a consecutive interval on the outer face of \(G_k\).

<strong>Schnyder Wood.</strong> The canonical ordering induces a partition of the edges of a triangulated graph into 3 spanning forests (actually spanning trees, after accounting for vertex indices).

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 5.3.5</strong>. The edge set of any triangulated simple graph can be partitioned into 3 edge-disjoint trees.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 5.3.2</strong> (Schnyder Wood). The three trees obtained from a canonical order as above.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 5.3.3</strong> (Arboricity). The minimum number of forests needed to cover all edges of a graph \(G\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 5.3.5.1</strong>. Every planar graph has arboricity at most 3.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary.</strong> Every planar graph has arboricity at most 3 — its edges can be covered by 3 forests.</div>

The arboricity bound is sharp: the icosahedron has arboricity exactly 3. It also implies that any planar graph can be 3-colored as a "forest" rather than a graph — a useful structural decomposition.

<strong>Straight-line embedding (Tutte / Canonical Order).</strong> The canonical ordering constructively proves that planar graphs do not need curved edges in their drawings — every planar graph has a drawing where all edges are straight line segments.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Fáry / Wagner). Every simple planar graph has a straight-line (no-curve) planar drawing.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Place \(v_k\) with an \(x\)-coordinate strictly between those of its leftmost and rightmost predecessors on the outer face of \(G_{k-1}\). The \(y\)-coordinate is chosen to avoid collinearity with predecessor pairs. Inductively, the outer face vertices maintain strictly increasing \(x\)-coordinates, guaranteeing all edges are straight-line non-crossing. \(\square\)</div>

The proof strategy — place each new vertex in the interior of the triangle formed by its outer-face neighbors — is an inductive geometric argument enabled entirely by the canonical ordering.

<strong>Visibility representation.</strong> A <em>bar visibility representation</em> maps each vertex to a horizontal segment and each edge to a vertical segment connecting its endpoints. The canonical ordering implies every triangulated graph has a strong visibility representation, with coordinates in \([1, 3n-6] \times [1, n]\).

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 5.3.4</strong> (Bar Visibility Representation). Each vertex is a horizontal line segment. Each edge is a vertical line segment connecting the bars of its endpoints and intersecting no other bars.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 5.3.5</strong> (Strong Visibility Representation). A bar visibility representation where edges between two vertices exist if and only if there is some vertical line intersecting both horizontal vertex segments and nothing else.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 5.3.6</strong> (Weak Visibility Representation). A bar visibility representation where the strong model rule does not necessarily apply.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 5.3.6</strong>. Every triangulated graph has a strong visibility representation.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 5.3.6.1</strong>. Every planar graph has a weak visibility representation. (Proof: triangulate and apply the theorem.)

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 5.3.6.2</strong>. Any planar graph has a weak visibility representation for which all endpoints are grid points in a grid of size \(O(n) \times O(n)\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 5.3.7</strong>. Every planar graph is the minor of a \(k \times k\)-grid for \(k \leq 3n\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 5.3.8</strong>. Every simple planar graph has a straight-line embedding.

</div>

![Planar drawing algorithm stages](/pics/cs762/planar-drawing-algorithm.png)

## Chapter 2.5: Graph Classes Beyond Planar

Planar graphs are the starting point of structural graph theory, but they are far from the end of the story. Once we have a clean characterization of one class — planar graphs — the natural question is: what lies on either side? Are there useful sub-classes of planar graphs, and are there larger classes that capture some but not all of the planarity structure?

This chapter charts a hierarchy of graph classes organized by how "planar-like" they are. On the more restrictive side, we have *outerplanar graphs* (all vertices on the outer face), *series-parallel graphs* (built by series and parallel combinations), and *Apollonian networks* (built by iterating triangle subdivision). Each restriction imposes stronger structural constraints and enables more efficient algorithms. On the less restrictive side, we have *near-planar graphs* — those with bounded crossing number, bounded skew number, or bounded local crossing number — and *3D graphs*, which can always be drawn in three dimensions without any crossings.

The deeper purpose of this survey is to calibrate our intuition for treewidth. Outerplanar graphs have treewidth at most 2; series-parallel graphs are precisely the partial 2-trees; Apollonian networks are 3-trees. Each sub-class of planar graphs sits at a specific treewidth level in the hierarchy we will make precise in Chapter 4. Understanding this hierarchy — and which problems are tractable at each level — is the conceptual scaffold for the entire second half of the course.

### 2.5.1 Graphs in 3D

Surprisingly, the restriction to the plane is what causes crossing problems. In three dimensions, there is so much "room" that any graph whatsoever can be drawn without crossings using only straight edges. The proof uses the *moment curve* — a one-dimensional algebraic variety in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with the property that no four of its points are coplanar.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 6.1.1</strong>. Any graph can be drawn straight-line without crossings in 3D.

</div>

The proof places vertices on the moment curve \(\{(x, x^2, x^3) : x \in \mathbb{R}^+\}\). Any potential crossing would require 4 coplanar points on the moment curve, but Vandermonde determinants show no 3 distinct points on the moment curve are collinear, so no 4 are coplanar.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.1.1</strong> (Knot-Less Graph). A graph that can be drawn in 3D such that any cycle is homeomorphic to the unit circle.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.1.2</strong> (Link-Less Graph). A graph that can be drawn in 3D such that no two disjoint cycles form curves that are linked.

</div>

### 2.5.2 Near-Planar Graphs

If we must work in the plane but cannot avoid all crossings, the natural question is how few crossings are unavoidable. The *crossing number* of a graph is the minimum over all drawings. Allowing even one crossing dramatically expands the class — graphs of crossing number 1 include many non-planar graphs — but crossing number remains NP-hard to compute. Weaker variants (bounded skew number, bounded local crossings) give tractable recognition problems and often preserve enough planar structure for algorithmic purposes.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.1.3</strong> (Bounded Crossing Number). A graph \(G\) has crossing number \(k\) if there is a drawing of \(G\) in the plane with exactly \(k\) crossings.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.1.4</strong> (Bounded Skew Number). A graph has skew number \(k\) if there exists a drawing of \(G\) in the plane with at most \(k\) edges removed to make it planar, and \(k\) is minimal.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.1.5</strong> (\(k\)-Planar Graphs). Graphs that can be drawn such that any edge crosses at most \(k\) other edges.

</div>

### 2.5.3 Outer-Planar Graphs

Outer-planar graphs are the simplest proper sub-class of planar graphs. The requirement that every vertex lies on the outer face is a strong restriction: it means the graph is "one-dimensional" in a sense — you can always see all vertices from the outside. This makes many hard problems tractable. For instance, any maximal outer-planar graph is a triangulated disk whose dual is a tree, so problems that require global reasoning on a general planar graph reduce to tree problems on outer-planar graphs.

The key algorithmic leverage is that outer-planar graphs have treewidth at most 2: their structure is captured by a tree of triangles (the interior dual), and the DP from Chapter 4 runs in \(O(n)\). This connection — outerplanar equals partial 2-tree — will be made precise in Section 4.4.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.2.1</strong> (Outer-Planar). A graph with a planar embedding such that all vertices lie on the outer face.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 6.2.1</strong>. A graph \(G\) is outer-planar if and only if adding a universal vertex \(u\) (adjacent to all others) yields a planar graph.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 6.2.1.1</strong>. Outer-planarity testing can be done in \(O(n)\) time.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 6.2.2</strong>. Any maximal outer-planar graph \(G\) with \(n \geq 3\) vertices satisfies: (1) every interior face is a triangle; (2) the outer face is a simple cycle (Hamiltonian); (3) \(G\) is a triangulated disk; (4) \(G\) has exactly \(n - 2\) interior faces; (5) \(G\) has exactly \(2n - 3\) edges.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 6.2.3</strong>. Let \(G\) be a maximal outer-planar graph. Then \(G^<em> = \{v^</em>\} \cup T\) where \(v^*\) is the outer-face vertex and \(T\) is a tree with maximum degree 3.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 6.2.4</strong>. Every maximal outer-planar graph has a vertex ordering \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) such that \(v_1 v_2\) is an outer-face edge and each \(v_i\) (\(i \geq 3\)) has exactly two predecessors that are adjacent.

</div>

The vertex order from Lemma 6.2.4 admits an easy linear-time 3-coloring by greedy on this order.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.2.2</strong> (\(k\)-Outer-Planar Graphs). Graphs that can be embedded in the plane such that all vertices are removed after removing the outer face \(k\) times (onion peeling).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 6.2.5</strong>. Let \(G\) be a planar graph with a planar grid-drawing in a \(w \times h\) grid. Then the outer-planarity of \(G\) is at most \(\min\!\left(\lceil w/2 \rceil, \lceil h/2 \rceil\right)\).

</div>

### 2.5.4 Series-Parallel Graphs

Series-parallel graphs generalize the circuits of electrical engineering. Any resistor network built from series and parallel combinations — no Wheatstone bridge, no \(Y\)-\(\Delta\) configuration — is a series-parallel graph. The recursive construction makes many optimization problems (shortest paths with constraints, maximum flow, network reliability) solvable in linear time by a simple bottom-up computation on the SP-tree. Series-parallel graphs sit at exactly treewidth 2 in the hierarchy: they are the partial 2-trees, strictly more general than outerplanar graphs but still far more tractable than general planar graphs.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.2.3</strong> (2-Terminal SP-Graph). A graph with two terminals \(s, t\) built inductively: base case is a single edge \(st\); series combination identifies \(t_1\) with \(s_2\); parallel combination identifies both \(s_1 = s_2\) and \(t_1 = t_2\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 6.2.6</strong>. Let \(G\) be a 2-terminal SP-graph with edge \(vw\). Both (1) duplicating \(vw\) and (2) subdividing \(vw\) with a new vertex yield 2-terminal SP-graphs.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 6.2.7</strong>. Let \(G\) be a 2-terminal SP-graph with \(n \geq 3\) and terminals \(s, t\). Then: (1) \(G\) has at most \(2n - 3\) edges; (2) \(G\) is either a single edge or contains a vertex of degree 2; (3) \(G\) is planar with terminals on the outer face; (4) \(G\) has a 3-coloring where the terminals receive different colors; (5) \(G\) has an acyclic edge orientation with \(s\) the only source and \(t\) the only target.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.2.4</strong> (Series-Parallel Graph). A graph that is a spanning subgraph of some 2-terminal series-parallel graph.

</div>

### 2.5.5 Apollonian Networks

Apollonian networks sit at treewidth 3 in the hierarchy. They arise naturally in geometry — the Apollonian gasket of classical circle-packing is their continuous analog — and they serve as the canonical example of 3-trees. Every Apollonian network is triangulated, planar, and has a clean recursive structure: at each step, a new vertex is inserted into a triangular face and connected to all three triangle vertices. This inductive definition gives a perfect elimination order almost by construction.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 6.2.5</strong> (Apollonian Network). A triangulated plane graph obtained as follows: base case is a triangle; inductive step inserts a new vertex inside a triangular inner face and connects it to all three triangle vertices.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 6.2.8</strong>. Any Apollonian network on \(n \geq 4\) vertices has a vertex order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) where \(v_1, v_2, v_3\) form a triangle and for \(i \geq 4\), vertex \(v_i\) has exactly three predecessors that form a triangle. Hence every Apollonian network is a 3-tree.

</div>

## Chapter 3: Separators and Decompositions

The previous chapters developed the rich structure of planar graphs and their embeddings. We now turn to one of the most powerful consequences of planarity for algorithm design: the existence of small *separators*. A separator is a small set of vertices whose removal splits the graph into balanced pieces. Small separators enable divide-and-conquer algorithms with sub-quadratic running times for problems that are otherwise expensive, and they lead directly to the concept of treewidth explored in Chapter 4.

### 3.1 Separators

The central question is: how cheaply can we split a planar graph into balanced pieces? "Cheaply" means using few vertices as the separator. If a separator has \(s\) vertices, then to solve a problem by divide-and-conquer we pay at most \(O(s \cdot T(n/2) + s \cdot C)\) for the boundary work, where \(C\) is the per-separator-vertex cost. For the recursion to beat \(O(n^2)\), we need \(s = o(n)\). The Lipton–Tarjan theorem gives \(s = O(\sqrt{n})\), which is enough to beat quadratic time for a wide range of problems.

To measure "balance," we allow vertices to have weights and ask that no piece of the divided graph is too heavy. This generality is important for the recursive application of the separator theorem.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (\(\alpha\)-Separator). For \(\alpha \in (0,1)\), an <em>\(\alpha\)-separator</em> is a set \(S \subseteq V\) such that every connected component (\emph{flap}) of \(G - S\) has total vertex weight at most \(\alpha \cdot w(V)\).</div>

A \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator is the strongest case: every piece has at most half the total weight. Separators of size \(O(\sqrt{n})\) in planar graphs — the Lipton–Tarjan theorem — are what make the divide-and-conquer algorithms of Section 3.2 possible.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 15.1.2</strong> (Uniform Weights). The weight function \(w(v) = 1\) for all \(v \in V\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 15.2.1</strong>. The \(a \times b\)-grid has a \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator of size \(\min(a, b)\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 15.2.2</strong>. The \(k \times k\)-grid does NOT have a \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator of size \(\frac{1}{2}k\), even for uniform weights.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 15.2.3</strong>. The 3-tree obtained from \(K_4\) by inserting 3 weight-1 vertices into each face does not have an \(\alpha\)-separator of size 3 or less for \(\alpha < \frac{3}{4}\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 15.3.1</strong>. In any tree, the vertex returned by the maximum-weighted-child walk is a \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 15.3.2</strong>. Every tree with \(n\) nodes has \(\mathrm{pw}(T) \leq \log_2 n\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 15.4.1</strong>. Every partial \(k\)-tree \(G\) has a weighted \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator of size at most \(k + 1\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 15.4.2</strong>. The \(k \times k\)-grid has treewidth at least \(k/2\).

</div>

![Planar graph separator highlighted](/pics/cs762/planar-separator.png)
*The planar separator theorem guarantees a small vertex cut (red) dividing the graph into balanced parts. Here the Petersen graph is shown with a small separator highlighted.*

**Separators in trees.** Any tree has a \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator of size 1: pick the root \(r\), walk to the heaviest child repeatedly until no child's subtree exceeds \(\frac{1}{2} w(V)\). The current vertex is the separator.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Planar Separator — Lipton & Tarjan). Every planar graph \(G\) has a weighted \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator of size \(O(\sqrt{n})\), computable in \(O(n)\) time.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Choose \(k = \lfloor \sqrt{n}/3 \rfloor\). Remove \(S_1\), every \((k+1)\)-st onion peel (\(\leq n/(k+1)\) vertices), to make the remainder \(k\)-outer-planar. This remainder has treewidth \(\leq 3k\), hence admits a \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator \(S_2\) of size \(\leq 3k+1\). Then \(S = S_1 \cup S_2\) has size \(\leq \frac{n}{k+1} + 3k + 1 \in O(\sqrt{n})\). \(\square\)</div>

The proof strategy is elegant: the onion peeling structure of planar graphs (Section 6.1) allows us to sparsify the graph to something of bounded treewidth, on which small separators are guaranteed. The optimal \(k = \Theta(\sqrt{n})\) balances the two separator contributions.

### 3.2 Divide-and-Conquer with Separators

The Planar Separator Theorem is not just a structural curiosity — it is a powerful algorithmic tool. For problems whose running time scales superlinearly with the number of vertices, a balanced separator lets us split the work, solve independently, and combine. The recursion tree has depth \(O(\log n)\) and at each level, the separator vertices require special attention.

Planar separators enable divide-and-conquer algorithms with sub-quadratic running times:

<strong>Shortest directed cycle.</strong> Use the \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separator recursively. At each level, run Dijkstra from each separator vertex. Recursion: \(T(n) = \sum T(n_i) + O(\sqrt{n} \cdot n \log n)\), yielding \(T(n) \in O(n^{3/2} \log n)\).

<strong>Global minimum cut.</strong> By duality, a minimum cut in an undirected planar graph corresponds to a shortest cycle in the dual. The same divide-and-conquer approach applies.

<strong>Maximum matching.</strong> Build the matching recursively in each flap, then augment along paths passing through each separator vertex. Total time: \(O(n^{3/2} \log n)\).

### 3.3 Separator Hierarchies and Pathwidth

We have used separators as a computational device. Now we show that the recursive separator structure carries deep information about the graph's <em>intrinsic</em> complexity: it gives an explicit upper bound on pathwidth, and through pathwidth, on treewidth. This is the bridge that takes us from the "divide-and-conquer" world of separator algorithms into the "bag decomposition" world of treewidth.

The recursive application of the separator theorem produces a hierarchical decomposition of the graph that is more than just a computational convenience — it encodes the graph's structure in a form that directly gives bounds on pathwidth and treewidth. This connection is the bridge between the separator world and the decomposition world of Chapter 4.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (\(\alpha\)-Hierarchy). An \(\alpha\)-separator hierarchy of \(G\) is a rooted tree where each node \(i\) stores a separator \(S(i)\) of the subgraph \(G(i)\) induced by descendants of \(i\), and the children of \(i\) correspond to flaps of \(G(i) - S(i)\).</div>

The hierarchy records the full recursive structure: at each level, the separator vertices are the "interface" between the two halves. Collecting these interfaces along a root-to-leaf path gives a sequence of bags that forms a path decomposition.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 15.7.1</strong> (\(\alpha\)-Hierarchy). An \(\alpha\)-separator hierarchy of a weighted graph \(G\) is a rooted tree where each node \(i\) stores a separator \(S(i) \neq \emptyset\) such that: (i) each \(v \in V\) is stored at exactly one node; (ii) for every internal node \(i\), \(S(i)\) is an \(\alpha\)-separator of \(G(i)\) (the graph at \(i\) and descendants); (iii) children of \(i\) correspond to flaps of \(G(i) - S(i)\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 15.7.1</strong>. In an \(\alpha\)-hierarchy with uniform weights, any node \(i\) at depth \(d\) has \(n(i) \leq n \alpha^d\) and the hierarchy height is at most \(\log_{1/\alpha} n\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 15.7.2</strong>. The path decomposition obtained from a separator hierarchy (by letting each leaf's bag be the union of all ancestor separators, arranged left-to-right) is a valid path decomposition of \(G\).

</div>

From a hierarchy, one extracts a path decomposition by letting each leaf's bag be the union of all ancestor separators. This yields:

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 15.7.3</strong>. Any induced-closed graph class \(\mathcal{G}\) with \(\alpha\)-separators of size \(\sigma(n)\) has pathwidth at most \(\sigma(n) \cdot \log_{1/\alpha} n\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 15.7.3.1</strong>. Every partial \(k\)-tree has pathwidth at most \((k+1) \log n\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 15.7.4</strong>. If an induced-closed graph class \(\mathcal{G}\) has \(\alpha\)-separators of size \(O(\sqrt{n})\), then all graphs in \(\mathcal{G}\) have pathwidth \(O(\sqrt{n})\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 15.7.4.1</strong>. Any planar graph has \(\mathrm{tw}(G) \leq \mathrm{pw}(G) \in O(\sqrt{n})\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 15.8.1</strong> (Generalized Separator). If an induced-closed graph class \(\mathcal{G}\) has \(\alpha\)-separators of size \(O(\sqrt{n})\), then for any \(\epsilon > 0\), it also has \(\epsilon\)-separators of size \(O(\sqrt{n/\epsilon})\).

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Any induced-closed graph class with \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separators of size \(\sigma(n)\) has pathwidth at most \(\sigma(n) \cdot \log_{1/\alpha} n\).</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Corollary.</strong> Planar graphs have pathwidth (and treewidth) \(O(\sqrt{n})\). This is tight: the \(k \times k\)-grid has \(k^2\) vertices and treewidth exactly \(k\).</div>

The tightness of the \(k \times k\)-grid example shows that the separator theorem is essentially sharp for planar graphs: neither the separator size nor the treewidth bound can be improved to \(o(\sqrt{n})\) in general. This motivates the quest for subclasses with smaller treewidth, addressed in the next chapter.

## Chapter 4: Tree Decompositions and Treewidth

This is the heart of the course. Tree decompositions generalize interval graph representations and allow dynamic programming to be lifted from trees to any graph with small "tree-like" structure. The concept of <em>treewidth</em> — the minimum width of any tree decomposition — emerges as the master parameter of the whole theory: small treewidth enables efficient algorithms for virtually all natural graph problems, while large treewidth (specifically, containing a large grid minor) signals computational hardness.

### 4.1 Interval Graphs and Chordal Graphs

Before defining tree decompositions in full generality, it is instructive to study two families where the decomposition structure is already visible: interval graphs and chordal graphs. These classes admit polynomial-time algorithms for problems that are NP-hard in general, and understanding <em>why</em> sets up the machinery for bounded-treewidth graphs.

Here is a useful mental model. Think of <strong>Gaussian elimination</strong> on a sparse symmetric matrix: when you eliminate a variable, you fill in edges between its remaining neighbors. If those neighbors already form a clique, no new edges are created — the matrix stays sparse. A graph where <em>every</em> vertex can be eliminated in this "fill-free" order is precisely a <strong>chordal graph</strong>, and the elimination order is its <em>perfect elimination order</em>. This connection is not merely analogical: Gaussian elimination on the adjacency matrix of a chordal graph runs without any fill-in, which is why chordal graphs are the key to efficient sparse linear solvers. The algorithm we study — Maximum Cardinality Search — is essentially the graph-theoretic version of choosing the best pivot at each elimination step.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.1.1</strong> (Intersection Graph of Intervals). Given a set of intervals, define a graph with one vertex \(v\) per interval \(I_v\) and an edge \(vw\) iff \(I_v \cap I_w \neq \emptyset\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.1.2</strong> (Interval Graph). \(G\) is an interval graph if it is the intersection graph of some set of intervals.

</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Interval Graph). A graph \(G\) is an <em>interval graph</em> if vertices correspond to intervals on the real line and edges correspond to intersecting intervals.</div>

Interval graphs model scheduling conflicts, molecular sequence overlaps, and a host of other practical problems where "overlap" is the fundamental relation. Their structure is essentially one-dimensional, which is why they are so tractable.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 7.1.1</strong>. A cycle of length \(k \geq 4\) cannot be an interval graph.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.1.3</strong> (Induced-Hereditary). A graph class closed under taking induced subgraphs. Interval graphs are induced-hereditary.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.1.4</strong> (Hereditary). A graph class closed under taking subgraphs. Interval graphs are NOT hereditary.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.2.1</strong> (Chordal Graph). A graph \(G\) is chordal if it does not contain an induced \(k\)-cycle for any \(k \geq 4\).

</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Chordal Graph). A graph \(G\) is <em>chordal</em> if every cycle of length \(\geq 4\) has a chord (an edge between non-adjacent cycle vertices).</div>

Chordality is the abstract algebraic generalization of interval graph structure: a graph is chordal when it has no "long" induced cycles, i.e., when every cycle can be "shortcut" by a chord.

Every interval graph is chordal. Every tree is chordal.

The key algorithmic handle on chordal graphs is the <strong>perfect elimination order</strong> (PEO): an ordering in which each vertex, together with its <em>earlier</em> neighbors, forms a clique. The PEO is the sequence in which vertices can be "safely eliminated" — removed from the graph without disturbing the clique structure of their neighborhood. Think of building up the graph in reverse: each new vertex \(v_i\) arrives and finds that all of its already-placed neighbors are mutually adjacent, forming a clique. This is exactly the condition that makes greedy coloring optimal and that allows the tree decomposition to be read off from the order.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.3.1</strong> (Perfect Elimination Order). A vertex order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) such that \(\mathrm{pred}(v_i) = \{v_j : j < i,\, v_j v_i \in E\}\) forms a clique for all \(i\).

</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Perfect Elimination Order, PEO). A vertex order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) such that for each \(i\), the set \(\{v_j : j < i, v_j v_i \in E\}\) (the predecessors of \(v_i\)) forms a clique. Equivalently, \(v_i\) is simplicial in \(G[v_1, \ldots, v_i]\).</div>

The PEO is precisely the order in which vertices can be eliminated — removed without destroying the clique structure of their neighborhood. This turns out to characterize chordality exactly.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 7.3.1</strong>. If \(G\) has a perfect elimination order, then \(G\) is chordal. The converse also holds: \(G\) is chordal if and only if it has a perfect elimination order.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> \(G\) is chordal if and only if it has a perfect elimination order.</div>

This equivalence is the key to understanding chordal graphs algorithmically: we can recognize them, solve optimization problems on them, and build their tree decompositions, all via the PEO.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 8.1.1</strong> (Simplicial Vertex). A vertex \(v \in V(G)\) whose neighbours form a clique in \(G\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 8.1.1</strong>. A graph \(G\) has a p.e.o. if and only if every induced subgraph of \(G\) has a simplicial vertex.

</div>

**Maximum Cardinality Search (MCS).** Repeatedly pick the vertex with the most already-chosen neighbors (breaking ties arbitrarily). This runs in \(O(m + n)\) and produces a PEO of any chordal graph.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 8.1.2</strong>. Let \(C = c_0, \ldots, c_{k-1}\) be a cycle in a chordal graph. Then for any \(0 \leq i < k\), either \(c_{i-1}c_{i+1}\) is an edge, or \(c_i\) is incident to a chord of \(C\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 8.1.3</strong>. Assume \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) was the output of MCS. Let \(C\) be a component of \(G \setminus \{v_1, \ldots, v_i\}\) for some \(1 \leq i < n\). Then \(N_i(C)\) is a clique.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 8.1.4</strong>. Let \(G\) be a chordal graph. Then the order \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) returned by MCS is a perfect elimination order, regardless of tie-breaking.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 8.2.1</strong>. Chordal graphs can be recognized in linear time.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 8.3.1</strong>. Let \(G\) have a p.e.o. \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\). Then any clique \(C\) is a subset of \(\{v_i\} \cup \mathrm{pred}(v_i)\) for some \(i\). If \(C\) is a maximal clique, then \(C = \{v_i\} \cup \mathrm{pred}(v_i)\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 8.3.2</strong>. \(G\) is an interval graph if and only if its maximal cliques can be ordered consecutively: there exist \(C_1, \ldots, C_k\) such that if \(v \in C_i \cap C_j\) then \(v \in C_h\) for all \(i < h < j\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 8.3.3</strong>. Fix a p.e.o. and set \(C_i := \{v_i\} \cup \mathrm{pred}(v_i)\). Then \(C_i\) is not a maximal clique if and only if there exists \(j > i\) such that \(v_i\) is the last predecessor of \(v_j\) and \(\mathrm{indeg}(v_j) = \mathrm{indeg}(v_i) + 1\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 8.3.4</strong>. Testing whether a graph is an interval graph can be done in linear time.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Chordal graphs (and interval graphs) can be recognized in \(O(m + n)\) time. Interval graph recognition additionally requires checking that maximal cliques can be ordered consecutively (using PQ-trees).</div>

The PQ-tree reappears here: the consecutive ordering of cliques in an interval graph is precisely the kind of constraint that PQ-trees were designed to encode and check efficiently.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 7.4.1</strong>. The greedy algorithm on a perfect elimination order yields the optimal coloring: \(\chi(G) = \omega(G)\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 7.4.1.1</strong>. For a graph with p.e.o. \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\), \(\omega(G) = \chi(G) = \max_i\{\mathrm{indeg}(v_i) + 1\}\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.4.1</strong> (Clique Cover). A partition of \(V(G)\) such that each class forms a clique in \(G\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 7.4.2</strong>. The greedy algorithm on a p.e.o. \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) (processed in reverse) yields a maximum independent set.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 7.4.3</strong>. Dominating Set is NP-hard in chordal graphs.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.5.1</strong> (Intersection Graph). A graph \(G\) is an intersection graph if there is a universe \(X\) such that every vertex \(v\) corresponds to a set \(S_v \subseteq X\), and edge \(uv\) exists iff \(S_u \cap S_v \neq \emptyset\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 7.5.1</strong>. Every graph is an intersection graph. (Take \(X = E\) and \(S_v = \{e = vw : e \in E\}\).)

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.5.2</strong> (Induced-\(\mathcal{H}\)-Free Graph). Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be a collection of graphs. The class of induced-\(\mathcal{H}\)-free graphs consists of those whose induced subgraphs never belong to \(\mathcal{H}\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 7.5.3</strong> (Perfect Graph). A graph \(G\) is perfect if \(\omega(H) = \chi(H)\) for every induced subgraph \(H\) of \(G\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 7.5.2</strong> (Strong Perfect Graph Theorem). A graph is perfect if and only if it does not have an odd hole or an odd anti-hole as an induced subgraph.

</div>

**Polynomial algorithms in chordal graphs.** The PEO does for chordal graphs what the minimum-degree ordering did for planar graphs: it provides a vertex ordering that makes greedy algorithms optimal. Because each vertex's predecessor set is a clique, there is no "wasted" coloring and no "hidden" interference that greedy might miss.

Using a PEO:
- **Coloring:** Greedy on the PEO gives the optimal \(\chi(G) = \omega(G)\) coloring.
- **Maximum independent set:** Greedy in reverse PEO (the marked-set argument) gives an optimal solution.
- **Maximum clique:** \(\omega(G) = \max_i(\text{indeg}(v_i) + 1)\).

Note: **Dominating Set** is NP-hard in chordal graphs (reduce from Vertex Cover in general graphs by subdividing every edge and adding all clique edges between original vertices). This shows that even well-structured graph classes can retain NP-hard problems — treewidth, not chordality, is the right parameter for universal tractability.

### 4.2 Strong Tree Decompositions

The connection between interval graphs and path decompositions, and between chordal graphs and tree decompositions, can be made precise via strong decompositions.

Let us pause to consolidate what the perfect elimination order tells us. For a chordal graph, the set \(\{v_i\} \cup \text{pred}(v_i)\) — vertex \(v_i\) together with its earlier neighbors — is a clique, and these cliques can be organized into a tree structure where adjacent bags share a large overlap. This is the **strong tree decomposition**: the tree whose nodes are the maximal cliques and whose edges connect cliques that differ by exactly one vertex. The "strong" refers to the biconditional in condition (iii): every pair sharing a bag is adjacent, and conversely. This is only possible for chordal graphs. For general graphs, we must relax this to the weak tree decomposition — every edge is covered by a bag, but two vertices sharing a bag need not be adjacent. That relaxation is the key to making the theory work for arbitrary graphs.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 9.1.1</strong> (Strong Path Decomposition). A strong path decomposition \(\mathcal{P}\) of \(G\) consists of a path \(P\) with nodes \(I\) and an assignment \(X : I \to 2^V\) such that: (i) every vertex of \(G\) belongs to at least one bag; (ii) for every \(v\), the bags containing \(v\) are consecutive along \(P\); (iii) \(vw \in E\) if and only if some bag \(X_i\) contains both \(v\) and \(w\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 9.1.0.1</strong>. A graph \(G\) is an interval graph if and only if it has a strong path decomposition.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 9.2.2</strong> (Intersection Graph of Subtrees). Given a tree \(T\) and subtrees \(T_1, \ldots, T_n\), define a graph with vertices \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) and edge \(v_i v_j\) iff \(T_i \cap T_j \neq \emptyset\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 9.2.1</strong>. A graph is an intersection graph of subtrees of a tree if and only if it has a strong tree decomposition.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 9.2.2</strong>. If \(G\) has a strong tree decomposition, then \(G\) has a perfect elimination order. Furthermore, for each \(j\), there exists a bag containing \(\{v_j\} \cup \mathrm{pred}(v_j)\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 9.2.2.1</strong>. Let \(T\) be a strong tree decomposition of \(G\). Then for any clique \(C\), some bag of \(T\) contains all vertices of \(C\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 9.2.3</strong>. If \(G\) has a p.e.o. \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\), then it has a strong tree decomposition where every bag has the form \(\{v_i\} \cup \mathrm{pred}(v_i)\) for some \(i\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 9.2.4</strong>. A graph \(G\) is chordal if and only if it has a strong tree decomposition \(T\). Furthermore, \(\omega(G) \leq k + 1\) if and only if all bags of \(T\) have size at most \(k + 1\).

</div>

The perfect elimination order of a chordal graph encodes a tree-like structure: each vertex's predecessor set is a clique, and these cliques can be organized into a tree. This is the **strong tree decomposition** — a tree whose bags are the maximal cliques, with edges representing shared elements. It is the prototype for the more general tree decomposition.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 9.2.1</strong> (Strong Tree Decomposition). A strong tree decomposition \(T\) of \(G\) consists of a tree \(T\) with nodes \(I\) and bags \(X : I \to 2^V\) such that: (i) every vertex belongs to at least one bag; (ii) for every \(v\), the bags containing \(v\) are connected (connectivity condition); (iii) \(vw \in E\) if and only if some bag \(X_i\) contains both \(v\) and \(w\).

</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Strong Tree Decomposition). A <em>strong tree decomposition</em> of \(G\) is a tree \(T\) with node-bags \(X_i \subseteq V\) satisfying: (i) every vertex is in some bag; (ii) for every \(v\), the bags containing \(v\) are connected in \(T\) (connectivity condition); (iii) \(vw \in E\) <strong>if and only if</strong> some bag contains both \(v\) and \(w\).</div>

Condition (iii) is the "if and only if" form: every edge is represented in some bag, <em>and</em> every pair of vertices sharing a bag is adjacent. This biconditional makes the decomposition exact — the tree completely encodes the graph structure.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> A graph is chordal if and only if it has a strong tree decomposition. Moreover, \(\omega(G) \leq k+1\) iff all bags have size \(\leq k+1\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Given a PEO, build the tree by: for each \(v_i\), create bag \(X_i = \{v_i\} \cup \text{pred}(v_i)\), attach it to the bag \(X_h\) where \(v_h\) is the last predecessor of \(v_i\). Connectivity and coverage follow from the PEO structure. Conversely, a pre-order traversal of any strong tree decomposition yields a PEO. \(\square\)</div>

The proof is constructive in both directions: given a chordal graph we build its strong tree decomposition via the PEO, and given any strong tree decomposition we recover a PEO by traversal. The two structures are in perfect correspondence.

### 4.3 Tree Decomposition and Treewidth

The strong tree decomposition requires the "if and only if" edge condition — every pair sharing a bag must be adjacent. For general (non-chordal) graphs, this is impossible. The solution is to relax to the <em>weak</em> tree decomposition, which only requires that edges are covered by bags, not that all pairs sharing a bag are adjacent. This relaxation is what allows tree decompositions to capture graphs far beyond the chordal class.

Before the definition, let us build intuition for what a tree decomposition <em>is</em>. Picture the graph \(G\) spread out on a table. A tree decomposition finds a way to "cover" \(G\) with overlapping clusters — the <strong>bags</strong> — arranged in a tree. Each vertex of \(G\) appears in one or more bags, and the bags containing any fixed vertex form a connected subtree (the <em>connectivity condition</em>). Every edge of \(G\) is "witnessed" by at least one bag that contains both its endpoints.

The tree structure means that if you take any bag \(X_i\) and remove it, the host tree splits into subtrees, and the corresponding subgraphs of \(G\) on each side can only interact through \(X_i\) itself. This is the fundamental separation property: \(X_i\) is a separator between the two sides. The <strong>width</strong> of a decomposition is one less than the size of the largest bag; we subtract one so that trees themselves (which have bags of size 2, one vertex per edge) get treewidth 1. Treewidth 0 means the graph is a collection of isolated vertices; treewidth 1 means a forest; treewidth 2 means outerplanar or series-parallel; treewidth \(k\) means "as complex as a \(k\)-tree but no more."

The treewidth of a graph is the minimum width over all possible tree decompositions. Finding the optimal decomposition is NP-hard in general — but the parameter itself, once computed or approximated, unlocks efficient algorithms for an enormous range of problems.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Tree Decomposition). A <em>(weak) tree decomposition</em> of \(G\) is a tree \(T\) with bags \(X_i \subseteq V\) satisfying conditions (i), (ii), and a weakened (iii): \(vw \in E \Rightarrow\) some bag contains \(\{v, w\}\) (but not necessarily the converse).</div>

The weaker condition means that a tree decomposition is not unique — every graph admits many tree decompositions of varying widths. We seek the decomposition of minimum width.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 10.3.1</strong> (Tree Decomposition). A (weak) tree decomposition of \(G\) is a strong tree decomposition of some supergraph of \(G\). Equivalently, a tree \(T\) with bags \(X_i \subseteq V\) such that: (i) every vertex appears in at least one bag; (ii) for every \(v\), the bags containing \(v\) form a connected subtree; (iii) if \(vw \in E\) then some bag contains \(\{v, w\}\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 10.3.2</strong> (Width). The width of a tree decomposition is \(\max_i |X_i| - 1\). The treewidth \(\mathrm{tw}(G)\) is the minimum width over all tree decompositions of \(G\).

</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Treewidth). The <em>width</em> of a tree decomposition is \(\max_i |X_i| - 1\). The <em>treewidth</em> \(\text{tw}(G)\) is the minimum width over all tree decompositions of \(G\).</div>

The shift to \(|X_i| - 1\) (rather than \(|X_i|\)) is a normalization convention that makes trees have treewidth 1 (every bag has size 2 = 1 + 1) and cliques have treewidth \(n-1\). Treewidth measures "how far from a tree the graph is," with trees at one extreme and dense graphs at the other.

![Graph and its tree decomposition](/pics/cs762/tree-decomposition.png)
<em>A graph \(G\) and a tree decomposition of width 2. Each bag covers a clique in the supergraph.</em>

The following theorem collects the most important equivalent characterizations of bounded treewidth, connecting the decomposition definition to a concrete graph-theoretic construction.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Equivalent characterizations). For \(n \geq k+1\), the following are equivalent:
1. \(G\) is a partial \(k\)-tree (spanning subgraph of a \(k\)-tree).
2. \(G\) is a subgraph of a chordal graph \(H\) with \(\omega(H) = k+1\).
3. \(\text{tw}(G) \leq k\).
</div>

This equivalence is the foundation of efficient DP on bounded-treewidth graphs: characterization 1 gives a concrete inductive construction, characterization 2 connects to the PEO theory of Section 4.1, and characterization 3 is the algorithmic handle.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 10.3.1</strong>. The following are equivalent for a graph \(G\) with \(n \geq k + 1\): (1) \(G\) is a partial \(k\)-tree; (2) \(G\) is a subgraph of a chordal graph \(H\) with \(\omega(H) = k + 1\); (3) \(\mathrm{tw}(G) \leq k\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 10.3.2</strong>. For any tree decomposition \(T\) of \(G\), any clique \(C\) of \(G\) is contained in some bag of \(T\). In particular, \(\omega(G) \leq \mathrm{tw}(G) + 1\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 10.3.3</strong>. If \(\mathrm{tw}(G) \leq k\) then for any subgraph \(G'\) of \(G\), \(\mathrm{tw}(G') \leq k\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 10.3.4</strong>. If \(G\) has treewidth at most \(k\) and \(H\) is obtained by contracting an edge \(vw\) in \(G\), then \(\mathrm{tw}(H) \leq k\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 10.3.4.1</strong>. Graphs of treewidth at most \(k\) are closed under taking minors.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 10.3.4.2</strong>. Let \(H\) be a connected subgraph of \(G\). In any tree decomposition \(T\) of \(G\), the set of bags containing a vertex from \(H\) is connected.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 10.3.5</strong>. Any graph containing \(K_{k+1}\) as a minor has treewidth at least \(k\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 10.3.6</strong>. The treewidth of the \(k \times k\)-grid is exactly \(k\) for \(k \geq 2\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 10.3.6.1</strong>. If a graph \(G\) contains a \(k \times k\)-grid as a minor then \(\mathrm{tw}(G) \geq k\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 10.3.7</strong>. Every simple 2-terminal SP-graph has treewidth at most 2.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 10.3.7.1</strong>. SP-graphs are the same as partial 2-trees.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 10.3.8</strong>. If \(G\) is a simple SP-graph, then any simple minor \(G'\) of \(G\) has a vertex of degree at most 2.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Key properties of treewidth.&nbsp;</strong> (a) \(\omega(G) \leq \text{tw}(G) + 1\). (b) Treewidth is minor-monotone: \(\text{tw}(H) \leq \text{tw}(G)\) for any minor \(H\) of \(G\). (c) Any clique \(C\) of \(G\) appears entirely in some bag of every tree decomposition. (d) \(\text{tw}(k \times k\text{-grid}) = k\).</div>

Property (b) — that treewidth is minor-monotone — is why treewidth fits so naturally into the Robertson–Seymour theory of Chapter 6. Property (d) calibrates our intuition: the \(k \times k\)-grid is the "most tree-unlike" planar graph of its size, and treewidth exactly captures this.

![Treewidth-2 graph with its tree decomposition](/pics/cs762/treewidth-2-example.png)
<em>A series-parallel (treewidth-2) graph and its width-2 tree decomposition. Bags of size 3 correspond to the clique-number \(\omega = 3 = k+1\).</em>

### 4.4 k-Trees and Partial k-Trees

Treewidth is defined as a minimum over all possible tree decompositions — an existential definition that does not immediately suggest what graphs of treewidth exactly \(k\) look like. The \(k\)-tree provides the answer: it is the <em>canonical</em> graph of treewidth \(k\), obtained by the simplest possible inductive construction, and every graph of treewidth \(\leq k\) is a subgraph (partial \(k\)-tree) of one.

To make the concept of bounded treewidth concrete, it helps to have an explicit recursive construction. A \(k\)-tree is built by starting with a \(k\)-clique and repeatedly adding vertices, each connected to an existing \(k\)-clique. This is the simplest graph of treewidth exactly \(k\).

A \(k\)-tree is the "maximally dense" graph of treewidth \(k\): we cannot add any vertex without either raising treewidth above \(k\) or creating a duplicate. A <em>partial</em> \(k\)-tree is simply any subgraph of a \(k\)-tree — equivalently, any graph of treewidth at most \(k\). The partial 1-trees are forests. The partial 2-trees are exactly the series-parallel graphs. The partial 3-trees include all planar graphs of treewidth 3, and the Apollonian networks (defined in Chapter 2.5) are exactly the 3-trees. This concrete characterization via the inductive construction is what makes it easy to build tree decompositions explicitly: the PEO of the host \(k\)-tree provides the decomposition for any partial \(k\)-tree subgraph.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 10.1.1</strong>. The following are equivalent for a graph with \(n \geq 2\): (1) \(G\) is a tree; (2) \(G\) has a p.e.o. \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) with \(\mathrm{indeg}(v_i) = 1\) for \(i > 1\); (3) \(G\) is chordal with \(\omega(G) = 2\) and \(n - 1\) edges.

</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (\(k\)-Tree). A graph with at least \(k+1\) vertices and a PEO \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) where \(\text{indeg}(v_i) = k\) for all \(i > k\). (The first \(k+1\) vertices form a clique.)</div>

The inductive construction is transparent: each new vertex is connected to exactly \(k\) existing vertices forming a clique, so the structure grows "uniformly" in a tree-like fashion.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 10.1.2</strong>. Let \(G\) have \(n \geq k + 1\). Then \(G\) is a \(k\)-tree if and only if: (i) \(G\) is chordal; (ii) \(\omega(G) = k + 1\); (iii) \(m = kn - \binom{k}{2} - \frac{k}{2}\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 10.1.3</strong>. Every 2-tree is a 2-terminal SP-graph.

</div>

The \(k\)-trees are exactly the chordal graphs with \(\omega = k+1\) and the exact edge count \(m = kn - \binom{k}{2} - \frac{k}{2}\). Partial \(k\)-trees (spanning subgraphs of \(k\)-trees) are exactly the graphs of treewidth \(\leq k\).

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 10.2.1</strong> (Partial \(k\)-Tree). A spanning subgraph of a \(k\)-tree.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 10.2.1</strong>. Any chordal graph \(G\) with \(\omega(G) \leq k + 1\) is a partial \(k\)-tree.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 10.2.1.1</strong>. If \(G\) is a subgraph of a \(k\)-tree \(H\), then \(G\) is a partial \(k\)-tree.

</div>

**Planar instances:**
- Trees = partial 1-trees.
- Maximal outer-planar graphs = 2-trees; outer-planar graphs = partial 2-trees = SP-graphs.
- Apollonian networks = 3-trees; partial 3-trees include all planar graphs of low treewidth.

### 4.5 Series-Parallel Graphs

Series-parallel graphs are a particularly clean family of graphs with treewidth at most 2. They are defined by a recursive construction that mimics the series and parallel combinations of electrical circuits — a connection that is more than coincidental, since SP-graphs are exactly the graphs for which Kirchhoff's equations can be solved by simple elimination.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (2-Terminal SP-Graph). Built from a single edge by applying: (1) <em>series combination</em> — identify the target of \(G_1\) with the source of \(G_2\); (2) <em>parallel combination</em> — identify both sources and both targets.</div>

The recursive structure is captured by the SP-tree, which makes many algorithms on SP-graphs straightforward bottom-up computations.

The <strong>SP-tree</strong> is a binary tree encoding the construction. SP-graphs are exactly the partial 2-trees. Recognition runs in linear time by repeatedly contracting degree-2 vertices or removing parallel edges.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Every SP-graph has treewidth at most 2.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof.</strong> Build the tree decomposition directly from the SP-tree: label each node by its two terminals (plus the identified vertex for S-nodes). \(\square\)</div>

The proof is constructive: the SP-tree directly encodes a tree decomposition. This is a paradigm that recurs in more general settings — an explicit recursive construction of the graph gives an explicit tree decomposition.

### 4.6 Branchwidth and Pathwidth

Having established treewidth as the central parameter, it is natural to ask whether it is the <em>right</em> parameter for every application. The answer is nuanced. For most theoretical purposes — FPT algorithms, Courcelle's theorem, the Robertson–Seymour theory — treewidth is ideal. But for specific algorithmic tasks, particularly on planar graphs, two variants offer advantages: <strong>branchwidth</strong> (computable in polynomial time for planar graphs) and <strong>pathwidth</strong> (relevant for linear-arrangement problems and graph searching games).

Treewidth is not the only width parameter that captures tree-like structure. Two important variants — branchwidth and pathwidth — offer different trade-offs and are useful in different algorithmic contexts.

Think of the three parameters as measuring "tree-likeness" in different ways. <strong>Treewidth</strong> puts vertices in the bags and asks that the host tree have a connected subtree for each vertex. <strong>Branchwidth</strong> puts <em>edges</em> at the leaves of a binary tree and measures how many vertices are "shared" when you cut any arc. <strong>Pathwidth</strong> is the most restrictive: the host tree must be a path, meaning the bags are arranged in a linear sequence. A graph can have small treewidth but large pathwidth if its tree structure fans out in many directions — for instance, a balanced binary tree has treewidth 1 (it is a tree!) but pathwidth \(\Theta(\log n)\).

The three parameters are related by inequalities: \(\text{tw}(G) \leq \text{pw}(G)\) always, and \(\text{bw}(G)\) is within a factor of \(3/2\) of \(\text{tw}(G)\). But their computational complexity differs sharply. All three are NP-hard to compute exactly on general graphs. However, <strong>branchwidth of planar graphs</strong> can be computed exactly in \(O(n^2)\) time via the sphere-cut decomposition — a remarkable exception that makes branchwidth the preferred parameter for exact planar graph algorithms.

Pathwidth has an elegant alternative characterization as the <em>vertex-separation number</em> — the number of vertices one must "remember" when processing the graph left to right in the best linear arrangement. This connects pathwidth to graph searching (the "cop number" for node searching) and to register allocation in compilers, where the pathwidth of a program's dependency graph bounds the number of registers needed.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Branch Decomposition). A binary tree \(T\) with edges of \(G\) at leaves. The <em>separator</em> at each arc \(a\) is the set of vertices incident to edges in both subtrees. The <em>branchwidth</em> \(\text{bw}(G)\) is the minimum over all branch decompositions of the maximum separator size.</div>

The branch decomposition is defined in two equivalent ways:

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 11.1.1</strong> (e-Separation). A recursively defined partition of the edges: if \(G\) has more than one edge, partition \(E\) into non-empty sets \(E_1, E_2\), then recursively partition each.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 11.1.2</strong> (Rooted Branch Decomposition). A rooted binary tree describing an e-separation: edges of \(G\) at its leaves and an interior node for each recursive partition.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 11.1.3</strong> (Width of Branch Decomposition). For any link/arc \(a\) of \(T\), the separator is the set of vertices incident to edges in both subtrees of \(T - a\). The width is the largest separator size over all links.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 11.1.4</strong> (Branch-Width). The branch-width \(\mathrm{bw}(G)\) is the smallest width over all branch decompositions.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 11.1.5</strong> (Branch Decomposition). An unrooted binary tree \(T\) with maximum degree 3 in which the edges of \(G\) are mapped to distinct leaves.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 11.1.1</strong>. A graph has a rooted branch decomposition of width \(w\) if and only if it has an (unrooted) branch decomposition of width \(w\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 11.1.2</strong>. If \(G\) has branchwidth at most \(k\), then any minor \(H\) of \(G\) also has branchwidth at most \(k\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 11.1.2.1</strong>. Every SP-graph has branchwidth at most 2.

</div>

Branchwidth and treewidth measure essentially the same thing, as confirmed by the following quantitative relationship.

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Treewidth–Branchwidth). For any \(G\) with \(\text{bw}(G) \geq 2\):
\[ \text{bw}(G) \leq \text{tw}(G) + 1 \leq \frac{3}{2}\text{bw}(G). \]
</div>

The two parameters are within a constant factor of each other, so for most purposes they are interchangeable. However, branchwidth has a decisive computational advantage for planar graphs: it can be computed exactly in polynomial time.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 11.2.1</strong>. For any graph \(G\) with \(\mathrm{bw}(G) \geq 2\):
\[\mathrm{tw}(G) \leq \frac{3}{2}\,\mathrm{bw}(G) - 1.\]

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 11.2.2</strong>. For any graph \(G\): \(\mathrm{bw}(G) \leq \mathrm{tw}(G) + 1\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 11.3.1</strong> (Sphere-Cut Decomposition). A branch decomposition of a planar graph \(G\) such that for every separator \(\sigma_a\) of a link \(a\), there is a noose (closed Jordan curve) of \(G\) containing exactly the vertices of \(\sigma_a\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 11.3.1</strong>. If a planar graph has branchwidth \(k\), then it also has a sphere-cut decomposition of width \(k\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 11.3.2</strong>. The branchwidth of a planar graph can be computed in \(O(n^2)\) time.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 11.3.2</strong> (Co-Tree). Given a spanning tree \(T\) in a connected planar graph \(G\), the co-tree \(T^<em>\) is the subgraph of \(G^</em>\) formed by the duals of edges in \(E \setminus E(T)\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 11.3.3</strong>. Let \(G\) be a connected planar graph with spanning tree \(T\). Then the co-tree \(T^<em>\) is a spanning tree of \(G^</em>\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 11.3.4</strong>. Let \(G\) be a triangulated planar graph with a spanning tree \(T\) of height \(R\). Then \(\mathrm{bw}(G) \leq 2R + 1\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 11.3.4.1</strong>. If \(G\) is planar with a spanning tree \(T\) of height \(R\), then \(\mathrm{bw}(G) \leq 2R + 1\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 11.3.4.2</strong>. Let \(G\) be a planar graph with a spanning tree of height \(R\). Then \(\mathrm{tw}(G) \leq 3R\).

</div>

Branchwidth of planar graphs is computable in \(O(n^2)\) time (and the optimal branch decomposition is sphere-cut). This gives a \(\frac{3}{2}\)-approximation for treewidth of planar graphs.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (Path Decomposition / Pathwidth). A tree decomposition where the host tree is a path. The <em>pathwidth</em> \(\text{pw}(G)\) is the minimum width over all path decompositions.</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.1.1</strong> (Path Decomposition). A tree decomposition \(T\) for which the host tree is a path.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.1.2</strong> (Pathwidth). The pathwidth \(\mathrm{pw}(G)\) of a graph \(G\) is the smallest \(k\) such that \(G\) has a path decomposition of width \(k\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 12.1.1</strong>. A graph \(G\) has pathwidth at most \(k\) if and only if \(G\) is a spanning subgraph of an interval graph \(G'\) with \(\omega(G') \leq k + 1\).

</div>

Pathwidth is a stronger condition than treewidth: \(\text{tw}(G) \leq \text{pw}(G)\) always, and the gap can be logarithmic. But for linear-time algorithms and certain graph searching problems, pathwidth is the natural parameter.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 12.2.1</strong>. A tree has pathwidth at most \(k > 0\) if and only if there is a path \(P\) in \(T\) such that all subtrees of \(T - P\) have pathwidth at most \(k - 1\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.2.1</strong> (\(k\)-Caterpillar). A 0-caterpillar is a single vertex. A \(k\)-caterpillar (\(k > 0\)) is a tree \(T\) with a path \(P\) (spine) such that every component of \(T - P\) is an \(\ell\)-caterpillar for some \(\ell < k\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 12.2.1.1</strong>. For any tree \(T\), the pathwidth \(\mathrm{pw}(T)\) equals the smallest \(k\) for which \(T\) is a \(k\)-caterpillar.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 12.2.2</strong>. Any tree with \(n\) nodes has \(\mathrm{pw}(T) \leq \log_2 n\).

</div>

The following linear-arrangement definitions are the key to proving the pathwidth equivalences.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.3.1</strong> (Cut-Edges). For a linear arrangement \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\), \(\mathrm{Cut}[i-1:i]\) is the set of edges in the cut induced by \(\{v_1, \ldots, v_{i-1}\}\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.3.2</strong> (Separation-Vertices of the Cut). \(\mathrm{Vs}[i-1:i]\) are the vertices in \(\{v_1, \ldots, v_{i-1}\}\) that have incident edges in \(\mathrm{Cut}[i-1:i]\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.3.3</strong> (Cutwidth). \(G\) has cutwidth at most \(k\) if there is a linear arrangement with \(|\mathrm{Cut}[i-1:i]| \leq k\) for all \(i\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.3.4</strong> (Vertex-Separation Number). \(G\) has vertex-separation number at most \(k\) if there is a linear arrangement with \(|\mathrm{Vs}[i-1:i]| \leq k\) for all \(i\).

</div>

*A path decomposition: bags are arranged linearly, each consecutive pair differs by at most one vertex (in a "nice" decomposition).*

<div class="theorem">
<strong>Theorem</strong> (Pathwidth equivalences). The following are equivalent:
1. \(\text{pw}(G) \leq k\).
2. \(G\) has a path decomposition of width \(k\).
3. \(G\) is a spanning subgraph of an interval graph \(H\) with \(\omega(H) \leq k+1\).
4. \(G\) has vertex-separation number \(\leq k\): some linear arrangement has \(|\text{Vs}[i-1:i]| \leq k\) for all cuts.
</div>

The equivalence between pathwidth and interval graph containment mirrors the equivalence between treewidth and chordal graph containment. In both cases, the "forbidden subgraph" perspective and the "decomposition" perspective give the same parameter.

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 12.4.1</strong> (Pathwidth Equivalences). The following are equivalent: (1) \(\mathrm{pw}(G) \leq k\); (2) \(G\) has a path decomposition with all bags of size \(\leq k+1\); (3) \(G\) is a subgraph of an interval graph \(H\) with \(\omega(H) \leq k+1\); (4) \(G\) has an interval representation where at most \(k+1\) intervals intersect at any point; (5) \(G\) has vertex-separation number at most \(k\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.5.1</strong> (Line Graph). The line graph \(L(G)\) has vertex \(v_e\) for each \(e \in E(G)\) and an edge \((v_e, v_{e'})\) iff \(e, e'\) share an endpoint.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 12.5.1</strong>. For any graph \(G\): \(\mathrm{pw}(L(G)) \leq \mathrm{cutwidth}(G) + \lfloor \Delta/2 \rfloor - 1\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 12.5.2</strong>. For any connected graph \(G\) with \(n \geq 3\): \(\mathrm{cutwidth}(G) \leq \mathrm{pw}(L(G))\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 12.5.2.1</strong>. For any graph \(G\) with \(\Delta(G) \leq 3\): \(\mathrm{cutwidth}(G) = \mathrm{pw}(L(G))\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.6.1</strong> (Weighted Cutwidth). For a graph \(G\) with positive edge-weights \(w : E \to \mathbb{R}^+\), the weighted cutwidth is at most \(k\) if there is a linear arrangement with \(\sum_{e \in \mathrm{Cut}[i-1:i]} w(e) \leq k\) for all cuts.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 12.6.1</strong>. Computing the weighted cutwidth of a graph is NP-hard, even in trees.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 12.6.2</strong> (Strongly NP-Hard). A weighted decision problem is strongly NP-hard when it remains NP-hard even if all weights are polynomial in the input size.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 12.6.2</strong>. Computing the weighted cutwidth is strongly NP-hard, even in trees.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 12.6.2.1</strong>. Computing the cutwidth is NP-hard even in a simple series-parallel graph of pathwidth 3.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 12.6.3</strong>. Computing the cutwidth is NP-hard even in planar graphs with maximum degree 3.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 12.6.3.1</strong>. Computing the pathwidth is NP-hard even in planar graphs with maximum degree 4.

</div>

**Pathwidth of trees.** Any tree has pathwidth at most \(\log_2 n\). A tree is a \(k\)-caterpillar iff its pathwidth is exactly \(k\); this gives a recursive characterization computable in linear time.

**Computing width parameters is NP-hard:** treewidth, pathwidth, and cutwidth are all NP-hard to compute exactly, even in restricted graph families. However, computing branchwidth of planar graphs is polynomial. This computational gap between exact treewidth computation (NP-hard) and the FPT algorithms that use treewidth as a parameter motivates the approximation algorithm discussed in Section 6.2.

## Chapter 5: Fixed-Parameter Tractability

Treewidth was introduced in Chapter 4 as a structural parameter measuring the "tree-likeness" of a graph. This chapter cashes in on that investment: graphs of small treewidth admit efficient *dynamic programming* algorithms for a wide range of hard combinatorial problems. The key insight is that a tree decomposition breaks a problem into subproblems that interact only through small "interfaces" — the bags — and DP over the decomposition solves the original problem in time exponential only in the treewidth, not in \(n\).

### 5.1 Dynamic Programming in Partial \(k\)-Trees

Why does bounded treewidth enable efficient dynamic programming? The answer lies in the *separation property* of tree decompositions. Consider any node \(i\) in the decomposition tree and remove its bag \(X_i\). The tree splits into subtrees, and the corresponding subgraphs of \(G\) can only "communicate" through \(X_i\): no edge crosses from one side to the other without touching \(X_i\). This is exactly the condition needed to apply the DP principle — once you fix the "state" of the vertices in \(X_i\), the two halves become independent subproblems. With \(|X_i| \leq k+1\), the number of states is at most \(2^{k+1}\) (for problems where the state is a subset of the bag), so the total work per node is exponential in \(k\) but only polynomial in \(n\).

To make this concrete: when we solve **maximum weight independent set** by DP on a tree decomposition, the state at each bag \(X_i\) is the intersection of the independent set with \(X_i\) — just a subset of \(k+1\) vertices. The DP table has \(O(2^k)\) entries per node, and there are \(O(n)\) nodes, giving an \(O(2^k n)\) algorithm. This is fixed-parameter tractable: polynomial in \(n\) for any fixed \(k\), with only the exponential dependence on \(k\) paying the price of generality.

Dynamic programming on trees exploits the fact that subtrees interact only through their root. Tree decompositions generalize this: the *bag* at a join node is the "interface" between two subproblems. The DP state records all information about the solved subgraph that is relevant to the remaining decisions — precisely the contents of the current bag.

Here is the fundamental template that underlies all DP algorithms on tree decompositions. Root the decomposition tree. For each node \(i\) (processed bottom-up), and for each possible "state" of the vertices in the current bag \(X_i\), compute the best solution for the subgraph \(G[Y_i]\) (all vertices in bags at or below \(i\)) that is *consistent* with that state. The key insight is that the state only needs to describe what is in the current bag — not the entire history of how we got here. The bag acts as a firewall: once you know the state of \(X_i\), the two sides of the decomposition are independent.

For **maximum weight independent set**, the state of bag \(X_i\) is simply a subset \(Z \subseteq X_i\) — the intersection of the independent set \(I\) with the current bag. There are at most \(2^{k+1}\) such subsets if the bag has size \(k+1\). The DP transitions are:
- At a **leaf node**: check directly whether \(Z\) is an independent set.
- At an **introduce node** (adding vertex \(x\)): if \(x \in Z\), verify no neighbor of \(x\) in the bag is also in \(Z\), and add \(w(x)\) to the child value.
- At a **forget node** (removing vertex \(x\)): take the maximum over states \(Z \cup \{x\}\) and \(Z \setminus \{x\}\) from the child.
- At a **join node** (merging two independent subtrees): add the values from the two children and subtract \(w(Z)\) to avoid double-counting the bag vertices.

This same template — with different state definitions — applies to weighted dominating set, maximum matching, graph coloring, and any other property expressible in terms of local constraints on the vertices in each bag. The exponential factor \(2^k\) in the runtime reflects the number of bag states; the linear factor in \(n\) reflects the size of the decomposition tree.

**DP on trees — maximum weight independent set.** Define \(\alpha(v, 1)\) (best IS weight in \(T_v\) including \(v\)) and \(\alpha(v, 0)\) (excluding \(v\)):

\[
\alpha(v, 0) = \sum_{c \text{ child}} \alpha(c, ?), \quad
\alpha(v, 1) = w(v) + \sum_{c \text{ child}} \alpha(c, 0)
\]

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 13.3.1</strong> (Nice Path Decomposition). A path decomposition where consecutive bags differ by exactly one vertex (alternating introduce and forget bags), starting from a leaf bag.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 13.3.1</strong>. Any graph with pathwidth \(k\) has a nice path decomposition of width \(k\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 13.3.2</strong>. Let \(G\) be a graph with a path decomposition of width \(k\). The maximum-weight independent set of \(G\) can be found in \(O(2^k n)\) time.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 13.4.1</strong> (Nice Tree Decomposition). A tree decomposition \(T\) that is rooted such that every node is one of: leaf (no children), introduce (one child, adds vertex \(x\)), forget (one child, removes vertex \(x\)), or join (two children with identical bags).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 13.4.1</strong>. Any partial \(k\)-tree has a nice tree decomposition of width \(k\) with \(O(n)\) nodes.

</div>

**Nice Tree Decomposition.** Every partial \(k\)-tree has a tree decomposition in which each node is one of four types:
- **Leaf node:** no children, single vertex.
- **Introduce node:** one child; \(X_i = X_j \cup \{x\}\).
- **Forget node:** one child; \(X_i = X_j \setminus \{x\}\).
- **Join node:** two children; \(X_i = X_{j_1} = X_{j_2}\).

<div class="key-box">

<strong>Algorithm: Maximum Weight Independent Set on Partial \(k\)-Trees</strong>

For node \(i\) and \(Z \subseteq X_i\), define \(\alpha(i, Z)\) = max weight of an IS \(I\) in \(G[Y_i]\) with \(I \cap X_i = Z\).

- <strong>Leaf:</strong> \(\alpha(1, Z) = w(Z)\) if \(Z\) is an IS, else \(-\infty\).
- <strong>Introduce</strong> \(x\): if \(Z\) is not IS, \(-\infty\); if \(x \notin Z\), copy from child; if \(x \in Z\), add \(w(x)\).
- <strong>Forget</strong> \(x\): \(\alpha(i, Z) = \max\{\alpha(j, Z \cup \{x\}), \alpha(j, Z)\}\).
- <strong>Join:</strong> \(\alpha(i, Z) = \alpha(j_1, Z) + \alpha(j_2, Z) - w(Z)\) (avoid double-counting).

<strong>Runtime:</strong> \(O(k \cdot 2^k \cdot n)\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 13.4.2</strong>. Let \(G\) be a graph with a tree decomposition of width \(k\). The maximum-weight independent set of \(G\) can be found in \(O(k \cdot 2^k \cdot n)\) time.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Maximum weight independent set on a graph with a given tree decomposition of width \(k\) can be solved in \(O(k \cdot 2^k \cdot n)\) time. Similarly for maximum matching and weighted dominating set.</div>

This result is fundamental: it shows that the exponential dependence on \(k\) is the price of generality, while the polynomial dependence on \(n\) means the algorithm is efficient when \(k\) is small. For planar graphs, \(k = O(\sqrt{n})\) from the separator theorem, giving sub-exponential overall runtime.

### 5.2 Fixed-Parameter Tractability

The DP algorithm for independent set on partial \(k\)-trees is an instance of a broader paradigm: <em>fixed-parameter tractability</em>, where the "hard" part of the computation depends only on a structural parameter \(k\), not on the input size \(n\).

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 13.5.1</strong> (Fixed-Parameter Tractable). Given a problem with input size \(n\) and parameter \(k\), the problem is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) if there is an algorithm with runtime \(O(f(k) \cdot \mathrm{poly}(k, n))\) for some function \(f\).

</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (FPT). A problem with input size \(n\) and parameter \(k\) is <em>fixed-parameter tractable</em> (FPT) if there is an algorithm running in \(O(f(k) \cdot \text{poly}(n))\) time for some (possibly non-polynomial) function \(f\).</div>

FPT is the appropriate notion of tractability when the parameter \(k\) is small in practice even if the input is large. Parameterized complexity theory (the study of what is and is not FPT) is a rich field; here we focus on treewidth as the parameter.

Independent set on partial \(k\)-trees is FPT parameterized by treewidth. The key observation: for any problem that can be solved by the bag-DP scheme, the number of states per node is at most \(2^{k+1}\), and there are \(O(n)\) nodes.

### 5.3 Monadic Second-Order Logic and Courcelle's Theorem

Every specific DP algorithm on tree decompositions — independent set, dominating set, Hamiltonian cycle, matching — follows the same template: define a state space, write down transitions, read off the answer. Courcelle's theorem asks: is there a <em>single</em> algorithm that handles all such problems automatically? The answer is yes, provided we can express the problem in the right logical language.

The language is <strong>Monadic Second-Order Logic (MSOL)</strong>: first-order logic augmented with variables ranging over <em>sets</em> of vertices (or edges), and set membership as a predicate. MSOL is expressive enough to capture connectivity, colorability, Hamiltonicity, and most natural graph properties — yet it is restricted enough that model checking on bounded-treewidth graphs is efficient. The key insight is that the DP automaton for any MSOL formula can be computed from the formula itself, and its state space depends only on the formula and the treewidth, not on the input graph.

Rather than designing bespoke DP algorithms for each problem, Courcelle's theorem provides a meta-theorem: any property expressible in a suitable logic is automatically FPT in treewidth. This is one of the most powerful and elegant results in algorithmic graph theory.

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 13.6.1</strong> (Monadic Second-Order Logic). A logic formula consisting of: variables over vertices or vertex sets; membership tests \(v \in I\); boolean operations \(\neg, \wedge, \vee, \Rightarrow\); monadic quantifiers \(\exists, \forall\) over vertices and vertex sets; and the adjacency predicate \(\mathrm{adj}(v, w)\). MSOL2 additionally allows variables over edge sets and the predicate \(\mathrm{adj}(u, v, E')\).

</div>

**MSOL** allows quantification over vertices and vertex sets, membership tests, boolean operations, adjacency predicates \(\text{adj}(u, v)\), and monadic quantifiers. **MSOL2** additionally allows edge sets and edge-incidence predicates.

**Expressible properties:** graph connectivity, bipartiteness, \(k\)-colorability, Hamiltonicity (MSOL2), perfect matching (MSOL2), dominating set, independent set, and many more.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Courcelle, 1990). If a graph property \(\Pi\) can be expressed in MSOL2, then testing whether \(G\) satisfies \(\Pi\) is FPT in \(\text{tw}(G)\). The runtime is linear in \(n\) when both treewidth and the formula size are constant.</div>

Courcelle's theorem is one of the most powerful meta-theorems in algorithmic graph theory: instead of designing bespoke DP algorithms, one can merely formalize the problem in MSOL2 and apply the theorem. The price paid is a function \(f(k)\) that can be enormous — towers of exponentials in \(k\) — but for small constant \(k\), the linear-in-\(n\) runtime is practically useful.

## Chapter 6: Graph Minors

The final chapter brings together the structural and algorithmic perspectives developed throughout the course.

We have now built a complete toolkit: planar graphs and their embeddings (Chapters 1–2), separators and treewidth (Chapters 3–4), and FPT algorithms on bounded-treewidth graphs (Chapter 5). The question now becomes: how do we bring these tools to bear on graphs that are not already of bounded treewidth? And what is the ultimate organizing principle that explains why planar graphs, bounded-genus graphs, and bounded-treewidth graphs all admit good algorithms?

The answer is <strong>graph minors</strong>. A graph \(H\) is a minor of \(G\) if \(H\) can be obtained from \(G\) by deleting vertices, deleting edges, and contracting edges. Minor containment is a powerful and natural quasi-order on graphs: it respects all the structural properties we care about (planarity, treewidth, genus are all minor-monotone), and it has the remarkable property — the Robertson–Seymour theorem — of being a <em>well-quasi-order</em> on all finite graphs.

This chapter develops three major results. First, <strong>Baker's scheme</strong>: an approximation framework for planar graphs that uses the onion-peeling structure to reduce to bounded-treewidth subproblems. Second, the <strong>Grid Minor Theorem</strong>: a fundamental dichotomy asserting that every graph either has small treewidth or contains a large grid as a minor. Third, the <strong>Robertson–Seymour Graph Minor Theorem</strong>: every minor-closed class has finitely many forbidden minors, giving polynomial-time recognition algorithms for any such class. Graph minors provide the "right" notion of substructure for planar and bounded-treewidth graphs: a graph \(H\) is a minor of \(G\) if \(H\) can be obtained by deleting and contracting edges. The Robertson–Seymour Graph Minor Theorem — proved over two decades in twenty papers — is the culmination: every minor-closed class of graphs has a finite forbidden minor characterization, leading to polynomial-time recognition algorithms for any minor-closed class.

### 6.1 \(k\)-Outer-Planar Graphs and Baker's Scheme

Before reaching the full power of the Robertson–Seymour theory, we develop Baker's PTAS for planar graph optimization. The key structural fact is that planar graphs have a hierarchical "onion" structure — successive outer-face deletions — and graphs that are deep in this hierarchy have small treewidth, enabling exact DP.

Baker's scheme (1994) is one of the most elegant ideas in approximation algorithms. The central observation is that a planar graph can be "peeled" like an onion: remove the outer face to get a smaller planar graph, repeat. The \(i\)-th layer \(L_i\) consists of the vertices on the outer face after \(i-1\) peelings. A graph with only \(k\) layers is <em>\(k\)-outer-planar</em> and has treewidth at most \(3k\) — giving an exact DP solution in time exponential in \(k\) but linear in \(n\).

The PTAS idea is then disarmingly simple: for a \((1-\epsilon)\)-approximation, choose \(k = \lceil 1/\epsilon \rceil\). Partition the layers into \(k\) groups — layers \(\{1, k+1, 2k+1, \ldots\}\), layers \(\{2, k+2, 2k+2, \ldots\}\), and so on. For each group \(i\), remove the layers in group \(i\) from the graph; the remainder is \(k\)-outer-planar (the layers "close up" after removal) and solvable exactly by DP. By an averaging argument, at least one of the \(k\) choices loses at most a \(1/k \leq \epsilon\) fraction of the optimal solution weight. Return the best of the \(k\) solutions. The total runtime is \(O(k \cdot 2^{3k} \cdot n)\) — polynomial in \(n\) for fixed \(\epsilon\), and as \(\epsilon \to 0\) the exponent grows, giving the classic PTAS tradeoff.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (\(k\)-Outer-Planar). A planar graph embeddable so that all vertices are removed by successively deleting the outer face \(k\) times. (\emph{Onion peeling:} \(L_1, L_2, \ldots\) are the successive outer-face vertex sets.)</div>

The \(k\)-outer-planar graphs are the planar graphs of "bounded depth" in the onion peeling order. Their bounded treewidth follows from the onion structure. Some combinatorial properties:

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 14.1.1</strong>. Let \(G\) be a plane graph with onion peels \(L_1, \ldots, L_\ell\). Any edge connects the same or adjacent onion peels.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 14.1.2</strong>. For any \(i, k \geq 1\), the graph induced by onion peels \(L_{i+1}, \ldots, L_{i+k}\) is \(k\)-outer-planar.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 14.1.3</strong>. Let \(G\) be a planar graph with spanning tree \(T\) of height \(k\) rooted at \(r \in V\). Then \(G\) is \((k+1)\)-outer-planar.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 14.2.1</strong>. Let \(G\) be a \(k\)-outer-planar graph and \(r\) a vertex on the outer face. Edges can be added to \(G\) while maintaining planarity such that \(G\) has a spanning tree rooted at \(r\) with height at most \(k\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 14.2.2</strong>. Every \(k\)-outer-planar graph has treewidth at most \(3k\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 14.2.3</strong>. Any \(k\)-outer-planar graph can be made triangulated by adding edges such that the result is \((k+1)\)-outer-planar.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Every \(k\)-outer-planar graph has treewidth at most \(3k\).</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Add edges to produce a spanning tree of height \(k\) (root on outer face; inductively connect each \(L_i\) vertex to a neighbour in \(L_{i-1}\)). By the branchwidth–spanning tree bound, \(\text{bw} \leq 2k+1\), hence \(\text{tw} \leq 3k\). \(\square\)</div>

This treewidth bound is the engine of Baker's PTAS: by discarding one onion layer in eleven, we make the remainder \(k\)-outer-planar and thus tractable by DP.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 14.3.1</strong>. Any planar graph \(G\) has an induced subgraph \(H\) that is outer-planar and has at least \(n/2\) vertices.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 14.3.2</strong>. Fix a planar embedding and compute onion peels \(L_i\). Set \(V_1\) to be the union of odd peels and \(V_2\) the even peels. Each \(V_i\) is outer-planar (treewidth \(\leq 3\)), and the better of the two independent sets \(I_i\) in \(G \setminus V_i\) achieves weight at least \(w(I^*)/2\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 14.3.3</strong>. Any planar graph can be made \(k\)-outer-planar by removing at most \(n/(k+1)\) vertices.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 14.3.4</strong>. There exists a PTAS for independent set in planar graphs.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Baker's PTAS, 1994). For every \(\epsilon > 0\), there is a polynomial-time \((1-\epsilon)\)-approximation for maximum weight independent set in planar graphs.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> Choose \(k = \lceil 1/\epsilon \rceil - 1\). Partition the onion peels into \(k+1\) groups \(V_1^{(k+1)}, \ldots, V_{k+1}^{(k+1)}\). For each \(i\), the graph \(G_i = G \setminus V_i^{(k+1)}\) is \(k\)-outer-planar (hence \(\text{tw} \leq 3k\)) and has an optimal IS \(I_i\) computable in \(O(k \cdot 2^{3k} \cdot n)\) time. At least one \(I_i\) achieves \(w(I_i) \geq (1-\epsilon)w(I^*)\). \(\square\)</div>

Baker's scheme is elegant in its simplicity: try all \(k+1\) possible "layers to remove," solve the remaining bounded-treewidth problem exactly, and take the best result. The averaging argument guarantees that at least one attempt loses at most an \(\epsilon\) fraction of the optimum.

The approximation generalizes to any problem polynomial on bounded-treewidth graphs for any graph class with locally bounded treewidth (bounded genus graphs, 1-planar graphs, etc.).

### 6.2 Approximating Treewidth

Computing treewidth exactly is NP-hard even for very restricted graph families. This creates a practical dilemma: the FPT algorithms of Chapter 5 need a tree decomposition as input, but producing one is itself hard. The resolution is approximation. For algorithmic purposes, a decomposition of width at most \(4k + 4\) is nearly as useful as one of width \(k\): the DP tables grow by at most a constant exponent. The following algorithm achieves a 4-approximation and is itself FPT in \(k\), making the overall pipeline FPT even when the decomposition must be computed from scratch.

But for many applications, an approximate tree decomposition suffices — as long as its width is within a constant factor of optimal. The following result gives a practical FPT algorithm that either certifies small treewidth or reports that treewidth is large.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (4-approximation). There is an algorithm that, given \(G\) and \(k\), either correctly reports \(\text{tw}(G) > k\) or returns a tree decomposition of width \(\leq 4k + 4\). Runtime: \(O(8^k \cdot k^2 \cdot n^2)\).</div>

The key subroutine: for a set \(W \subseteq V\), finding the minimum \(W\)-separation of balance \(\frac{2}{3}\) reduces to minimum vertex cuts between auxiliary sources (Menger's theorem), with \(2^{|W|}\) bipartitions to try. This expands to a full tree decomposition by a recursive application that is FPT in \(k\).

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 16.1.1</strong> (\((\alpha, W)\)-Separator). Let \(G\) be a graph and \(W \subseteq V\). An \((\alpha, W)\)-separator is an \(\alpha\)-separator with respect to the weight function \(\chi_W\) (indicator of \(W\)).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 16.1.2</strong> (2-Way Separation). A partition \(V = A_1 \cup S \cup A_2\) with no edges between \(A_1\) and \(A_2\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 16.1.3</strong> (Balance). A 2-way separation has balance \(\alpha\) if \(w(A_1), w(A_2) \leq \alpha w(V)\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 16.1.4</strong> (Separation Size). The separation size is \(|S|\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 16.1.1</strong>. If \(G\) has an \(\alpha\)-separator \(S\), then \(G\) has a separation \((A_1, S, A_2)\) with balance \(\max(\alpha, 2/3)\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 16.1.2</strong>. Let \(G\) be a graph and \(W \subseteq V\). For any \(\alpha \geq 2/3\), there is an algorithm that finds the smallest \(W\)-separation of balance \(\alpha\) in \(O(2^{|W|} |W|(n+m))\) time.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 16.1.5</strong> (\(W\)-Tree-Decomposition). A tree decomposition of \(G\) in which all vertices of \(W\) appear in one bag.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 16.1.3</strong>. Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be an induced-closed graph class where any \(G \in \mathcal{G}\) has a \((\frac{D-1}{D}, W)\)-separator of size at most \(w\) for any \(|W| \leq Dw + 1\) and \(D \in \{2,3,4\}\). Then \(G\) has a \(W\)-tree-decomposition of width at most \((D+1)w\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 16.1.4</strong>. Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be an induced-closed graph class with \(\frac{D-1}{D}\)-separators of size at most \(w\) for some \(D \in \{2,3,4\}\). Any graph in \(\mathcal{G}\) has treewidth at most \((D+1)w\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 16.1.4.1</strong>. Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be an induced-closed graph class with \(\frac{1}{2}\)-separators of size \(k+1\). Then the graphs in \(\mathcal{G}\) have treewidth at most \(3k + 3\).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 16.1.5</strong>. There is an algorithm that either correctly reports \(\mathrm{tw}(G) > k\) or finds a \(W\)-tree-decomposition of width at most \(4k + 4\), in time \(O(8^k k^2 n^2)\).

</div>

### 6.3 Grid Minors

The Grid Minor Theorem is the deepest structural result in the course. It establishes a fundamental dichotomy: a graph either has small treewidth (and is thus algorithmically tractable) or contains a large grid as a minor (witnessing that it is "essentially planar at large scale" and thus as hard as the hardest planar instances). This dichotomy is the cornerstone of the Robertson–Seymour theory.

Why is the grid the right "universal" large-treewidth graph? The \(k \times k\)-grid has treewidth exactly \(k\) — this is Lemma 10.3.6, and it is tight. If we try to separate the grid into two pieces of size \(\leq n/2\), we must cut at least \(k\) vertices (one per row or column). The grid is thus the canonical hard case for treewidth: dense, symmetric, with no shortcuts.

The Grid Minor Theorem turns this observation into a characterization: *large treewidth is the only obstruction to structural simplicity*. More precisely, if \(\text{tw}(G) \geq f(k)\), then \(G\) must contain a \(k \times k\)-grid as a minor. The function \(f(k)\) has been substantially improved over the decades: Robertson and Seymour's original bound was super-exponential, but Chekuri and Chuzhoy (2016) showed \(f(k) = k^{O(1)}\) (polynomial). For **planar graphs**, the theorem is cleaner: either \(G\) contains a \(k \times k\)-grid minor, or \(\text{tw}(G) \leq 5k - 5\). This linear bound is the engine behind bidimensionality theory — a broad framework for designing FPT algorithms on planar graphs by showing that the solution size on a \(k \times k\)-grid grows as \(\Theta(k^2)\), so bounded solution size forces bounded treewidth and thus efficient DP.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Grid Minor Theorem — Robertson & Seymour). There exists a function \(f(k)\) such that any graph with treewidth at least \(f(k)\) contains a \(k \times k\)-grid as a minor.</div>

The Grid Minor Theorem gives the converse to the trivial observation that the \(k \times k\)-grid has treewidth \(k\): large treewidth <em>forces</em> the presence of a large grid minor.

For <strong>planar graphs</strong>, this dichotomy is particularly clean:

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 16.2.1</strong>. Let \(G\) be a plane graph and \(W\) a set of at most \(4k - 4\) vertices on the outer face. Then either \(G\) has a \(k \times k\)-grid as a minor, or we can find a \(W\)-tree-decomposition of width at most \(5k - 5\).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 16.2.1.1</strong>. For any planar graph \(G\) and any fixed \(k\), there is an \(O(n^2)\) algorithm that either returns a \(k \times k\)-grid minor or a tree decomposition of width at most \(5k - 5\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 16.2.2</strong>. Let \(G\) be a planar graph. Either \(G\) contains a \(k \times k\)-grid as a minor (so \(\mathrm{tw}(G) \geq k\)) or \(\mathrm{tw}(G) \leq 5k - 5\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 16.2.3</strong>. Testing whether \(H\) is a minor of \(G\) can be done in \(O(f(h, \mathrm{tw}(G)) \cdot n)\) time for some function \(f\), by encoding the minor condition in MSOL and applying Courcelle's theorem.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 16.2.4</strong>. Let \(H, G\) be two planar graphs on \(h, n\) vertices. Testing whether \(H\) is a minor of \(G\) can be done in \(O(f(h)n + n^2)\) time.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 16.2.5</strong>. There is a function \(f(k)\) such that any graph of treewidth at least \(f(k)\) contains a \(k \times k\)-grid minor.

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 16.2.5.1</strong>. Let \(H\) be a planar graph with \(h\) vertices and \(G\) an arbitrary graph. Testing whether \(H\) is a minor of \(G\) can be done in \(O(f(h) \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n))\) time.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 16.3.1</strong>. There is an algorithm testing if \(G\) has a path of length \(\ell\) with runtime \(O(f(\ell)(n+m))\).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 16.3.2</strong>. Given a planar graph \(G\) and an integer \(\ell\), there is an \(O(f(\sqrt{\ell})n + n^2)\) algorithm testing whether \(G\) has a path of length \(\ell\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 16.3.1</strong> (Contraction Bi-Dimensional). A problem \(P\) is contraction bi-dimensional if: (i) if \(P\) has solution size \(\ell\) in \(G\), it has solution size at most \(\ell\) in any minor; (ii) in the \(k \times k\)-grid, \(P\) has solution size at least \(ck^2\); (iii) \(P\) is FPT in treewidth.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> For any planar \(G\), either \(G\) contains a \(k \times k\)-grid minor (so \(\text{tw}(G) \geq k\)) or \(\text{tw}(G) \leq 5k - 5\). This can be decided in \(O(n^2)\) time.</div>

The proof uses right-first search to find \(k\) vertex-disjoint \(v_N v_S\)-paths and \(k\) vertex-disjoint \(v_E v_W\)-paths; intersecting them gives the grid minor. The planarity of the graph is essential: in a non-planar graph, finding disjoint crossing paths is much harder.

### 6.4 Testing Minor Membership

The Grid Minor Theorem has a striking algorithmic consequence: testing whether a fixed graph \(H\) is a minor of an input graph \(G\) is polynomial for any fixed \(H\). The argument is two-step. First, check whether \(\text{tw}(G) \leq f(|H|)\): if not, by the Grid Minor Theorem \(G\) contains a large grid minor, from which one can extract any fixed pattern. If yes, the treewidth is bounded by a constant depending only on \(H\), and Courcelle's theorem gives a linear-time algorithm. This yields a polynomial runtime for each fixed \(H\) — a remarkable unification of the structural and logical approaches.

The Grid Minor Theorem and Courcelle's theorem combine to give a general minor testing algorithm: express the minor embedding in MSOL2 and apply the treewidth-based FPT algorithm. For planar minors, an even cleaner result is available.

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem.</strong> Testing whether \(H\) is a minor of \(G\) can be done in \(O(f(|H|, \text{tw}(G)) \cdot n)\) time (via Courcelle's theorem applied to an MSOL2 formula encoding the existence of vertex-disjoint connected subsets \(V_1, \ldots, V_h\) with the right adjacencies).</div>

This result says that, for graphs of bounded treewidth, minor testing is linear in \(n\) for any fixed \(H\). The exponential dependence on \(|H|\) is unavoidable in general, but for small patterns it is not an obstacle.

For planar \(H\) and general \(G\):

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Robertson–Seymour). Testing whether a planar graph \(H\) is a minor of an arbitrary graph \(G\) runs in \(O(f(|H|) \cdot \text{poly}(n))\) time.</div>

The planarity of the pattern \(H\) is what makes this possible: planar patterns have "local" embedding conditions that can be checked efficiently.

### 6.5 The Graph Minor Theorem

We arrive at the crown jewel of the entire course, and one of the deepest theorems in twentieth-century combinatorics: the <strong>Robertson–Seymour Graph Minor Theorem</strong>. To appreciate its power, recall the structure of the course. We have seen that <em>planar graphs</em> are characterized by two forbidden minors (\(K_5\) and \(K_{3,3}\)). We have seen that <em>forests</em> are characterized by one forbidden minor (\(K_3 = C_3\)). And we have seen that graphs of treewidth at most 2 are characterized by one forbidden minor (\(K_4\)). In each case, a minor-closed class is described by finitely many "obstructions." Is this always the case?

The naive answer might be "no" — after all, chordal graphs require forbidding infinitely many induced subgraphs (\(C_4, C_5, C_6, \ldots\)). But the key is the choice of relation. The Robertson–Seymour theorem asserts that for the <strong>minor</strong> relation, the answer is always yes: every minor-closed class is described by finitely many forbidden minors, no matter how complex the class. The proof required twenty papers and over two decades of work; it is widely regarded as the deepest theorem in graph theory.

The logical heart of the theorem is <strong>well-quasi-ordering (WQO)</strong>. A partial order is WQO if every infinite sequence contains an increasing pair — equivalently, there are no infinite antichains. The Robertson–Seymour theorem asserts that the minor order on all finite graphs is WQO. This is not obvious: the minor relation is defined by a complex sequence of operations (deletion, contraction), and graphs can be arbitrarily complicated. The proof proceeds inductively through graph classes ordered by treewidth, using Kruskal's WQO theorem for trees as the base case and the Grid Minor Theorem to handle graphs of large treewidth.

The <strong>algorithmic consequence</strong> is striking: once we know that every minor-closed class has a finite forbidden minor set, and once we know (from Section 6.4) that testing for any fixed minor is polynomial, we get a polynomial-time recognition algorithm for <em>every</em> minor-closed class — even classes whose forbidden minors we cannot explicitly compute. The existence of the algorithm is guaranteed by the theorem; we need not know the forbidden minors. This is a rare and beautiful example of a non-constructive existence proof having a direct algorithmic consequence.

<strong>Minor-closed classes</strong> are classes closed under taking minors (edge deletion, vertex deletion, edge contraction). Examples: forests, planar graphs, graphs of bounded genus, graphs of bounded treewidth.

<div class="definition"><strong>Definition</strong> (WQO). A partial order is <em>well-quasi-ordered</em> (WQO) if every infinite sequence contains indices \(i < j\) with \(G_i \preceq G_j\). Equivalently, there are no infinite anti-chains.</div>

The WQO property is what we want for graphs: if the minor order is a WQO, then every downward-closed set (minor-closed class) is determined by a finite set of minimal excluded elements — the forbidden minors. The formal development proceeds through several lemmas.

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 17.1.1</strong>. If a graph class \(\mathcal{G}\) is \(\alpha\)-closed, then \(\mathcal{G}\) is the same as the \(\mathcal{H}\)-\(\alpha\)-free graphs for some \(\mathcal{H}\) (take \(\mathcal{H} := \mathcal{G}\)).

</div>

<div class="corollary">

<strong>Corollary 17.1.1.1</strong>. There exists a set \(\mathcal{H}\) of graphs such that the interval graphs are exactly the induced-\(\mathcal{H}\)-free graphs.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Proposition 17.1.2</strong>. There exist graph classes \(\mathcal{G}\) that are \(\alpha\)-closed but for which any description as \(\mathcal{H}\)-\(\alpha\)-free graphs requires an infinite list \(\mathcal{H}\). (Example: chordal graphs require forbidding all \(C_k\), \(k \geq 4\).)

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 17.2.1</strong> (Anti-Chain). An anti-chain of a partial order is a set of mutually incomparable elements.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 17.2.1</strong>. Suppose for any graph class \(\mathcal{F}\), the minor poset defined by \(\mathcal{F}\) has no infinite anti-chain. Then for any minor-closed graph class \(\mathcal{G}\), there is a finite set \(\mathcal{H}\) of graphs such that \(\mathcal{G}\) is precisely the \(\mathcal{H}\)-minor-free graphs.

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 17.2.2</strong> (Sink). In a sequence of graphs \(G_1, G_2, \ldots\), a sink is a graph \(G_i\) such that no \(G_j\) with \(j > i\) satisfies \(G_i \preceq G_j\).

</div>

<div class="definition">

<strong>Definition 17.2.3</strong> (Sink Sequence). A sequence of graphs where every element is a sink.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 17.2.2</strong>. If for any graph class \(\mathcal{F}\), the partial order has no infinite sink-sequence, then for any graph class \(\mathcal{G}\), the minor poset has no infinite anti-chain.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 17.2.3</strong> (Robertson–Seymour). For any graph class \(\mathcal{G}\), the minor poset is well-quasi-ordered (WQO).

</div>

The proof builds up WQO classes inductively. Key stepping stones:

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 17.3.1</strong>. The class of stars is WQO with respect to the minor relation.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 17.3.2</strong>. Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be a WQO graph class. Any sequence \(G_1, G_2, \ldots\) contains an infinite minor chain as a subsequence.

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 17.3.3</strong>. If \(\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{G}'\) are WQO, then so is \(\mathcal{G} - \mathcal{G}'\) (graphs with two components, one from each class).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 17.3.4</strong>. If \(\mathcal{G}\) is WQO, then so is \(\mathcal{G}^*\) (graphs whose connected components all belong to \(\mathcal{G}\)).

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 17.3.5</strong> (Kruskal). The set of forests is WQO (with respect to the minor relation).

</div>

<div class="lemma">

<strong>Lemma 17.3.6</strong>. The class of partial \(k\)-trees is well-quasi-ordered.

</div>

<div class="theorem">

<strong>Theorem 17.3.7</strong>. The class of planar graphs is WQO.

</div>

<div class="theorem"><strong>Theorem</strong> (Robertson–Seymour, 2004). The class of all finite graphs, ordered by the minor relation, is WQO.</div>

<div class="proof"><strong>Proof sketch.</strong> The proof proceeds by showing that partial \(k\)-trees are WQO (using Kruskal's theorem for forests and induction on \(k\)), then that planar graphs are WQO by the Grid Minor Theorem dichotomy (either bounded treewidth — apply partial \(k\)-tree WQO — or contains a large grid — apply induction on \(G_1\)). For arbitrary graphs, an induction on genus, using a "cutting" operation that reduces genus by 1, completes the argument. \(\square\)</div>

The proof is a tour de force of structural graph theory, weaving together all the major themes of the course: treewidth, planar separators, grid minors, and genus. The twenty-paper series that established this result took Robertson and Seymour over two decades to complete.

What makes the Robertson–Seymour theorem so philosophically satisfying is that it closes the loop on the entire course. We started with planar graphs and their two forbidden minors. We developed treewidth as the central parameter. We proved that large treewidth forces a large grid minor. We established FPT algorithms via tree decompositions. And now we learn that <em>all of this structure</em> — the entire ladder of graph classes — is governed by a single organizing principle: the finiteness of forbidden minors in the minor order. Every graph class that is closed under taking minors, no matter how it was defined, has this finite characterization. It is one of the few results in mathematics where a single theorem simultaneously guarantees the existence of polynomial-time algorithms for an infinite family of recognition problems — most of them for classes we have not even named.

<strong>Corollary (Robertson–Seymour).</strong> Every minor-closed graph class can be characterized by a <em>finite</em> set of forbidden minors. Hence, for every minor-closed class, there is a polynomial-time membership test (test each forbidden minor, all of which are finitely many and of bounded size).

<strong>Examples of finite forbidden minor sets:</strong>
- Forests: \(\{C_3\}\) (forbidden minor = triangle).
- Planar graphs: \(\{K_5, K_{3,3}\}\) (Kuratowski/Wagner).
- Outer-planar graphs: \(\{K_4, K_{2,3}\}\).
- Graphs of treewidth \(\leq 2\): \(\{K_4\}\).
- Graphs of treewidth \(\leq 3\): \(\{K_5, \text{Octahedron}, \text{Pentagonal prism}, \text{Wagner graph}\}\).

## Summary and Connections

The following diagram captures the major graph class inclusions studied in this course:

```
Trees ⊆ Forests (partial 1-trees)
  ⊆ Outer-planar graphs = partial 2-trees = SP-graphs
      ⊆ partial 3-trees ⊆ ... ⊆ partial k-trees (bounded treewidth)
                                        ⊆ planar graphs
                                              ⊆ bounded-genus graphs
                                                    ⊆ minor-closed classes

Interval graphs ⊆ Chordal graphs (strong tree decomp exists)
Interval graphs = graphs with strong path decomposition
Chordal graphs = graphs with perfect elimination order
```

The central algorithmic message: <strong>treewidth is the master parameter</strong>. For any fixed \(k\):
- Graphs of treewidth \(\leq k\) admit FPT algorithms for all MSOL2-expressible problems.
- Planar graphs have treewidth \(O(\sqrt{n})\) and admit PTASes via Baker's scheme.
- The Robertson–Seymour theorem guarantees that any minor-closed class has a polynomial-time recognition algorithm.
