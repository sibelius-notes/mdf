This course begins from another scene familiar to readers of [PHIL 145C](/1219/phil145c.md): a widely shared claim that the age of using education to achieve class mobility is over. In PHIL 145C, that piece was analyzed as an argument. The focus there fell on overgeneralization, faulty analogy, ideological slippage, and the misuse of anecdote. Those were real weaknesses. Yet the article spread because it touched a genuine social wound. Large numbers of readers recognized the feeling that educational effort no longer maps clearly onto security, dignity, or advancement.

Sociology starts from that recognition but refuses to remain at the level of viral discourse. Instead it asks why the promise of education became so central to modern social order, why credentials multiplied, how institutions sort and stratify, how families invest in schooling, why labor markets attach value to degrees, and what happens when the mobility promise weakens. It treats frustration not as a private mood but as a clue to a broader arrangement of institutions, expectations, and inequalities.

The course is organized around a China-led comparative problem. Contemporary China offers a particularly clear case of educational expansion, credential competition, housing pressure, family investment, and youth anxiety. But the core questions are not uniquely Chinese. Similar tensions appear across Canada, the United States, South Korea, Japan, and the United Kingdom. Modern societies promise mobility through schooling while simultaneously reproducing hierarchy through unequal resources, prestige sorting, and labor-market filtering. This contradiction is one of the defining problems of the sociology of education and stratification.

Throughout the course, we draw on sociology of education, social stratification research, labor sociology, political economy, and selected psychological and philosophical literature. But the center of gravity remains sociological. The goal is not self-help advice for anxious graduates. It is a systematic account of how education became the privileged route of hope, how credentialism reshapes institutions, and why disappointment is often privatized even when its causes are structural.

Three broad claims organize the course. First, education is simultaneously a mechanism of opportunity and a mechanism of social reproduction. Second, credentialism does not simply reflect skill requirements; it also reflects status competition, closure, and organizational convenience. Third, the emotional weight placed on educational success is itself a sociological fact. Anxiety, shame, overinvestment, and the rhetoric of "falling behind" are not merely psychological side effects. They are built into the modern mobility regime.

The importance of this formulation is that it blocks a false choice. Public discourse often invites readers to decide whether education is a ladder or a trap. Sociology insists that it is historically both. It can open life chances for millions while simultaneously reorganizing inequality through subtler and more institutionally legitimate forms. This is why the educational question remains so politically charged. If education were only fraud, people would eventually abandon it; if it were only liberation, resentment would be harder to explain. Its power lies in its contradictory reality.

# Chapter 1: From Viral Anxiety to Sociological Inquiry

The public claim that education no longer guarantees social mobility is powerful because it compresses many experiences into one sentence. A graduate cannot find work in their field. A family has spent heavily on tutoring and housing near a good school. A student who did everything "right" still feels stalled. A degree once imagined as a ladder now feels like a crowded waiting room. In PHIL 145C, the problem with this type of claim was that it leapt too quickly from anecdote to epochal conclusion. Sociology agrees that the leap is analytically weak. But it also asks why the leap feels plausible to so many people.

That question directs us to the idea of a **mobility regime (流动体制)**: the institutional arrangement through which societies distribute hope, reward effort, and justify inequality. Modern societies do not merely educate; they moralize education. They tell citizens that schooling is the legitimate route to advancement and that outcomes are, at least in principle, deserved. The force of the mobility promise is therefore not just economic. It is ethical and emotional. When the promise weakens, disappointment is experienced not only as lost income but as existential disorientation.

Sociology studies this by linking biography to structure. A student's anxiety about grades, internships, majors, or graduate school is not simply a private psychological event. It is connected to educational expansion, labor-market filtering, housing regimes, family strategy, prestige hierarchies, and state policy. The goal of this course is to reconstruct those connections.

China offers a particularly revealing case. Educational expansion since the late twentieth century has dramatically increased access, aspiration, and competition. Elite university hierarchies remain powerful. Urban labor markets are crowded and unequal. Housing costs shape marriage and family timing. Parents invest extraordinary resources in children's educational trajectories. Meanwhile, digital publics circulate contradictory messages: work harder, choose practical majors, lower your expectations, become more adaptable, accept ordinariness, or rage against the system. These messages are not random. They are ideological responses to the instability of the mobility regime.

Comparison matters because this instability is not purely Chinese. Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, and South Korea all confront forms of credential inflation, unequal access, and labor-market uncertainty. Yet the symbolic centrality of exams, the family politics of investment, and the relation between housing and marriage give the Chinese case particular intensity. The course therefore uses China as its lead case while placing it within wider theories of education, stratification, and social closure.

The chapters that follow move from foundational concepts to comparative institutional analysis. We begin with key terms, then examine human capital theory, signaling, educational expansion, class reproduction, labor-market competition, housing, debt, emotional life, and public discourse. The aim is not to declare education useless or to repeat meritocratic optimism. It is to understand why education remains socially indispensable even when its promises become unstable.

The course also treats public commentary itself as data. Viral essays, online advice, parental strategy talk, campus discourse, and media narratives do not merely reflect educational reality; they help organize how people live it. They tell families how much to sacrifice, students what counts as failure, and graduates how to interpret stalled mobility. Thus the sociology of education must also be a sociology of educational common sense.

That educational common sense has changed historically. Earlier industrial societies often treated schooling as one institution among several routes into adulthood. In many contemporary societies, especially those organized around knowledge-economy rhetoric, schooling has become the central narrative through which future worth is imagined. This inflation in cultural meaning helps explain why criticism of education can sound like criticism of the person, the family, or the nation.

This also means that educational disappointment is often overinterpreted by those who suffer it. A poor outcome at one institutional checkpoint can be made to stand for the collapse of an entire life project. That response is not simply individual overreaction. It is the effect of living in a society that has concentrated too much moral meaning into educational transition points. Sociology does not mock that concentration; it explains how schools, states, employers, and families jointly produced it.

# Chapter 2: Key Terms (关键词) and the Sociology of Mobility

Any serious discussion of education and mobility requires conceptual discipline. Public discourse often moves rapidly between very different meanings of "value," "success," and "mobility" without distinguishing them. Sociology insists that we separate these ideas.

## Key Terms (关键词)

- **Social mobility (社会流动)**: movement of individuals or families within a stratification system across time.
- **Intergenerational mobility (代际流动)**: movement relative to one's parents' class, income, occupation, or educational position.
- **Credentialism (文凭主义)**: the increasing use of educational credentials to allocate jobs, status, or opportunities, often beyond what job tasks strictly require.
- **Credential inflation (文凭膨胀)**: the process by which more schooling is required for positions that previously demanded less.
- **Human capital (人力资本)**: the skills, knowledge, and capacities that increase productive potential.
- **Signaling (信号作用)**: the use of credentials to indicate desirable traits such as discipline, intelligence, conformity, or social fit, whether or not schooling itself caused them.
- **Social reproduction (社会再生产)**: the reproduction of class structure, cultural advantage, and institutional hierarchy across generations.
- **Meritocracy (精英主义 / 能力至上)**: the principle that rewards should track talent and effort.
- **Status closure (地位封闭)**: the exclusionary process by which groups monopolize opportunities and restrict access.
- **Underemployment (不充分就业)**: employment below one's level of training, skill, or aspiration.
- **Positional competition (位置竞争)**: competition for relative advantage when value depends partly on rank rather than absolute attainment.

The concept of **social mobility (社会流动)** is often used loosely as a synonym for personal improvement. Sociology is more precise. Mobility refers to movement within a stratification order. That movement can be absolute or relative. In a rapidly growing economy, many people may improve in absolute terms even if relative hierarchy remains rigid. Conversely, some people may improve in rank while facing stagnant living standards. These distinctions matter because public disappointment often results from their conflation. A society may expand access to higher education and some white-collar work while still feeling immobile if relative competition intensifies.

**Intergenerational mobility (代际流动)** is central because modern education systems are normatively justified in its name. If family origin strongly predicts educational outcome and occupational destination, then the moral authority of schooling as an equalizer is weakened. This is why sociologists care so much about family background, cultural capital, neighborhood effects, and institutional hierarchy.

The distinction between **human capital (人力资本)** and **signaling (信号作用)** is also fundamental. Human capital theory treats schooling as productive investment: students learn skills that raise their productivity. Signaling theory treats education partly as a sorting device: degrees tell employers something about the bearer, such as persistence, literacy, or social fit. In reality, both mechanisms often operate together. The sociological importance of the distinction lies in how it changes the meaning of expansion. If degrees mainly signal relative position, then mass expansion will intensify competition without eliminating hierarchy.

This brings us to **credential inflation (文凭膨胀)**. As more people obtain a given credential, its scarcity declines, and employers may demand higher qualifications even where job content has not changed proportionately. Randall Collins argued that this is not an accidental byproduct but a structural tendency of credential societies. Once credentials become the default language of legitimacy, competition moves upward through the credential ladder.

Sociologists also insist on the concept of **social reproduction (社会再生产)**. Families do not simply respond to the education system; they also help reproduce it. They transmit language styles, aspirations, confidence, tutoring access, cultural familiarity, and strategic information. This means competition in schooling is never just competition between isolated students. It is structured competition between unequally resourced households.

Finally, the concept of **positional competition (位置竞争)** helps explain why educational success often feels unsatisfying. If rewards depend partly on rank, then everyone can improve credentials while many remain in the same relative place. This is why educational expansion can coexist with widespread anxiety. More schooling does not abolish hierarchy; it often reorganizes it.

These key terms provide the conceptual base for the rest of the course. Without them, public debate drifts between nostalgia, self-help, and fatalism. With them, we can begin to ask why mobility promises are made, how they are institutionalized, and why they are so difficult to fulfill on equal terms.

They also help disentangle the different meanings of educational "return." A credential may deliver wage return, status return, marriage-market return, migration return, family honor, or psychological confidence. These returns do not always move together. A graduate may earn only modestly more than before yet gain symbolic security; another may gain income but feel status disappointment. Public debate often compresses these distinct dimensions into a single judgment of worth, generating confusion and resentment.

The distinction between **absolute mobility (绝对流动)** and **relative mobility (相对流动)** is particularly important in this respect. A generation may become more educated and somewhat more prosperous in absolute terms while still feeling stuck if competition for elite positions intensifies. In public discourse, relative disappointment often dominates absolute improvement because people evaluate themselves against peers and expected rank, not only against past deprivation.

For that reason, sociological analysis must be comfortable with apparent paradox. A society can produce more graduates, more consumption, more white-collar jobs, and still generate strong popular feelings of blockage. These outcomes are not mutually exclusive. They arise when the symbolic and positional meaning of success changes faster than aggregate improvement can satisfy.

# Chapter 3: Human Capital, Signaling, and Credential Society Theory

Modern societies often justify educational inequality by invoking investment. Students work hard, acquire skills, and are rewarded. This is the language of **human capital (人力资本)**. In economics and public policy, human capital theory explains schooling as a productive investment that increases individual earnings and national growth. At one level, this framework captures something real. Literacy, numeracy, technical knowledge, and professional training do matter. But sociology is interested in what the framework leaves out.

First, human capital theory tends to individualize success. It invites the question: how much did this student invest, and what return did they earn? Sociology asks a different question: which institutions define valuable skill, which families can invest, and which labor markets reward which capacities? A return to education is never generated by skill alone. It depends on employers, occupational closure, professional licensing, union density, region, and macroeconomic conditions. A degree can have different meanings in different times and places.

This is where **signaling theory (信号理论)** becomes indispensable. Michael Spence's basic insight was that education may function not just by building skill but by signaling desirable traits to employers. Degrees can indicate endurance, conformity to institutional expectations, symbolic polish, and familiarity with elite norms. Sociology expands this insight by showing that signals are socially coded. Employers do not read credentials neutrally. They interpret them through prestige hierarchies, accent, demeanor, social networks, internship histories, and cultural fit.

Randall Collins pushed this argument further in *The Credential Society*. He suggested that modern societies increasingly allocate position through formal educational credentials not because schooling perfectly matches skill to job, but because credentials provide a socially legitimate mechanism for exclusion. This is close to Max Weber's idea of **status closure (地位封闭)**. Groups seek to monopolize opportunities by controlling the credentials needed to enter. Professional schools, licensing systems, elite universities, and recruitment rituals all participate in closure.

This perspective clarifies why educational expansion can fail to deliver broad mobility. When one level of credential becomes common, gatekeeping may simply move upward. A bachelor's degree becomes baseline; then a master's becomes desirable; then elite branding, internships, overseas experience, or coded forms of polish become the new differentiators. Hierarchy survives by changing form.

Yet sociology should not reduce all education to cynical closure. Schooling does produce knowledge, and some occupations require intensive specialized training. The key point is that education is both substance and signal. To analyze it adequately, we must keep both in view. Public discourse often swings between naive human capital optimism and total disillusionment. The former imagines pure return on effort; the latter imagines pure fraud. Sociology rejects both.

The human capital / signaling distinction also helps interpret the emotional structure of contemporary anxiety. If families believe schooling is productive investment, then poor outcomes feel like tragic but contingent setbacks. If they suspect schooling is increasingly a positional contest, then outcomes feel rigged or hollow. This is one reason public narratives about "学历贬值" are so powerful. They give language to the suspicion that education is no longer delivering what it morally promised.

The sociological question is therefore not whether education has value in the abstract. It is what kind of value it has, for whom, under which institutional conditions, and at what social cost.

Organizational sociology further strengthens this analysis. Employers, universities, and states all face coordination problems. Credentials provide a cheap common language for sorting large populations. This makes them institutionally attractive even when they imperfectly measure skill. Once many organizations depend on credentials, the system becomes path dependent. Families adapt by investing more heavily, students adapt by accumulating certificates, and institutions adapt by refining prestige distinctions. Credentialism becomes self-reinforcing.

That self-reinforcement helps explain why societies often expand schooling even when they publicly doubt its returns. The degree remains necessary not because everyone fully believes in it, but because no household wants to be the one that opts out while others continue competing. Educational systems are therefore sustained by both conviction and defensive conformity.

This defensive conformity gives credentialism a collective-action character. If every family knows the race is exhausting but fears unilateral withdrawal, the result resembles an arms race in which no one can easily disarm. Public complaints about "involution" make little sense unless read in this light. The problem is not irrational households alone; it is the absence of trusted collective alternatives.

That absence of alternatives is sociologically crucial. Credential systems persist not because all actors fully believe in them, but because too many institutional domains are coordinated through them at once. A household considering a less conventional path must weigh not only labor-market uncertainty, but also status risk, family judgment, marriage-market implications, and the possibility that employers will simply refuse to read nonstandard signals. Under these conditions, conformity becomes understandable even when privately resented.

# Chapter 4: Mass Higher Education, Expansion, and Credential Inflation

Martin Trow's classic distinction between elite, mass, and universal higher education remains one of the best starting points for understanding modern educational expansion. As systems move from educating small elites to educating large proportions of the population, they do not simply become larger versions of themselves. Their social meaning changes. Higher education becomes less a mark of exceptional distinction and more a normal expectation of citizenship, employability, and adulthood.

This shift has profound consequences. In an elite system, admission itself provides scarcity value. In a mass system, stratification moves inside the system. Institutional prestige, field of study, postgraduate credentials, internships, and informal networks become more important. Expansion therefore democratizes access while also multiplying internal hierarchy. This is one reason public discussion of mass education is so contradictory: it is both genuinely more open and newly stratified.

China's higher education expansion since the late 1990s provides a major contemporary case. Enrollment growth dramatically increased access and transformed family expectations. But expansion did not flatten prestige. On the contrary, elite hierarchies such as 985, 211, and later Double First Class retained and reorganized status distinctions. A mass system therefore emerged with highly visible internal tiers. This is sociologically important because disappointment in mass systems is often not disappointment with education per se but with one's location inside the new hierarchy.

Credential inflation follows naturally in such systems. As more graduates enter labor markets, employers can raise educational requirements. Positions once open to high-school or college diploma holders begin requiring bachelor's degrees; jobs once open to bachelor's holders begin favoring master's degrees or elite institutional pedigree. The key issue is not simply oversupply. It is the interaction of expansion with employer behavior, occupational closure, and symbolic expectations about what educated workers should look like.

This process is often experienced as unfair because it converts collective expansion into individual burden. Families are told that education is the route to security, yet as participation rises each household must invest more merely to avoid falling behind. The burden is intensified by tutoring industries, test preparation, and extracurricular signaling. Educational expansion thus creates both democratization and arms race dynamics.

Sociologists therefore describe mass education as a contradictory institution. It can increase literacy, widen participation, and create real opportunities. At the same time, it can intensify competition, postpone secure adulthood, and privatize the costs of stratification. Public narratives often treat these two sides as mutually exclusive. Sociology insists they coexist.

The broader comparative picture supports this. South Korea, Japan, the United States, and Canada all show versions of expansion accompanied by differentiation, status competition, and concern about graduate outcomes. The Chinese case is distinctive in scale and in the relation between exams, family strategy, and urban development, but it is not anomalous.

Mass higher education therefore does not abolish hierarchy. It reorganizes it through new credentials, new rankings, and new thresholds of normality. This is the institutional context in which mobility promises are made increasingly difficult to satisfy.

Expansion also changes the meaning of failure. In elite systems, exclusion can be framed as ordinary because access is narrow by design. In mass systems, exclusion from the upper tiers of opportunity feels more personally charged because higher education participation itself has become normalized. When many possess degrees but only some secure stable trajectories, disappointment becomes internal to the educated population. This is one reason graduate grievance has become such a visible public force.

Universities themselves respond strategically to expansion. They differentiate programs, build brands, internationalize, market employability, and seek ranking visibility. Students are encouraged to think of themselves as investors, yet institutions also cultivate ideals of intellectual growth and citizenship. These mixed messages are sociologically important. They produce ambivalent students who are asked to be both scholars and entrepreneurs, citizens and competitors, self-developing persons and labor-market projects.

Massification also changes the internal social life of universities. As institutions grow and diversify, peer comparison becomes more intense, and informal hierarchies proliferate among majors, internships, exchange opportunities, and extracurricular credentials. Students do not simply compete for grades; they compete for narrative advantage. Who seems employable, cosmopolitan, practical, serious, or exceptional becomes part of everyday campus culture. Sociology therefore treats campus life as a status order, not merely a learning environment.

This point matters because many students first encounter the practical meaning of stratification inside the university itself. They discover that "being in higher education" is not a single social condition. The distinction between departments, campuses, research opportunities, and professional pipelines can be as decisive as the difference between attending university and not attending at all. Mass systems therefore relocate hierarchy inward, making subtle forms of comparison central to student life.

# Chapter 5: Meritocracy, Reproduction, and the Hidden Structure of Unequal Opportunity

Few modern ideals are as powerful as **meritocracy (能力至上 / 精英主义)**. The meritocratic promise says that individuals should advance according to talent and effort rather than birth. Schools are central to this promise because they appear to evaluate performance through standardized and ostensibly neutral procedures. Yet sociologists have repeatedly shown that educational competition is structured by unequal starting points.

Bourdieu and Passeron's theory of reproduction remains foundational here. They argued that schools misrecognize socially inherited advantages as individual merit. Families transmit not only money but language styles, tastes, dispositions, confidence, and familiarity with institutional expectations. These forms of **cultural capital (文化资本)** align differently with school demands. Students from privileged backgrounds are therefore more likely to appear naturally capable even when what they possess is socially inherited fit with the institution.

Annette Lareau's work extends this point by showing how parenting styles differ by class. Middle-class families are more likely to practice what she calls concerted cultivation: structured activities, adult negotiation, and active institutional intervention. Working-class and poor families are more likely to rely on the accomplishment of natural growth, where children have more unstructured time but less institutional coaching. The important sociological lesson is not moral superiority of one style. It is that institutions reward some family practices more than others.

This is why equal formal access does not produce equal effective opportunity. Students do not enter school as abstract individuals. They enter as carriers of unequal family resources, neighborhood conditions, health burdens, informational networks, and future expectations. The ideology of meritocracy hides these conditions by translating outcome into deservedness.

Educational institutions also differ in their capacity to compensate for inequality. Some schools intensify advantage by offering enriched curricula, counseling, extracurricular pathways, and professionalized application support. Others simply manage scarcity. Sociologists therefore treat school systems as stratified organizations, not as neutral pipelines. The question is never only whether students work hard, but what opportunities their institutions can make real.

Meritocracy is also sustained by ritual. Exams, ranking lists, graduation ceremonies, honor rolls, and elite admissions narratives create visible moments in which inequality is transformed into celebrated achievement. Ritual matters because it naturalizes outcome. Students and families experience rank not merely as data but as socially validated judgment. This makes inequality emotionally sticky.

The hidden curriculum is part of this ritual order. Schools teach students how to wait, how to present themselves, how to speak to authority, how to narrate ambition, and how to accept evaluation. These lessons matter in labor markets precisely because organizations often reward comportment as much as competence. Thus the hidden curriculum links meritocratic ideology to workplace hierarchy.

The hidden curriculum also helps explain why apparently fair competition can remain socially selective. Students who already possess the styles of speech, self-management, and confidence valued by institutions appear naturally meritorious, while others appear less polished or less prepared even when they are equally capable. The school thereby translates classed and cultured differences into educationally meaningful distinctions. This is one of the central mechanisms by which reproduction hides inside merit.

Michael Sandel's critique of meritocracy adds a normative dimension. Meritocratic cultures not only reward some and disappoint others; they moralize these outcomes. Success becomes proof of deservingness. Failure becomes evidence of inadequacy. This moralization deepens humiliation among those who do not advance. It also shields institutions from scrutiny, because structural outcomes can be reframed as personal shortfall.

Chinese educational discourse illustrates this dynamic vividly. High-stakes examinations, elite university hierarchies, and family sacrifice intensify the moral symbolism of schooling. Educational success is not just a path to income; it is proof of family virtue, self-discipline, and future legitimacy. Under such conditions, unequal opportunity is especially painful, because it appears to betray a sacred script of effort and reward.

Yet the sociological critique of meritocracy should not be mistaken for the claim that effort never matters. Effort matters profoundly. The problem is that effort is socially conditioned and unequally convertible into reward. A sociology of meritocracy therefore asks not whether individuals strive, but under what conditions striving is likely to pay, who can afford failure, and how institutions translate effort into recognition.

# Chapter 6: Class, Region, Family Resources, and Educational Trajectories

Educational competition is always embedded in family strategy. Households invest money, attention, time, emotional pressure, and sometimes geographic mobility into children's schooling. This makes education a major site where class is converted into future advantage. It also means that educational trajectories cannot be understood without class, region, and family structure.

Class matters in obvious ways through income and wealth, but also in less visible ways: stability of housing, access to quiet study space, ability to pay for tutoring, familiarity with application systems, willingness to delay children's earnings, and tolerance for risk. A family with savings can support unpaid internships, exam retakes, or graduate study. A family without such cushions may require early labor-market entry even for talented students. Thus educational choice is never purely educational; it is shaped by household risk capacity.

Region matters because institutions are geographically distributed. In China, disparities between large metropolitan centers and smaller cities or rural areas shape school quality, access to elite universities, labor-market return, and migration strategy. Regional inequality interacts with hukou status, local fiscal capacity, and urban concentration of opportunity. Educational competition is therefore also territorial competition.

Family structure and intergenerational expectation matter as well. In many societies, but especially where family support remains central to housing and marriage formation, young adults' educational trajectories are deeply entangled with parental aspirations. Parents may invest heavily not only because they believe in learning but because they view children's credentials as family insurance against future precarity. This gives education emotional weight far beyond the classroom.

Sociology also attends to the unequal costs of aspiration. For some families, aiming high is risky but feasible. For others, failure at the top of the hierarchy can be devastating because so much has been sacrificed. Educational ambition is therefore not evenly distributed in meaning. The same exam score can represent ordinary disappointment for one household and catastrophic loss for another.

This helps explain why discourse about education is often morally intense. Families are not merely calculating returns. They are making identity investments. Being a "good parent" or a "serious student" becomes tied to visible commitment to schooling. Public criticism of credential competition may therefore be heard as criticism of family sacrifice itself.

At the same time, sociology insists that not all educational routes are valued equally. Vocational pathways, non-elite colleges, local institutions, and less prestigious majors may provide stable lives, yet public discourse often codes them as signs of diminished worth. That coding is itself a social fact. It reflects status hierarchies, not only labor-market data.

Educational trajectories thus emerge from the interaction of household resources, regional opportunity, institutional hierarchy, and cultural expectation. To speak of mobility without these conditions is to describe only the ideological surface of the problem.

The family strategy dimension is especially visible in contexts where one child's educational success is expected to compensate for wider household vulnerability. This can place extraordinary burdens on students, who become bearers of collective aspiration. In such cases, educational disappointment is not individual disappointment multiplied many times; it is a crisis in the family's moral economy. Sociology helps us see why reactions may therefore appear disproportionate to outsiders.

Migration and region complicate this further. A university admission or job offer may imply relocation, detachment from care networks, and a recalibration of identity. The mobile educated subject celebrated by modern discourse is not universally desired or equally feasible. Some households need proximity more than prestige. Such tradeoffs rarely appear in meritocratic ideology, but they shape actual educational decision-making.

Rural-urban inequalities make these tradeoffs especially visible. A student may gain educational mobility while simultaneously entering a city in which they remain socially peripheral, economically pressured, and dependent on family transfer. The category of "success" can therefore conceal unstable incorporation. Sociology studies not only movement upward but the quality and durability of the position reached.

This is why it is not enough to ask whether someone left a village, entered a university, or obtained a white-collar title. We must also ask whether their new position is secure, whether it can be reproduced across time, and whether it transforms the family's long-term vulnerability. Mobility that is unstable, debt-dependent, or geographically fragile may feel less like arrival than like suspended risk.

# Chapter 7: Degrees, Labour Markets, Underemployment, and Status Competition

Education is often judged by labor-market outcome, but that relationship is neither simple nor uniform. Degrees affect access to occupations, wages, unemployment risk, and symbolic status. Yet their effects vary by field, institution, region, and macroeconomic moment. Sociology therefore studies not only whether education "pays" but how labor markets classify, sort, and downgrade credentials.

One key phenomenon is **underemployment (不充分就业)**. A graduate may find work, but not work that matches their training, aspirations, or expected status. Underemployment matters because it reveals that mobility frustration is not exhausted by unemployment rates. A society can place many graduates into jobs while still generating widespread disappointment if the jobs do not align with what credentials were socially promised to mean.

Labor sociology further shows that employers use credentials as filters under uncertainty. Degrees can reduce search cost. Prestige brands can signal discipline or fit. This means that hiring is not purely about task requirements. It is also about risk management, cultural matching, and symbolic legitimacy. Lauren Rivera's work on elite hiring is revealing here: employers often recruit not only skill but polished style, institutional pedigree, and social resemblance.

Status competition intensifies because work is not just income. Occupation is also moral and symbolic placement. Graduates compare not only salaries but the dignity attached to roles. Two jobs may offer similar pay yet vastly different status. This is one reason discourse about "wasting a degree" persists even when some graduates remain economically stable.

In China's urban labor markets, these issues intersect with the prestige hierarchy of universities and majors, the expansion of white-collar aspirations, and periodic labor-market contraction. Public and private sectors carry different status meanings. Government jobs, state-owned enterprises, tech firms, finance, education, and platform work all occupy distinct symbolic positions. A student's sense of success depends not only on employment but on where within these symbolic maps they land.

Comparatively, similar tensions appear elsewhere. In Canada and the United States, graduates may work outside their major or rotate through precarious employment despite having higher average lifetime earnings than non-graduates. This coexistence of aggregate premium and individual instability is sociologically significant. Macro truth does not erase biographical disappointment.

The lesson is that labor markets do not simply reward human capital. They translate credentials through employer strategies, institutional prestige, economic cycles, and status cultures. This is why the question "is college worth it?" is sociologically underdeveloped when posed in the singular. Worth for whom, at what institutional tier, in which field, at what cost, with which alternatives, and according to which concept of success?

This chapter also requires a distinction between **occupational closure (职业封闭)** and **occupational drift (职业漂移)**. Some professions maintain relatively stable barriers to entry and thus preserve stronger educational payoffs. Others are more open, crowded, or reorganized by technological and organizational change. A degree's value is therefore partly occupationally specific. Public discourse that generalizes from one sector, such as tech, finance, or education, often mistakes a sectoral cycle for a universal truth.

Informal networks matter too. Granovetter's classic work on the strength of weak ties reminds us that jobs are often found through relational channels rather than open competition alone. This means that labor-market outcomes depend not only on formal qualification but on network position. Families with broader social capital may convert the same degree into better outcomes. This is one more way that formal merit and social reproduction remain intertwined.

Employers also vary in how they value educational experience. Some use strict degree thresholds because they lack time or tools to evaluate candidates deeply. Others rely on internships and references that are themselves unequally accessible. Still others prioritize prestige because prestige economizes judgment. From the employer side, credentialism is often organizationally rational. From the social side, it may still be profoundly unequal.

This organizational perspective helps explain why criticism of employers for using credentials, while often justified, does not reach the whole problem. Employers are operating inside environments of applicant overload, low trust, and uncertain information. Prestige and credential filters reduce complexity for them. The sociological issue is that the costs of this simplification are displaced onto households and students, who must increasingly carry the burden of proving worth in ever more refined institutional languages.

# Chapter 8: Housing, Debt, Marriage Markets, and the Material Conditions of Mobility

Educational mobility cannot be understood in isolation from housing, debt, and family formation. In many contemporary societies, especially urban East Asia, adulthood is organized through a bundle of transitions: finishing school, finding stable work, acquiring housing or housing access, marrying or partnering, and beginning family life. When these transitions become expensive and uncertain, the meaning of education changes.

Housing is central because it converts labor-market success into lived stability. In settings where housing prices are high relative to income, educational and occupational achievement may no longer suffice to secure the social markers of adulthood. This creates a distinctive form of frustration: one may be objectively better educated than previous generations yet less able to purchase the institutional goods associated with adulthood.

Debt intensifies this condition. Educational debt, family debt, and mortgage pressure can transform education from promise into burden. Debt also shapes risk tolerance. Graduates burdened by repayment or by expectations of family support may be less able to experiment, retrain, or accept lower-paid developmental roles. Thus the mobility regime becomes more rigid precisely when the labor market becomes less predictable.

Marriage markets add another layer. In societies where educational attainment, income prospects, and housing ownership are deeply tied to marriageability, schooling acquires intimate significance. Educational competition is no longer just about work; it is about becoming a viable spouse. This is especially salient in Chinese discourse, where property, urban residence, and educational prestige frequently enter conversations about dating and marriage. Gendered expectations shape this process asymmetrically, but the broader point is that mobility is embodied in family formation.

Sociology calls attention to the cumulative nature of these pressures. Educational success that once might have secured relative stability now confronts inflated housing, postponed marriage, and highly selective labor markets. The result is not simply disappointment but a mismatch between institutional timelines. Schooling promises adulthood, but adulthood becomes materially deferred.

This mismatch is one reason educational discourse becomes moralized. Families and public commentators search for explanations: young people are too picky, too idealistic, too fragile, too impractical, too entitled. Such narratives privatize what are often structural timing problems. The question is not merely whether individuals choose wisely, but whether the material conditions of transition make formerly normal aspirations difficult to realize.

Thus housing and debt are not side issues. They are constitutive of the mobility problem. A sociology of credentialism that ignores them remains incomplete.

Housing also converts educational inequality into spatial inequality. Those who can remain in high-opportunity cities preserve access to stronger labor markets and denser networks; those priced out may accept inferior matches or return to localities with fewer options. Educational mobility is therefore mediated by urban geography. A degree may open doors in principle yet fail to deliver transition if the city in which those doors exist is materially inaccessible.

Intergenerational transfer further complicates the picture. Family assistance with down payments, rent, or job-search support can stabilize early adulthood for some graduates while others confront adulthood on fully market terms. Public narratives often treat these differences as private family matters, but sociologically they are mechanisms of class reproduction operating after graduation.

This means that graduation does not mark a clean shift from meritocratic competition to labor-market reward. Family resources remain active after formal schooling ends. They shape how long a graduate can search, where they can live, whether they can accept training positions, and how much risk they can tolerate. In practice, the school-to-work transition is often a school-family-market transition, and the family side of that triangle is too often hidden by public meritocratic language.

The marriage-market dimension should also be treated carefully. It is easy to caricature discussions of "marriageability" as superficial or reactionary. But sociologically, marriage markets reflect the intersection of family strategy, housing, gender norms, and status competition. Educational attainment enters these markets not only as income proxy but as moral signal. This makes educational disappointment resonate beyond work, affecting self-worth and intimate prospects simultaneously.

# Chapter 9: Youth Anxiety, Learned Helplessness, Identity Capital, and the Emotional Life of Credentialism

Educational systems do not only sort people materially. They shape emotion. Anxiety, shame, aspiration, resentment, and exhaustion are not accidental byproducts of credential competition. They are built into systems that tie self-worth to evaluation and future security to institutional ranking.

One useful concept here is **identity capital (身份资本)**, associated with James Cote. Identity capital refers to the resources individuals use to navigate uncertain institutions: confidence, self-efficacy, social skills, credentials, and narrative coherence. In unstable mobility regimes, identity capital becomes more important and more unequal. Those with strong family support and institutional familiarity can reinterpret setbacks as detours. Others may experience the same setback as proof of total failure.

Psychological concepts such as **learned helplessness (习得性无助)** also help explain why mobility disappointment can become generalized despair. When individuals repeatedly encounter a mismatch between effort and reward, they may begin to doubt not one institution but the value of striving itself. Sociology integrates this with structure: helplessness is not only a mental state. It is often a rational affective adaptation to repeated institutional opacity.

Public discourse around "lower your expectations," "accept ordinariness," or "stop chasing prestige" should therefore be read sociologically. Such discourse can function as care, realism, ideology, or all three. It may genuinely help some individuals avoid self-destruction. But it may also convert structural blockage into personal attitude management. This is what makes it politically ambivalent.

Youth anxiety is thus not merely an overreaction to competition. It is tied to the moral and temporal structure of modern education. Students are encouraged to see adolescence and early adulthood as decisive periods during which the right choices determine life course. This concentrates pressure. Failure appears irreversible even when sociologically it is often not.

At the same time, digital media intensify comparison. Students now encounter endless visibility of others' successes, internships, study routines, applications, and lifestyle markers. Competition becomes not only institutional but ambient. The self is measured continuously against peers and aspirational strangers.

Sociology helps us see that emotional life is not external to credentialism. Credential systems produce subjects who must narrate themselves as competitive, resilient, employable, and strategically adaptive. Anxiety is therefore part of the social organization of meritocratic life.

Another useful concept here is **anticipatory socialization (预期性社会化)**. Students do not wait until graduation to become labor-market subjects. They begin shaping themselves around expected future evaluation long before entry into work: choosing majors strategically, cultivating "practical" skills, policing hobbies for usefulness, and converting leisure into portfolio items. This anticipatory orientation expands the reach of labor-market logic into educational and personal life.

There is also a class dimension to emotional resilience discourse. Calls to be flexible, entrepreneurial, or willing to "start from the bottom" sound different depending on family safety net. What is framed as admirable grit for some may be untenable risk for others. Psychological advice detached from material context thus often universalizes middle-class fallback capacity.

The emotional life of credentialism also includes resentment toward those perceived to have chosen better, earlier, or more strategically. Credential systems personalize structural scarcity. Instead of experiencing blocked mobility as a problem of institutional design, individuals are encouraged to compare themselves to more successful peers. This comparison culture deepens shame and weakens collective interpretation.

# Chapter 10: China's Education-Mobility Regime in Comparative Perspective

China's education-mobility regime combines several features that make it especially intense: high-stakes examination culture, rapid higher-education expansion, visible prestige hierarchy, strong family investment, regionally uneven opportunity, urban housing pressure, and a digital public sphere saturated with evaluative discourse. The result is a system in which education remains enormously consequential even as its payoffs become more uncertain and internally differentiated.

The **gaokao (高考)** remains central to the moral imagination of merit. It is often represented as a relatively fair competition in a society marked by many inequalities. This gives it enormous legitimacy. Yet the exam operates within unequal school systems, tutoring access, regional quotas, and family resources. Sociology therefore treats the gaokao not as pure merit nor pure illusion, but as a selective institution embedded in broader inequalities.

Elite university branding further structures mobility. Admission to top institutions is not simply educational placement; it is social certification. This certification then interacts with labor markets, civil service competition, overseas study, and marriage markets. A mass system thus preserves elite distinction through a refined hierarchy of institutions.

Comparing China with Canada or the United States is instructive. In North America, admissions pathways may be more holistic and fragmented, but family resources, neighborhood inequality, and institutional prestige remain decisive. In South Korea and Japan, exam pressure and educational arms races are more directly comparable. Across all cases, educational expansion coexists with persistent stratification. What differs is the symbolic form through which competition is narrated and justified.

The Chinese case also highlights the relation between education and state development strategy. Expansion has been linked to modernization, competitiveness, and national strength. This strengthens the ideological centrality of education. Yet when graduate labor markets soften or youth unemployment rises, the developmental narrative collides with lived uncertainty. Public frustration is then managed through a mix of adaptation advice, entrepreneurial rhetoric, patriotic developmentalism, and blame shifting.

Comparative sociology therefore treats China neither as uniquely broken nor as simply converging with a generic global model. It is a specific articulation of mass education, state ambition, market stratification, and family strategy.

The comparative perspective also reveals different balances between public and private burden. In some countries, tuition and debt are central; in others, exam pressure, tutoring, and family transfer dominate. In some, vocational education retains strong legitimacy; in others, it is stigmatized. These differences matter because they alter how mobility disappointment is interpreted. A student in one country may blame debt, another may blame exam hierarchy, another may blame labor-market oligopoly. Sociology reads these narratives as clues to institutional design.

China's case further highlights the role of state discourse in managing mobility expectations. Official narratives of national development, innovation, and educational modernization can coexist with calls for youth resilience and patience. This coexistence is not accidental. It is one way institutions absorb tension between collective progress and uneven individual outcomes.

At the same time, cross-national comparison shows that no single institutional lever solves the problem. Countries with low tuition may still have fierce prestige competition. Countries with strong vocational systems may still reproduce class through educational tracking. Countries with broad university access may still intensify labor-market crowding. Comparative sociology therefore shifts the question from "which country got it right?" to "which combinations of institutions reduce cruelty, overinvestment, and false promise?"

# Chapter 11: Public Discourse, Self-Help Adaptation, and the Depoliticization of Structural Problems

When mobility regimes become unstable, public discourse often turns therapeutic. Commentators advise students and graduates to become more realistic, more flexible, less attached to prestige, more skill-oriented, more entrepreneurial, or more emotionally resilient. Some of this advice is practical. But sociology asks what social function it serves.

One possibility is that adaptation discourse provides genuine coping resources. Individuals do need ways to interpret disappointment without collapse. Yet another possibility is **depoliticization (去政治化)**: structural problems are redefined as matters of personal mindset. In this frame, the issue is not labor-market saturation, housing inflation, prestige hierarchy, or unequal school quality. The issue is that individuals expected too much or failed to update their strategy.

C. Wright Mills's distinction between personal troubles and public issues is crucial here. A student's failed job search may feel personal, but when a large generation confronts similar problems, the phenomenon is public and structural. Self-help discourse can obscure this by returning explanation to the level of private psychology.

This does not mean all adaptation discourse is cynical. The sociological point is more subtle: advice can be both useful and ideological. It can help individuals survive while also narrowing collective imagination. Telling graduates to become "better screws in the machine" may reduce immediate distress yet leave untouched the structure generating the distress.

Public discourse about education therefore deserves analysis in its own right. Viral pessimism, elite optimism, entrepreneurial realism, nostalgic stories of upward mobility, and anti-involution commentary all help societies process the contradiction between educational expansion and stratified reward. These narratives do not merely reflect reality; they shape how people interpret and endure it.

Studying these narratives sociologically brings us back to PHIL 145C. Argument analysis showed where reasoning overreached. Sociology shows why overreaching narratives remain socially powerful. They give emotional form to structural contradiction.

This is why critique alone is insufficient. Exposing exaggeration in a viral essay may improve analytical clarity, but it does not reduce the demand for such essays. The demand persists because people require narratives that convert diffuse structural strain into intelligible story. One major task of critical sociology is to offer explanations that are more accurate without being emotionally empty.

Public discourse also plays a sorting role. Some narratives encourage continued competition; others encourage exit into alternative life scripts; others legitimate hierarchy by praising sacrifice and endurance. Students and families consume these narratives as strategic resources. The sociology of educational discourse therefore asks not only what stories say, but what actions they make more thinkable.

This is why self-help language deserves serious analysis rather than dismissal. To tell young people to lower expectations, cultivate resilience, or become more practical is not merely to comfort them. It is also to govern aspiration. It redistributes what kinds of hope appear respectable and what kinds of frustration appear immature or excessive. In that sense, discourse becomes part of the mobility regime itself.

# Chapter 12: Rethinking Mobility, Justice, and the Future of Education

The sociology of education does not end by asking whether education is good or bad. That binary is too crude. Education remains one of the most important institutions in modern life. It can widen opportunity, enrich thought, support democratic participation, and generate skilled labor. Yet it can also reproduce hierarchy, intensify competition, and moralize inequality. The question is therefore not whether to have educational mobility, but what kind of mobility regime is justifiable.

One route is to rethink the excessive burden placed on schooling. Modern societies ask education to do too much: sort talent, legitimate inequality, deliver national competitiveness, produce employability, cultivate citizenship, and provide personal meaning. No single institution can bear all these demands without contradiction. Part of the future of educational justice may require reducing the centrality of credentials in labor-market allocation and strengthening non-educational supports for dignity and stability.

Another route is to confront hierarchy inside education itself. Prestige concentration, unequal school quality, and costly signaling make expansion feel hollow. More equal funding, broader access to high-quality institutions, stronger vocational and technical pathways with genuine dignity, and reduced dependence on narrow prestige markers would alter the meaning of competition.

Labor-market reform matters too. If graduates encounter precarious work, weak training, and inflated housing costs, educational reform alone cannot restore mobility confidence. Education policy must therefore be linked to labor policy, housing policy, welfare, and regional development. Sociology's advantage is that it keeps these connections visible.

Finally, we need a different moral vocabulary. The language of pure merit obscures inherited inequality and converts structural failure into private shame. But fatalism is no answer either. A better public vocabulary would acknowledge effort without mythologizing it, recognize structure without erasing agency, and defend education as a public good without promising that every degree guarantees elite status.

This course has argued that mobility disappointment is not merely psychological disappointment. It is a social diagnosis. The task of sociology is to read that diagnosis clearly: to see how institutions produce hope, why they fail unevenly, and how societies narrate the gap between promise and reality. Only then can we move beyond the false choice between naive meritocracy and total despair.

The future of education, from this perspective, depends on institutional honesty. If societies continue to demand mass participation in higher education while quietly preserving steep prestige hierarchies and precarious labor-market transitions, public cynicism will deepen. If, however, educational systems are linked to broader reforms in work, housing, regional development, and social protection, the mobility promise may become less deceptive and less cruel. Sociology cannot guarantee that outcome. But it can clarify what must change for education to be more than a disciplined competition for shrinking symbolic advantage.

That clarification is itself politically important. Modern societies often oscillate between praising education as salvation and condemning it as scam. Both positions are too simple. Education is a socially necessary but unevenly rewarding institution whose meaning depends on how it is linked to labor markets, welfare, region, and family strategy. The challenge is not to abandon the idea of educational mobility, but to redesign the surrounding social order so that mobility is less mythic, less punitive, and less dependent on the private capacities of already advantaged households.

The larger implication is that educational justice cannot be achieved by schooling policy alone. If labor markets continue to sort through prestige scarcity, if housing continues to convert family wealth into life chances, and if families continue to bear disproportionate responsibility for navigating uncertainty, then schools will keep absorbing aspirations they cannot fairly fulfill. Sociology's role is to keep these linked institutions in view. Only then can we understand why educational hope remains so powerful, so costly, and so unevenly rewarded.
