This course begins from a scene that readers of [PHIL 145C](/1219/phil145c.md:1746) will recognize immediately: a seemingly small online dispute about sex, morality, and gendered double standards grows into a much larger struggle over identity, social norms, public reason, and institutional legitimacy. In PHIL 145C, that case was treated as a problem in **argument analysis**. The emphasis there fell on presuppositions, equivocations, analogies, and the structure of competing claims. That work remains useful, but it leaves another question open: why do these exchanges appear so predictably, travel so quickly, and generate such intense emotional investment?

Sociology approaches that question differently. Rather than asking only whether a speaker's reasoning is valid, it asks how platforms, institutions, gender hierarchies, legal regimes, cultural scripts, and economic pressures organize the field in which people speak. It studies not just the argument but the **social conditions of argument**. It examines why certain labels become available, why certain grievances become legible, why certain emotional tones are rewarded by algorithms, and why some positions are experienced as moral common sense while others are experienced as deviant, threatening, or unserious.

The course is therefore organized around a China-led comparative problem: how should we understand the rise of highly antagonistic online gender discourse in contemporary Chinese digital publics, and how does that phenomenon compare with parallel developments elsewhere? To answer that question, we draw from sociology, gender studies, social psychology, media studies, legal scholarship, sexuality research, and political economy. But the organizing voice remains sociological. Our concern is with patterned relations, institutional structures, collective meanings, and the reproduction or transformation of inequality.

The course is also deliberately research-driven. Public controversies may serve as entry points, but they are not treated as self-evident truth. The task is not to endorse one internet faction against another. The task is to reconstruct the larger social machinery that makes conflict appear, feel, and persist as it does. Throughout, key concepts are introduced bilingually and defined carefully, because clear concepts are themselves part of sociological discipline.

Three working assumptions guide the text. First, online gender conflict is not reducible to bad argument or bad personality. It is tied to institutions of family, labor, media, and law. Second, the conflict is not purely symbolic. Material insecurities around housing, marriage, care work, and physical safety are continuously translated into discourse. Third, no serious analysis can proceed by treating "men" or "women" as internally uniform blocs. The strongest versions of sociological explanation are those that preserve differentiation while still identifying patterned inequality.

These assumptions are necessary because digital discourse constantly invites actors to forget them. A post that travels successfully usually does so by narrowing explanation, not widening it. It identifies one salient injury, one obvious villain, one emotionally satisfying frame. Sociology is less efficient in that sense. It slows the pace of certainty. But that slowing is precisely what makes explanation possible.

# Chapter 1: From PHIL 145C Case to Sociological Problem

The public argument that anchors this course begins with a deceptively simple question: why do some people explain their refusal of a sexual practice in pragmatic terms such as disease risk, money, embarrassment, or social danger, while others expect moral language to come first? In a philosophy classroom, that question invites analysis of **presupposition (预设)**, **analogy (类比)**, and **loaded questions (复合问题 / 预设谬误)**. In sociology, however, the same exchange immediately raises a broader set of issues. Why do participants assume that private sexual speech reveals stable moral character? Why does one party hear a pragmatic response as evidence of ethical deficiency? Why do comments sections convert personal anecdotes into judgments about whole genders? Why do digital platforms reward precisely the most totalizing and inflammatory versions of these claims?

To answer those questions, sociology begins by refusing the idea that such exchanges are merely spontaneous. Public speech is patterned. It draws on preexisting vocabularies, familiar stereotypes, institutional scripts, and media affordances. When a user says "men always..." or "women always...," they are not inventing a new description from scratch. They are entering a field of already circulating labels, emotional expectations, and recognizable genre conventions. Digital publics provide ready-made frames for interpreting conflict. Some frames emphasize victimization, others hypocrisy, others structural oppression, others personal responsibility. Participants inherit these frames before they type a single sentence.

This is what we mean by a **public discourse (公共话语)** approach. A public discourse is not just any collection of statements. It is a socially organized field of meaning in which certain ways of speaking become normal, legible, and amplifiable. Discourses do not merely describe reality. They sort and weight it. They tell users which details count as evidence, which feelings count as seriousness, which identities count as injured parties, and which counterarguments count as proof of bad faith. This is why online gender conflict is so often repetitive. People are not simply disagreeing as isolated individuals; they are inhabiting already stabilized interpretive positions.

This approach has an important methodological payoff. It lets us study repetition without treating participants as dupes. People may be sincerely angry, wounded, or fearful, yet still draw on publicly available scripts that pre-structure what their anger can mean. Social patterning does not cancel authentic feeling. It helps explain why authentic feeling so often takes familiar form.

Sociology also asks why these discourse positions are emotionally compelling. Here the concept of **affective investment (情感投入)** becomes crucial. Arguments about sex, consent, loyalty, family obligation, and dignity do not feel like abstract debates because they are connected to lived experiences of recognition and misrecognition. For many users, online gender discourse functions as a place where diffuse frustrations become narratable. Relationship disappointment, employment pressure, marriage-market anxiety, urban precarity, and resentment toward unequal expectations can all be translated into the language of gender grievance. That translation is one reason antagonistic discourse persists even when it appears analytically weak. Its sociological force does not depend on logical rigor alone.

Another important move is to treat the platform as an actor in the problem. A platform is not a neutral container. Recommendation systems, engagement metrics, repost mechanics, comment ranking, and moderation practices all shape which voices become visible. If outrage, humiliation, and simplified blame reliably generate interaction, then the platform environment itself becomes a selective pressure favoring those forms. This does not mean algorithms determine beliefs in a mechanical way. It means they reward some rhetorical forms more than others, thereby influencing what kinds of speech appear common, legitimate, or worth imitating.

Seen sociologically, this means platforms are not just environments in which gender politics happens. They are institutions that reorganize gender politics by altering how quickly claims travel, which tones appear credible, and what counts as public proof. A grievance with metrics behind it feels more real than a grievance without them, even when those metrics reflect circulation rather than representative social distribution.

The case that inspired this course also reveals a central sociological distinction between **micro interaction (微观互动)** and **social structure (社会结构)**. At the micro level, one can analyze the conversational situation: who is speaking to whom, what identities are being presented, and what speech acts are being performed. At the structural level, one asks why the available scripts are what they are. Why is moral purity feminized in some contexts and realism masculinized in others? Why do discussions of sex work become proxies for larger struggles over respectability, danger, autonomy, and the meaning of exploitation? Why do legal categories, media stereotypes, and family norms enter these conversations so quickly?

This distinction is essential because online debate often oscillates between two bad explanations. One explanation psychologizes everything: the conflict exists because some users are irrational, bitter, manipulative, or hateful. The other explanation totalizes everything: the conflict is a direct and transparent expression of one single structure, whether patriarchy, capitalism, feminism, or state control. Sociology resists both simplifications. It assumes that individuals act within patterned environments and that structures are reproduced through interaction. Good analysis therefore moves across levels. It connects speech, institutions, and inequality without collapsing one into the other.

The course takes China as its lead case not because China is uniquely irrational or uniquely conflictual, but because Chinese digital publics provide a particularly rich setting in which rapid platformization, family transformation, educational expansion, urban housing pressure, shifting marriage norms, and state-managed public discourse intersect. Similar conflicts appear elsewhere, but the Chinese case lets us observe the entanglement of modernity, gender, and digital mediation with unusual intensity. Comparative material from North America, Europe, Japan, and South Korea will be used throughout to show both recurring dynamics and historically specific differences.

The rest of the course proceeds by widening the problem. We move from one recognizable controversy to the larger gender order, from there to platform infrastructures, emotional labor, moral psychology, sexual regulation, legal models, and finally to methods and institutional design. The aim is not to suppress disagreement. It is to understand how disagreement becomes an industry, an identity, and a recurring mode of social life.

One additional distinction will be useful throughout: the distinction between **issue conflict (议题冲突)** and **identity conflict (身份冲突)**. In issue conflict, participants disagree over specific claims, policies, or cases. In identity conflict, the disagreement itself becomes proof of who a person is. Once this shift occurs, a discussion about dating norms or sex-work law becomes a test of moral belonging. Sociology is particularly interested in that transition, because it marks the point at which ordinary disagreement becomes durable antagonism.

# Chapter 2: Key Terms (关键词) and Core Theories of Gender, Power, and Social Reproduction

Before we can analyze online gender conflict, we need a disciplined vocabulary. Sociology depends on concepts that are more precise than ordinary polemical speech. Public controversy often relies on what one might call conceptual inflation: the same label is stretched to cover many different phenomena until it loses diagnostic value. A first task of this course is therefore to stabilize some of the most important terms.

## Key Terms (关键词)

- **Gender order (性别秩序)**: the patterned organization of social life through institutionalized expectations, divisions, and hierarchies connected to masculinity, femininity, and other gendered positions.
- **Patriarchy (父权制)**: a historically variable system in which men as a group are structurally advantaged in authority, status, resources, and control over institutions, though not all men benefit equally.
- **Hegemonic masculinity (霸权男性气质)**: the culturally dominant form of masculinity that claims legitimacy over other masculinities and over femininity.
- **Intersectionality (交叉性)**: the principle that gender is lived and structured together with class, race, region, sexuality, disability, age, and other axes of power.
- **Social reproduction (社会再生产)**: the labor and institutions that reproduce everyday life and the labor force across generations, including care, household work, and the reproduction of norms.
- **Emotional labour (情感劳动)**: the management of feeling and its display according to social rules.
- **Impression management (印象管理)**: the strategic presentation of self in interaction.
- **Framing (框架化)**: the interpretive process through which events are made meaningful according to a recognizable storyline or moral lens.
- **Moral regulation (道德规制)**: the shaping of conduct through norms, institutions, law, media, and public judgment.
- **Platform governance (平台治理)**: the set of rules, design choices, moderation practices, and algorithmic systems through which platforms structure communication.

The term **gender order** comes most prominently from R.W. Connell. It is useful because it avoids two opposite mistakes. One mistake is to imagine gender as a purely personal identity. The other is to imagine gender as a single unchanging hierarchy operating identically in every context. The language of gender order captures patterned inequality while preserving historical variation. The gender order of a rural village, a global technology firm, a marriage market, a university campus, and a livestream platform may overlap, but they are not identical. Each has its own incentives, sanctions, and symbolic struggles.

Connell's related concept of **hegemonic masculinity (霸权男性气质)** is equally important. It does not mean that most men actually embody an ideal type of dominant masculinity. Rather, it names the culturally authoritative model against which other masculinities are judged. That model might emphasize toughness, sexual competence, economic provision, emotional control, or rational mastery, depending on context. The point is relational: hegemonic masculinity orders other masculinities and helps secure the broader gender hierarchy. Online discourse matters here because platforms are spaces where masculine status can be publicly claimed, challenged, and dramatized.

At the same time, feminist sociology insists that gender cannot be treated as a single axis detached from all others. **Intersectionality (交叉性)**, first articulated most influentially by Kimberle Crenshaw, was developed to show how legal and political categories often fail to capture the combined effects of race and gender. Sociology has extended this insight. The women discussed in digital discourse are not socially interchangeable; nor are the men. Region, educational attainment, hukou location, family wealth, occupational insecurity, sexual identity, and age profoundly shape how gender is lived. A discourse that speaks only of "men" and "women" as totalized blocs is therefore usually a sign of analytical collapse.

Another foundational concept is **social reproduction (社会再生产)**. In Marxist and feminist sociology, social reproduction names the labor and institutions required to sustain life and labor power across time. This includes raising children, caring for the elderly, managing households, organizing affective support, and reproducing norms and identities. Online gender conflict is saturated with anxieties about social reproduction: who owes care, who bears emotional burden, who pays housing costs, who sacrifices for marriage, whose body is at risk, whose labor is invisible. Public arguments about dating, family, and sexuality frequently serve as displaced struggles over the distribution of reproductive labor.

Sociology also draws heavily on Erving Goffman's interactionism. Goffman helps us see that public conflict is not merely the release of authentic inner truth. It is a performance governed by situational expectations. People manage impressions, save face, claim moral seriousness, and avoid humiliation. Online discourse intensifies these pressures because interaction is semi-public, archived, and ranked. The user is never speaking only to one interlocutor; they are speaking to an imagined audience, potential allies, and potential punishers. This makes **impression management (印象管理)** a central concept for understanding why participants often choose rhetorically effective identity signals over analytically careful speech.

Finally, we need the concept of **moral regulation (道德规制)**. This term directs attention away from the simplistic question "what do individuals privately believe?" and toward the social machinery through which conduct is judged, normalized, and sanctioned. Schools, families, courts, media, influencers, police, and platform moderation systems all participate in moral regulation. Debates over sex work, dating norms, or pornography are never only private arguments. They are also arguments about which institutions have the right to define legitimate conduct.

These concepts form the base of the course. They do not settle empirical questions automatically. But they allow us to ask better questions. Instead of "which gender is worse?," sociology asks what institutional arrangements make that question appear meaningful in the first place.

They also help us distinguish levels of explanation. For example, a viral post may be analyzed interactionally through impression management, structurally through gender order, politically through moral regulation, and technologically through platform governance. None of these levels invalidates the others. The strongest research designs in this field combine them.

# Chapter 3: Gender Order, Patriarchy, Hegemonic Masculinity, and Intersectionality

The language of patriarchy is often used casually online, sometimes as a total explanation for any objectionable behavior by men, sometimes as a straw target dismissed as ideological exaggeration. Sociology uses the term more carefully. **Patriarchy (父权制)** refers not to the wickedness of individual men, but to historically patterned arrangements in which men as a group are advantaged in authority, status, institutional access, or control over valued resources. Importantly, this does not imply uniform male power. Men are stratified among themselves by class, age, region, sexuality, race, and institutional position. A migrant worker, an unemployed graduate, and a billionaire founder do not occupy the same practical relation to power, even if they may all benefit in some ways from male privilege.

This is why Connell's theory is so useful. Hegemonic masculinity does not claim that all men dominate equally. It shows how one culturally authoritative form of masculinity legitimizes a hierarchy both over women and over subordinated masculinities. In many modern contexts, hegemonic masculinity fuses economic success, sexual desirability, emotional discipline, and public competence. Men who cannot perform those traits may feel both structurally constrained and symbolically humiliated. This matters because online gender grievance often emerges where lived precarity collides with aspirational masculine norms. A user may be economically insecure yet still judge himself according to standards of provision, romantic success, and self-mastery that remain culturally powerful.

Feminist sociology complements this analysis by showing that women's relation to gender order is similarly stratified. Women do not experience patriarchy identically. Middle-class urban women may have educational and occupational opportunities unavailable to poorer rural women, while still confronting harsh marriage-market expectations or beauty standards. Highly educated women may gain autonomy in some institutions while facing new pressures around delayed marriage, fertility timing, and the management of professional respectability. This is why intersectional analysis is indispensable: it blocks the fantasy that "women's experience" can be represented by one dominant subgroup.

Intersectionality also clarifies why online discourse so often misfires. Conflict intensifies when structurally real grievances are generalized through the narrow experience of one slice of the population. For example, urban heterosexual dating discourse may be treated as if it captures gender relations as such, even though it excludes queer experience, rural arrangements, informal labor economies, and non-elite family structures. Platform discourse therefore tends to universalize the experience most legible to the platform's dominant users. One sociological task is to ask whose experiences become general theory and whose disappear.

The family remains central to this story. Sociologists of family have long shown that households are not just private emotional units but sites where gendered labor and authority are organized. The "second shift" described by Hochschild is a classic example: even where women entered paid work in large numbers, unpaid domestic labor remained unequally distributed. In many East Asian contexts, including China, rapid economic modernization has not dissolved the family as a site of obligation. Instead it has reorganized intergenerational pressure. Housing purchases, marriage timing, care obligations, and childrearing expectations all remain deeply familial. Online gender conflict often encodes these pressures in personalized form: selfish men, materialistic women, irresponsible fathers, manipulative mothers-in-law. But the sociological question is how institutions make these roles plausible and consequential.

This familial layer explains why so many apparently separate online controversies converge. Arguments about bride price, divorce law, reproductive rights, childcare, career sacrifice, and dating norms all become debates about who should bear the labor of social reproduction. Online discourse personalizes these issues, but the pressures themselves are institutional and intergenerational.

We should also resist a common mistake in digital debate: the assumption that naming patriarchy ends the analysis. Patriarchy is not a magic answer. It is an invitation to specify mechanisms. Does a given outcome arise through law, workplace discrimination, state policy, school culture, algorithmic amplification, family norms, sexual violence, or market incentives? The answer may be several at once. A strong sociological explanation names the levels of mediation. A weak one replaces mechanism with moral certainty.

At the same time, the backlash against feminist explanations often relies on another reduction: the claim that gender conflict is now simply the result of female privilege or anti-male culture. Sociology again demands specification. In what domain? By what measure? Compared with whom? Are we discussing legal rights, educational outcomes, cultural sympathy, sentencing patterns, occupational hazards, or intimate expectations? Different indicators may point in different directions. The fact that some men feel aggrieved does not automatically prove structural reversal. It may instead indicate that previously stable gender bargains are breaking down under conditions of economic and cultural change.

This helps explain why gender conflict is both structurally grounded and symbolically intensified. Material changes such as educational expansion, labor-market competition, urban housing inflation, and delayed marriage alter the practical terms on which gendered lives are negotiated. But these changes are lived through cultural narratives. People do not encounter "the labor market" in the abstract; they encounter stories about what a real man should provide, what a respectable woman should demand, what intimacy should feel like, and what counts as exploitation. Online platforms then compress these narratives into portable symbolic forms.

The conclusion is not that structure determines speech in a simple way. Rather, speech becomes one of the places where structure is interpreted, contested, and emotionally processed. Gender order therefore cannot be understood without both institutions and discourse. The next chapter examines how platforms actively mediate this relationship.

This is also why apparently contradictory perceptions can coexist. Some women may experience expanding educational and professional opportunity while also confronting intensified misogyny online. Some men may experience real labor-market precarity while also benefiting from masculine privilege in other domains. Online conflict becomes explosive when partial truths are weaponized as total explanations. Sociology's task is to retain those partial truths while rejecting the totalization.

# Chapter 4: Online Platforms, Algorithms, Attention, and the Production of Conflict

To understand contemporary gender antagonism, we need a sociology of platforms. A platform is not merely a technical service that hosts user content. It is a social institution that organizes visibility, incentives, temporality, and interaction. The major digital platforms of the present era are attention markets. Their economic logic rewards content that produces engagement, and engagement is disproportionately generated by material that activates fear, anger, humiliation, envy, moral outrage, and identity threat. This is one reason gender conflict travels so well.

The first key idea here is **algorithmic amplification (算法放大)**. Contrary to popular myth, algorithms do not create hatred ex nihilo. They sort, prioritize, and distribute content according to criteria linked to engagement, relevance, or retention. Yet because antagonistic content often performs strongly on those criteria, amplification creates a skewed informational environment in which conflict appears ubiquitous and representative. Users come to believe that "everyone is saying this" or "this is what the other side really thinks" because the platform disproportionately surfaces the most inflammatory material. Sociology treats this not as a cognitive accident alone but as a product of institutional design.

The second idea is **platform governance (平台治理)**. Moderation decisions, account penalties, keyword filtering, monetization rules, comment ordering, and search visibility all shape public discourse. Importantly, governance is uneven. Platforms may remove explicit slurs while leaving more coded forms of harassment untouched. They may punish nudity more consistently than organized misogyny. They may suppress some politically sensitive topics while tacitly profiting from interpersonal conflict that drives time on site. Governance is therefore not simply censorship or freedom. It is a patterned management of communicative risk and value.

Research on digital media has repeatedly shown that online publics are not flat. They are structured by recommender systems, influencer hierarchies, repost chains, and cross-platform feedback loops. A phrase originating in one subcommunity can be picked up by lifestyle bloggers, reformulated as relationship advice, attacked by opponents, and finally mainstreamed through press coverage. Gender labels often follow exactly this trajectory. What begins as in-group shorthand becomes mass public vocabulary. By the time it is widely visible, its meaning has often shifted several times. This is why sociologists track discourse ecologies rather than isolated statements.

Chinese platforms add specific institutional conditions. Platformization in China has unfolded under a distinctive combination of commercialization, intense mobile integration, influencer economies, and state oversight. Short-video platforms, lifestyle apps, forums, and microblog systems each have their own rhetorical cultures. Some favor confessional storytelling, some hot-take confrontation, some pseudo-professional advice, and some emotional diary forms. The same gender topic will therefore appear differently depending on platform format. A one-minute video encouraging resentment toward "high-maintenance women" operates differently from a long知乎 answer about婚恋市场, even if the ideological content overlaps.

Another important concept is **affective public (情感公众)**. This term describes publics constituted not just by shared information but by circulating feelings. Users gather around moods as much as propositions: resentment, mockery, fear, vindication, exhaustion. Gender conflict communities are often held together by affective synchrony rather than stable doctrine. People may disagree about law or policy but still share the feeling that they have been dismissed, exploited, or made ridiculous by the other side. Platform design intensifies this by making reaction immediate and countable. Likes, reposts, and comments transform feeling into visible social evidence.

Platform conflict also encourages what Whitney Phillips and Ryan Milner describe as forms of networked pollution. Not every participant intends to radicalize discourse. Many simply respond, mock, quote-tweet, or defend themselves. Yet the cumulative effect can still be degradation of the communicative environment. This is especially relevant for gender conflict. Even users who oppose misogyny or misandry may inadvertently amplify those materials by treating them as content to be publicly ridiculed. The platform does not reliably distinguish denunciation from endorsement at the level of circulation. Outrage remains circulation fuel.

The sociology of platforms therefore complicates moralistic explanations. It is not enough to say that users should be kinder, more rational, or more responsible, although those may be desirable norms. If the environment systematically rewards conflict and compresses nuance, then appeals to individual virtue alone will not solve the problem. Institutional design matters. Friction in reposting, stronger anti-harassment moderation, limits on monetized outrage, better contextual signals, and support for slower forms of discussion may alter the ecology. But such measures also raise questions about governance power, political control, and the legitimacy of moderation.

Thus platform analysis does not replace gender theory; it mediates it. Gender antagonism today is not simply patriarchy plus screens. It is a reorganization of conflict through infrastructures of ranking, circulation, and monetization. To understand why some claims are made, we also need to understand why those claims pay.

The temporal structure of platforms also matters. Digital environments shorten response cycles and reward immediacy. Users are encouraged to react before context is established and to treat speed as authenticity. Slow interpretation can therefore appear evasive or elitist. This temporal pressure is sociologically significant because gender discourse often concerns morally loaded topics where immediate judgment feels righteous. Platform time thus interacts with moral time.

Finally, platform conflict is deeply shaped by metrics. Follower counts, view counts, repost numbers, and comment volume transform speech into visible competition. A grievance that would once have remained local can now be publicly certified as important through metrics alone. Metrics do not prove truth, but they confer social weight. In this way, platforms do not merely host discourse; they quantify it and thereby help decide what counts as socially real.

This quantification has epistemic consequences. Users may come to treat popularity as evidence, as though a widely circulated grievance had automatically achieved sociological representativeness. But virality is a record of platform success, not of population distribution. A central discipline of this course is learning not to confuse the two.

# Chapter 5: Emotional Labour, Impression Management, and Gendered Self-Presentation

When people debate sex, dating, and moral character online, they are rarely just exchanging propositions. They are performing selves. The classic sociological vocabulary for this comes from Erving Goffman and Arlie Hochschild. Goffman teaches us to see interaction as staged self-presentation. Hochschild teaches us to see emotional expression as labor governed by rules. Together they provide a powerful framework for understanding why gender discourse is so often experienced as exhausting, strategic, and morally charged.

Consider the deceptively simple issue that animated the original PHIL 145C case: which reason one gives first. At face value, this looks like a question of sincerity or moral hierarchy. But from a sociological perspective, the ordering of reasons is also a performance calibrated to audience expectation. A man speaking to peers, a man speaking to a woman friend, a woman speaking on a public platform, and an influencer speaking to followers all occupy different interactional situations. Each situation carries expectations about seriousness, empathy, irony, realism, and moral cleanliness. What counts as the "right" answer is therefore partly situational.

This is where **emotional labour (情感劳动)** becomes important. Hochschild originally used the term to describe labor performed in service work, where employees must produce approved feelings or displays. But the concept has wider sociological use. In intimate and public life, individuals are often expected to manage emotion according to gendered rules. Women are frequently expected to demonstrate care, tact, emotional literacy, and relational accountability. Men are often expected to display control, practical realism, low dependency, and a reluctance toward overt moral vulnerability. These expectations vary by class, age, and setting, but they remain widespread enough to shape interaction.

If that is correct, then apparently simple linguistic choices may be less revealing of deep morality than of situational norm competence. Saying "I would not do that because it is risky, expensive, and socially dangerous" may function as a recognizable masculine realism performance. Saying "I would not do that because it is wrong and exploitative" may function as a moral-accountability performance. Neither utterance transparently reveals the total ethical structure of the speaker. Each is also a move within a gendered interaction order.

Goffman's concept of **face-work (面子工作)** adds another layer. Participants try to avoid humiliation and protect a coherent public self. Online gender discourse is saturated with face threats. To be labeled naive, predatory, manipulative, bitter, prudish, cheap, or hypocritical is to suffer symbolic injury. As a result, many users adopt defensive rhetorical positions before substantive argument even begins. They preemptively frame themselves as rational, morally serious, trauma-aware, or structurally informed. These are not random mannerisms. They are strategies of self-legitimation in a hostile public.

Because these threats accumulate historically, users often speak to prior injuries rather than only to the person in front of them. They enter the discussion already anticipating ridicule, dismissal, or moral contamination. This is one reason online conflict can feel strangely displaced: the immediate topic becomes a stage on which older humiliations are replayed.

Sociology also helps us understand why people often misread one another in these exchanges. If one party interprets language primarily as a clue to internal morality and the other uses language primarily as situated self-presentation, mutual frustration is likely. The first party hears evasion; the second hears impossible moralization. Public conflict escalates because participants assign different social meanings to the same act of reason-giving.

This is not to say that all performances are superficial or false. Goffman never meant that the self is merely fake theater. Rather, the self is socially organized through performances that are necessary to interaction. The key point is that performances are structurally shaped. Gender, class, and platform norms influence what a credible self looks like. For example, a female influencer may be rewarded for emotional transparency yet punished for excessive anger, while a male commentator may be rewarded for ironic detachment yet punished for visible neediness. These asymmetries help explain why certain styles cluster with certain identities.

Public discussions of dating and sexuality also reveal the burdens of **relational labour (关系劳动)**. Many women report being expected to explain exploitation, risk, and emotional harm patiently to skeptical audiences. Many men report feeling expected to publicly perform moral refinement in ways that conflict with peer norms of realism or defensiveness. These complaints are not symmetrical in structure or consequence, but both illustrate that discourse itself becomes labor. People must manage the presentation of injury, moral worth, and good faith.

Understanding this helps us avoid a common analytical trap. We should not treat online self-presentation as trivial simply because it is performative. The performance is itself a social fact. It tells us what kinds of selves are intelligible and rewarded. It reveals where emotional responsibility is distributed and where it is disowned. And it shows that gender discourse is not only about beliefs. It is about the socially organized labor of appearing as the right kind of person.

This framework also clarifies why accusations of hypocrisy are so central in gender conflict. Hypocrisy matters because public actors are judged not only on what they believe but on whether their performed self matches the moral role they claim. A commentator who speaks in therapeutic language but appears cruel, or in realist language but appears evasive, becomes vulnerable to reputational collapse. The resulting vigilance pushes users toward increasingly rigid self-branding.

In this sense, online gender discourse resembles other domains of symbolic labor under late capitalism. People do not simply have opinions; they curate moral personae. Gender conflict is especially intense because the persona itself is under evaluation. The audience asks not only "is this claim true?" but "what kind of man says this?" or "what kind of woman says this?" This is one reason dialogue becomes fragile even before substantive disagreement is addressed.

# Chapter 6: Sexual Scripts, Respectability, and the Moral Regulation of Intimacy

Sexual conduct is never governed by law alone. It is also governed by **sexual scripts (性脚本)**, norms of respectability, and culturally specific narratives about danger, desire, purity, and duty. Sociology treats these scripts not as private opinions but as organized patterns of expectation. They tell people what situations mean, what roles are available, what risks are thinkable, and what kinds of justification count as acceptable.

Sexual script theory, associated especially with Gagnon and Simon, argues that sexuality is socially organized at multiple levels. There are cultural scenarios, interpersonal scripts, and intrapsychic meanings. This helps explain why debates about sex work, casual sex, pornography, or "what decent people do" can become so intense. Participants are not only debating isolated acts. They are drawing on culturally sedimented scenarios about what sex means. Is it intimacy, commerce, domination, leisure, danger, exploitation, freedom, or all of these under different conditions? The answer depends on script.

One of the most powerful scripts in many societies is **respectability (体面 / 可敬性)**. Respectability politics refers to the demand that legitimacy be earned through disciplined conformity to dominant norms. In gendered contexts, women are often asked to display sexual caution, emotional control, and reputational self-management in order to secure recognition. Men, by contrast, may be granted wider latitude in some forms of sexual experimentation while being judged harshly for weakness, dependency, or failure of provision. Neither arrangement is neutral. Both are mechanisms of moral regulation.

This becomes especially visible in discourse around sex work. Public debate frequently oscillates between two simplifications. One simplification insists that sex work is inherently degrading and therefore obviously condemnable. The other insists that any adult sexual commerce chosen under formal consent is straightforwardly liberating. Sociology is wary of both. It asks about labor conditions, coercion, migration, policing, housing vulnerability, gendered labor markets, client power, stigma, and the differential effects of criminalization. The relevant question is not merely whether one can imagine a consenting transaction. It is what social arrangements make choices possible, risky, coerced, or survivable.

At the level of discourse, however, sex work often functions less as a labor question than as a symbolic battlefield. People use it to stage broader struggles over what counts as autonomy, male desire, female vulnerability, state intervention, and the line between intimacy and commerce. Because those larger questions are already moralized, discussions quickly slide from empirical assessment to identity accusation. Those who emphasize harm may be accused of paternalism or prudery; those who emphasize autonomy may be accused of indifference to exploitation. Sociology slows this slide by restoring institutional detail.

That restoration matters because symbolic struggle often erases the worker. The sex worker appears as an emblem, a warning, a victim, or a fantasy rather than as a laboring subject embedded in migration, policing, urban survival, and stigma. Returning institutional detail to the discussion does not dissolve moral conflict, but it prevents the topic from being used only as shorthand for larger factional battles.

Legal regimes matter here, but they do not settle moral meaning automatically. Criminalization, decriminalization, legalization, and Nordic-style models each reorganize risk differently. Comparative research shows that formal law interacts with policing style, welfare provision, migration controls, neighborhood governance, and public stigma. A policy model cannot be evaluated from label alone. This is why the course treats law as part of moral regulation rather than as its transparent expression. The law often codifies, displaces, or selectively intensifies moral judgments already circulating in society.

Family and marriage systems further shape how sexuality is moralized. In settings where marriage remains linked to housing acquisition, filial duty, and status mobility, sexual discourse carries more than personal meaning. It becomes tied to intergenerational investment and social respectability. Anxiety about "good women," "responsible men," or declining trust between genders may thus reflect not only sexual ethics but also the fragility of the institutions through which adulthood is organized.

The concept of **moral panic (道德恐慌)** is useful but must be applied carefully. Some public reactions to gender and sexuality are indeed panic-like: disproportionate, repetitive, and sustained by media amplification. Yet not every intense concern is a panic. Some reflect real injuries, such as violence, coercion, workplace harassment, or exploitative labor conditions. Sociology's task is not to dismiss concern but to distinguish between empirically grounded fear and symbolically inflated threat narratives.

Ultimately, this chapter argues that intimate life is public in two senses. First, because institutions and norms structure it. Second, because digital media transform private dilemmas into public scenes of judgment. Sexual scripts are therefore not a peripheral topic. They are central to understanding why gender discourse feels at once deeply personal and unmistakably social.

The chapter also suggests why ordinary people often experience sexual ethics as unstable. Different institutions send different signals. Law may define one thing, peer culture another, feminist critique another, and family expectation another. Individuals must navigate these overlapping script systems without any guarantee of coherence. The resulting uncertainty generates both experimentation and backlash. Public discourse is where that uncertainty is fought over symbolically.

This instability is one reason certainty is so attractive online. In a world of mixed signals, factional discourse offers clarity: who is innocent, who is guilty, which risks matter, which desires are shameful, which grievances are legitimate. Sociology does not provide certainty in that register. It provides something harder and more useful: an account of why certainty is craved and how institutions help manufacture its objects.

# Chapter 7: Moral Psychology, Identity-Protective Cognition, and Polarization

Although this is a sociology course, it cannot explain online gender conflict without engaging moral psychology. Public antagonism is not sustained by incentives alone. It is also sustained by how people process threat, belonging, and moral meaning. The crucial point is that psychological mechanisms operate socially. They are shaped by group membership, media environments, and institutional trust.

Jonathan Haidt's **social intuitionist model (社会直觉主义模型)** is a useful starting point. Haidt argues that moral judgment is often intuitive first and justificatory second. People typically feel that something is wrong before they can explain why. They then recruit reasons after the fact. In online gender conflict, this helps explain why apparently weak arguments often remain persuasive to their own side. The function of many reasons is not to discover truth but to express alignment, defend dignity, and preserve group coherence.

This does not mean reasoning is irrelevant. It means reasoning is socially situated. A user may choose reasons that fit the interpretive style of their in-group. One community values structural language; another values practical realism; another values therapeutic vocabulary; another values irony. The reasons presented are therefore both cognitive and performative. They are selected under conditions of anticipated audience judgment.

Another key concept is **identity-protective cognition (身份保护性认知)**, associated with Dan Kahan and related research. People do not evaluate evidence in a vacuum. They interpret facts in ways that protect valued group identities. When a controversy becomes identity-linked, more information does not necessarily reduce disagreement. It may intensify it, because participants become more skilled at defending their side. This is especially relevant for gender discourse, where positions are often tied to self-worth, trauma interpretation, or collective grievance.

Social identity theory adds that group boundaries organize perception. Once people sort themselves into antagonistic camps, they become prone to in-group favoritism and out-group simplification. The out-group is encountered less as a diverse set of actual persons than as a symbolic enemy represented by its most extreme screenshots and most humiliating anecdotes. Platforms accelerate this by making out-group exemplars highly portable. A single outrageous post is circulated as representative of the whole camp. This is one mechanism of polarization.

Polarization is not only ideological; it is affective. **Affective polarization (情感极化)** refers to growing emotional hostility between groups. People may be less divided on every concrete policy than they are in their disgust or mistrust toward one another. In gender conflict, affective polarization can outpace substantive difference. Participants come to despise the other side's style, motives, or tone even before substantive claims are processed. Once this happens, calls for nuance may themselves be heard as betrayal.

Moral outrage is a particularly powerful engine in digital environments. Studies in communication and psychology show that outrage travels well because it signals norm enforcement, invites alliance, and offers emotional reward. But outrage also has cognitive side effects. It compresses uncertainty, increases confidence, and narrows the space for ambivalence. In contexts of repeated exposure, users may come to feel that extreme cases are typical cases. This is one way platform logics and moral psychology reinforce one another.

Sociology's contribution is to re-socialize these mechanisms. Users are not merely biased brains on screens. Their reasoning is shaped by social trust, precarity, institutional legitimacy, and the availability of cross-group contact. In highly stratified and distrustful environments, identity protection becomes more rational from the actor's point of view. If one believes that institutions are hostile, the in-group becomes epistemically and morally safer. The resulting closure is not simply ignorance. It is often a strategy of survival in a fragmented public sphere.

This insight has practical consequences. Efforts to reduce polarization cannot rely only on debunking or individual fact correction. They must address group identity, institutional trust, and the architectures of communication. If discourse is rewarding users for moral display while punishing hesitation, then better argument alone will not be enough.

Research on deliberation supports this caution. Groups placed in adversarial environments often become more certain, not less, because social reward attaches to confident norm defense. In digital gender conflict, a tentative or mixed position may be punished by both sides as weakness or betrayal. This reduces the visible supply of nuance, which then reinforces the perception that only extreme positions exist. The public sphere becomes more polarized partly because moderating speech is selectively silenced.

# Chapter 8: Labels, Framing, Meme Logic, and Escalation in Chinese Internet Gender Conflict

Chinese digital gender discourse offers an especially rich case of how labels and frames organize antagonism. Online conflict rarely begins as an open field of infinite interpretation. It begins with available names. Labels such as "普信男," "女拳," "舔狗," "妈宝男," "雌竞," "厌女," and many others function as compressed sociologies. They carry causal stories, moral judgments, and emotional instructions in miniature. A label tells users what kind of person they are confronting and how seriously they should be taken.

From a sociological perspective, labels are powerful because they reduce interpretive cost. In fast-moving digital environments, users do not want to reconstruct every controversy from first principles. Labels allow rapid sorting. Yet this efficiency comes at a cost: diagnostic precision falls. A label that initially names a specific pattern of behavior often expands into a generalized insult. Once this happens, it ceases to clarify and begins to harden camps.

Framing processes magnify this dynamic. A frame selects some dimensions of a situation while excluding others. For example, a conflict about unequal dating expectations may be framed as structural patriarchy, female opportunism, class anxiety, urban precarity, platform sensationalism, or moral decline. Each frame highlights some truths and suppresses others. Online escalation often occurs not because one frame is wholly false and another wholly true, but because users treat their favored frame as exhaustive.

Chinese platform culture intensifies framing because of the tight relation between speech genres and monetization. Influencers, advice accounts, anonymous posters, and forum commentators each learn what types of framing reliably generate interaction. Highly portable frames win. "This proves men are..." or "This is what modern women have become..." outperform painstaking situational analysis. The incentive is toward moral compression.

Meme logic adds another layer. Memes are not just jokes; they are forms of distributed classification. They convert complex grievances into recognizable repeatable units. The advantage of the meme is speed. The disadvantage is that it encourages what we might call **discursive typification (话语类型化)**: people come to encounter one another not as concrete persons but as meme characters. Once a person is rendered as a type, curiosity declines and punitive interpretation becomes easier.

This is also where platform algorithms and everyday sexism or grievance politics meet. The meme or label that performs well does not need to be analytically rigorous. It needs to be legible, emotionally charged, and adaptable across cases. Users learn these rules quickly. They begin to narrate personal life in platform-native forms: screenshot, hot take, ironic confession, listicle of red flags, humiliating anecdote, call-out thread. The platform thus standardizes the narrative form through which gender grievance is made public.

The Chinese case also shows how commercialized discourse and state-regulated discourse can coexist. Not every form of polarizing content is equally governable or equally profitable. Some topics may be suppressed for overt political reasons, while interpersonal or gender antagonism remains comparatively visible so long as it stays within acceptable boundaries. This asymmetry can redirect public affect toward safer objects of contention. That does not mean gender conflict is manufactured from above. It means it develops within a broader ecology of managed visibility.

Another recurring pattern is **asymmetric evidence politics (不对称证据政治)**. Participants often rely on anecdote as proof, but they do not treat all anecdotes equally. Stories from the in-group become structural evidence; stories from the out-group become exceptions, propaganda, or bad-faith manipulation. This asymmetry is not unique to China, but Chinese platform cultures provide abundant examples because personal storytelling is central to traffic generation. The result is a hybrid of confessional culture and factional certainty.

For sociology, the goal is not to declare all labels useless. Some labels do identify recurring structural phenomena. The problem is not naming. The problem is when naming substitutes for analysis. A label can begin as diagnosis and end as identity weapon. Understanding that transformation is central to any serious sociology of online gender conflict.

# Chapter 9: Sex Work, Criminalization, Harm Reduction, and Comparative Policy Models

Few issues condense the tensions of gender discourse as sharply as sex work. Debates over prostitution, cam work, pornography, and transactional intimacy often become sites where broader conflicts over exploitation, autonomy, male desire, state power, and feminist politics are fought. Sociology's contribution is to move beyond slogan and ask how different institutional arrangements distribute harm, risk, and agency.

First, the term itself matters. **Sex work (性工作)** emerged partly to shift attention from moral stigma to labor conditions. Critics argue that the term can sanitize coercion or obscure structural inequality. Supporters argue that it allows clearer analysis of working conditions, legal rights, and state violence. Sociology does not settle the normative dispute by fiat. But it recognizes that language choices organize the problem. Calling something vice, immorality, trafficking, survival strategy, informal labor, or commercial sex each activates different institutional responses.

Comparative policy scholarship typically distinguishes at least four broad models: criminalization, legalization, decriminalization, and Nordic-style demand criminalization. Each produces different incentive structures. Full criminalization may increase police leverage and push labor underground. Legalization can reduce some forms of precarity while creating two-tier systems in which compliant workers are protected and noncompliant workers remain exposed. Decriminalization may reduce direct criminal penalty but requires welfare, labor protection, migration justice, and anti-violence policy to be effective in practice. Nordic models seek to reduce demand while defining sellers as protected parties, but empirical assessments remain contested.

Gender discourse often collapses these differences. Public debate proceeds as if one must choose between "moral permissiveness" and "moral seriousness." Comparative research shows otherwise. A sociological approach evaluates institutions by their effects on violence, stigma, bargaining power, health, policing, and the lived options available to workers. This is why harm reduction perspectives remain important. Even where one normatively opposes commodified sex, one may still oppose legal arrangements that intensify worker vulnerability.

At the same time, feminist disagreement on sex work is genuine and cannot be reduced to misunderstanding. Radical and abolitionist perspectives emphasize patriarchal domination, male entitlement, and the market's power to normalize inequality. Sex-positive and labor-oriented perspectives emphasize agency, stigma reduction, and the dangers of conflating all commercial sex with coercion. Materialist feminists often insist that both exploitation and constrained agency can coexist. Sociology benefits from this disagreement because it prevents moral closure.

The Chinese context introduces further complexity. Official prohibition coexists with highly varied local enforcement, digital mediation, migration, entertainment economies, and changing forms of transactional intimacy. Public discourse about prostitution in China is also shaped by family morality, public-order ideology, and gendered narratives of social decay. Yet prohibition does not eliminate the market; it reconfigures visibility and vulnerability. A sociological analysis must therefore examine not only law on the books but policing, neighborhood governance, labor precarity, and stigma.

Online arguments about sex work are rarely just about workers. They often become stand-ins for judgments about ordinary men and women. Men are accused of predation or hypocrisy; women are accused of market opportunism or false innocence. The labor question disappears into factional gender judgment. This is precisely why sociology insists on institutional re-centering. What are the conditions of entry? What role do migration and debt play? How do policing and shame structure risk? Which forms of violence are intensified by criminalization? These are the questions that turn moral heat back into analysis.

The comparative lesson is not that one model is universally correct under all conditions. It is that symbolic moral certainty is a poor substitute for policy evaluation. Any serious sociology of sexual politics must therefore keep both normative conflict and empirical comparison in view.

# Chapter 10: Comparative Cases Beyond China

Comparison is indispensable because it reveals what is historically specific and what is more general. If online gender conflict appears only in one setting, we might treat it as a local pathology. But similar patterns appear across North America, South Korea, Japan, Europe, and beyond. The recurring presence of antagonistic discourse suggests broader links between platformization, labor-market uncertainty, shifting family norms, and changing gender expectations. At the same time, national cases differ sharply in institutional history and cultural grammar.

The United States provides one major comparison. There, online gender conflict has been shaped by a highly polarized media environment, influencer economies, and long-standing divisions over feminism, sexuality, race, and family life. Manosphere communities, anti-feminist commentary, tradwife cultures, call-out feminism, and therapeutic vocabularies coexist and compete. Public discourse is heavily shaped by platform celebrity and political branding. Compared with China, state regulation is different, but monetized polarization is in many ways more overt.

South Korea offers another important comparison because demographic crisis, intense educational competition, youth precarity, and online gender conflict are tightly linked. Anti-feminist mobilization among young men, feminist digital activism, and political parties' attempts to instrumentalize gender grievance have made South Korea a particularly revealing case. Here we see how economic stagnation and status competition can be translated into explicitly gendered political identity. Similar mechanisms may appear in China, but under distinct institutional conditions.

Japan provides a somewhat different configuration. Long-standing anxieties over declining marriage, low fertility, precarious work, and gendered labor expectations have generated public narratives about herbivore men, withdrawn youth, and overburdened women. The idioms differ from Chinese platform speech, yet the underlying struggles over masculinity, employment, and social reproduction are recognizable. Japan reminds us that gender conflict can emerge not only through loud ideological confrontation but also through diffuse disinvestment from conventional intimacy.

European and North American debates over sex work, consent, and platform harassment add yet another comparative dimension. In some contexts, institutionalized feminist discourse is more visible within law, universities, and media. In others, backlash narratives portray feminism as elite capture or punitive orthodoxy. These variations matter because they shape what counts as mainstream grievance. A discourse that appears dissident in one setting may be common sense in another.

Comparison also helps us avoid civilizational explanations. It is tempting to explain Chinese gender conflict by invoking uniquely Chinese culture, or Western conflict by invoking uniquely Western decadence. Sociology resists these broad culturalist moves. Similar pressures recur where educational expansion outpaces stable mobility, where housing and family formation are expensive, where emotional life is commodified, and where platforms reward antagonism. The forms vary, but the underlying conjunction of insecurity, identity, and mediation is widely shared.

Yet comparison should not erase institutional difference. Marriage law, welfare states, housing regimes, labor protections, censorship practices, education systems, and migration patterns all shape the concrete experience of gender order. That is why comparative work must stay specific. The goal is not to flatten cases into one global script but to identify how general mechanisms are articulated through local institutions.

In this sense, comparison is not decorative. It is methodologically essential. It prevents both exceptionalism and false universality. It allows us to say: this part of the problem is broadly modern and platformized; this part is tied to the Chinese family system; this part resembles South Korea; this part is intensified by local governance; this part is common to digital attention economies everywhere.

Comparison also changes normative analysis. A policy or discourse style that appears inevitable in one setting may be revealed as contingent when compared with another. For example, the relationship between platform misogyny and state response differs sharply across political systems. The relation between dating discourse and welfare provision differs across housing regimes. Comparative work therefore expands political imagination by demonstrating that institutional arrangements could be otherwise.

This comparative sensibility is especially important when users invoke national decline or civilizational incompatibility. Such narratives often present contemporary gender conflict as proof that one society's women have become too demanding or one society's men too damaged. Comparative sociology shows instead that similar forms of complaint emerge under recurring conditions of precarious adulthood, competitive intimacy, and digital visibility. The vocabulary differs; the structural tensions often rhyme.

# Chapter 11: Methods for Studying Online Gender Conflict

If online gender conflict is a real sociological object, how should we study it? The first answer is methodological pluralism. No single method can capture discourse, affect, structure, and institutional effect all at once. Good research therefore combines approaches while remaining clear about each method's limits.

**Content analysis (内容分析)** is one obvious starting point. Researchers can code recurring themes, labels, moral vocabularies, and narrative structures across large samples of posts, comments, videos, or forums. This helps identify discourse patterns at scale. But content analysis alone cannot tell us how users interpret material, how algorithms shape exposure, or how discourse connects to offline institutions.

**Digital ethnography (数字民族志)** addresses some of these limits by observing communities over time, tracking platform cultures, interaction norms, humor, sanctions, and identity performances. Ethnography is especially valuable where irony, in-group slang, or shifting meme meanings matter. Yet ethnography carries its own problems: access, ethical risk, researcher positionality, and the instability of digital archives.

**Survey research (问卷调查)** can measure attitudes, experiences of harassment, relationship expectations, trust in institutions, or exposure to specific types of content. But surveys are limited by self-report, question wording, and the gap between declared attitude and observed interaction. They also struggle to capture fast-moving meme ecologies.

**In-depth interviews (深度访谈)** can illuminate how users make sense of gender discourse in relation to biography, work, family, and intimacy. Interviews are especially useful for understanding affective investment and identity-protective reasoning. But interviewees are narrators, not transparent windows into truth. Their stories must be interpreted within broader social structures.

**Platform studies (平台研究)** examines recommendation systems, moderation regimes, monetization, interface design, and governance. This is crucial because communicative behavior cannot be separated from technical affordances. Researchers may combine interface analysis, policy review, scraping where ethically and legally appropriate, and interviews with moderators or creators.

Comparative research is equally important. A post that seems uniquely toxic may be typical of a certain genre across countries. A legal debate that appears universal may actually depend on a specific regulatory regime. Cross-national comparison, however, requires caution. Terms do not map perfectly across languages, and similar labels may carry different institutional meanings.

Ethics are central in this field. Researchers studying online harassment, sexual politics, or semi-anonymous communities face serious issues of consent, traceability, and harm. Public posts are not automatically harmless to quote. De-anonymization risk is real. Researchers must also reflect on whether analysis amplifies the very content it seeks to study.

Methodological reflexivity is therefore part of the substance of the field. The sociology of gender conflict studies a topic that is emotionally charged, politically live, and commercially amplified. Researchers are never fully outside the discourse they study. Good work does not pretend to be passionless. It makes its concepts, evidence standards, and limitations explicit.

Historical method also belongs in this toolkit. Many apparently novel conflicts become more intelligible when placed against longer histories of moral regulation, feminist mobilization, labor-market change, or media commercialization. Without historical depth, platform conflicts can appear self-generating. With it, they become episodes in longer transformations of gender order and public culture.

Mixed methods are especially valuable in this field because the object itself is multi-layered. A researcher might use content analysis to identify recurring accusation patterns, interviews to reconstruct users' lived interpretations, and policy analysis to study platform moderation or legal response. The field benefits when these methods are combined rather than treated as mutually exclusive epistemologies.

# Chapter 12: Public Reason, Institutional Design, and Possibilities Beyond Antagonism

The point of this course is not to produce a fantasy of perfect harmony. Gender conflict reflects real injuries, structural inequalities, and incompatible interests in some domains. Sociology should not convert all conflict into misunderstanding. Yet it should also resist the idea that antagonism is the natural endpoint of gender modernity. Conflict is socially organized, and therefore its intensity and form are socially alterable.

One path forward lies in the recovery of **public reason (公共理性)**. Public reason does not mean bloodless neutrality or the suppression of emotion. It means argument that distinguishes empirical claims from symbolic insults, structural analysis from totalized blame, and policy evaluation from identity warfare. This is difficult in digital environments because the incentives run in the opposite direction. But difficulty is not the same as impossibility.

Institutional design matters here. Platforms can reduce frictionless amplification of harassment and humiliation. Educational institutions can teach media literacy that includes not only fact-checking but frame analysis, audience awareness, and emotional manipulation. Legal institutions can address harassment, exploitation, and violence more consistently. Welfare and housing policies can reduce the material desperation that so often gets translated into gender resentment. Family policy can reshape the distribution of care burdens that fuel conflict in intimate life.

Cross-group communication also requires realism. Not every conversation is redeemable. Some speech is strategic provocation; some communities are organized around hate. Sociology does not demand endless empathy for bad faith. But it does suggest that stable reduction of polarization depends less on winning every debate than on changing the conditions under which debate is staged. When platforms pay for resentment, resentment will remain abundant.

The course therefore ends where it began: with the distinction between argument quality and social conditions. PHIL 145C taught us to identify fallacies and presuppositions. Sociology adds that even excellent argument struggles when institutions reward simplification, when insecurity invites projection, and when identities are organized through grievance. To analyze online gender conflict adequately, we need both levels. We need conceptual discipline and structural explanation. Without the first, we become morally noisy. Without the second, we become sociologically naive.

The broader lesson extends beyond gender. Contemporary publics are full of conflicts that travel through the same circuits of platform amplification, emotional labor, identity protection, and symbolic escalation. Gender discourse is simply one of the most intense and revealing versions. Studying it sociologically teaches us not only about men and women, sex and law, or intimacy and stigma. It teaches us how modern publics are made.

That is the final reason this topic belongs in sociology rather than only in moral debate. The course does not ask students to resolve every normative conflict. It asks them to see that even the form of conflict is socially produced. Once that is visible, the question is no longer merely "who is right?" but "what kind of social world keeps reproducing this style of wrongness, injury, and accusation?" That is a properly sociological question, and it is the question that remains after the comment thread ends.

There is also a pedagogical implication. Students trained only to identify bad arguments may become sharper critics without becoming better analysts of institutions. Students trained only to name structures may become rhetorically certain without learning evidentiary discipline. The ambition of this course is to hold those capacities together: conceptual clarity, empirical caution, and structural imagination. The result should not be moral quietism. It should be a more demanding form of public understanding.

That public understanding will still leave disagreement intact. Sociology does not abolish conflict, and it should not pretend to. But it can change what kind of conflict is imaginable. It can move us from totalized accusation toward better specified institutional dispute, from symbolic enemies toward patterned explanation, and from the fantasy of one final verdict toward the harder task of collective interpretation. In a media environment structured against that task, the effort is necessarily difficult. It is also necessary.
